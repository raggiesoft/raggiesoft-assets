## A Day of Two Nations

After sharing lemonade and stories on the Pattersons' Vermont deck, it was time to move on. The two families said their goodbyes, promising to see them again before heading back to Virginia, and all six of them piled back into the Masons' minivan.

"Alright, Tom, where to next?" James Miller asked from the middle row as Thomas started the engine.

"I think a visit to the most famous landmark in town is in order," Thomas replied with a wink.

He pulled out of the Pattersons' driveway, making a careful right turn onto the American lane of Canusa Street, heading east. They drove past the last few American houses, the road narrowing slightly. Alex, watching from the back seat, saw the American houses on his right simply end. The road then took a gentle but definitive curve to the left, and suddenly, the architecture changed. The houses were now closer to the road, some with the distinct swooping roofs of Québécois style, and the street signs were in French. They were now on Rue Canusa.

"Wait a second," Emily Miller said from her seat, looking around in genuine confusion. "Did we just... cross back into Canada?"

"I think we did," James added, equally baffled. "I didn't see a customs post. There was nothing."

From the driver's seat, Thomas laughed heartily, not even slowing down. "Relax, everyone! That's just the end of the street. Everyone does it. The road starts in Canada, splits the border for a block, and then goes back into Canada. As long as you're a local just driving around town, nobody bats an eye. It's just... how you get from that part of Beebe Plain to this part of Stanstead."

James shook his head in amazement from the middle row. "This place is truly something else."

"You haven't seen anything yet," Thomas said, continuing to drive through Stanstead. "We're heading to the library."

He parked on Rue Church, a quiet street just a block away from the main road. In front of them stood a stately, handsome old building made of granite and brick. A sign out front read: The Stanstead-Derby Line Free Library and Arts Centre.

"Okay, here's the fun part," Chloé said, her eyes sparkling as they all got out of the minivan. "The library is in both countries. But the front door... is in America."

She led the way, walking down the sidewalk of Rue Church to the corner. Then, she turned right onto the sidewalk of what was now Caswell Avenue. "We just stepped into Vermont," she announced casually.

The Millers looked down at their feet as if expecting to see a change in the concrete. Alex, maneuvering his wheelchair, felt a thrill at the sheer absurdity of it. They walked a few dozen feet along the American sidewalk and went up the steps to the grand main entrance.

Inside, the library was hushed and smelled wonderfully of old paper and wood polish. It was a beautiful, classic library space. Chloé, however, didn't lead them to the books. She led them to the center of the main reading room and pointed down at the floor.

Running right across the polished wood was a thick, painted black line. On one side, a small, discreet Canadian flag was mounted on a bookshelf. On the other, an American one.

"There it is," Chloé whispered with reverence. "The border. We're standing in two countries right now."

Alex wheeled his chair right up to the line, placing one wheel in Canada and one in the United States. He looked at Chloé, his expression one of pure, unadulterated fascination. THIS, he typed on his AAC, his synthesized voice a quiet hum in the library's stillness, IS THE COOLEST THING I HAVE EVER SEEN.

Chloé beamed, her heart swelling with pride for her strange, wonderful hometown. She reached out, took his hand across the black line, and gave it a squeeze. Their parents watched, smiling. In this quiet, shared space, the border wasn't a barrier. It was just a line on the floor, an invitation to stand in two worlds at once, a perfect symbol of the community itself.

## The Montréal Rule

The next morning dawned bright and clear at the Derby Line Inn. A palpable sense of excitement buzzed among the six of them as they gathered after breakfast. The plan for the day was their most ambitious yet: a day trip to Montréal. For the Millers, it was a chance to see a world-class city they'd never visited. For Alex, it was an adventure into the heart of the French-speaking world he was just beginning to explore.

"Alright," James Miller said with a laugh as they all piled into the Mason minivan. "Should I even bother asking if we need our passports at this point?"

"Always good to have them, just in case," Thomas replied with a knowing smile.

As Thomas guided the minivan to the Canadian Port of Entry at Stanstead, a different officer was on duty. He was younger than Jean-Guy, with a sharp, energetic air about him, but his face broke into an equally familiar smile as he saw the Masons' van.

"Bonjour, Thomas! Bonjour, Sophie!" he called out. "Heading out for another adventure?"

"Bonjour, Michel!" Sophie replied warmly. "We're taking our friends on a big trip today. All the way to Montréal to see the Old Port and maybe get some smoked meat!"

Michel's friendly expression immediately became a little more professional, though his tone remained pleasant. "Ah, Montréal! C'est super! Okay, well, since you're heading that far into the country, you know the drill. I'm going to have to do this one by the book. Can I see everyone's passports, please?"

In the middle row, Emily Miller looked surprised. "Oh! Really? I was starting to think you didn't use them here," she said with a light, curious laugh.

"Ah, that's the 'Montréal Rule,'" Sophie explained gently to her and James. "It's different when you're leaving the immediate border area. The agents here know us, but if we were to get pulled over for something way up in the city, the police there wouldn't know us from Adam. The stamp in the passport proves we all entered Canada legally today. It's an unwritten rule, but an important one."

"Well, that makes perfect sense," James said, a look of dawning understanding on his face as he passed his family's passports forward to Thomas.

Thomas gathered all six passports—three dark blue Canadian ones for his family and three navy American ones for the Millers—and handed the mixed stack up to Michel. The officer took them without a second glance; this, too, was a normal part of life on the line. He opened each one, looking from the photo to the person inside the bustling van.

"Chloé," he said with a nod, "you're getting as tall as your mother. And Alex, welcome to Canada. First time to Montréal?"

YES, SIR. VERY EXCITED, Alex typed.

"You'll love it," Michel said, stamping the documents with a series of firm thunks. He asked the standard questions about what they were bringing into the country, which Thomas answered clearly.

"Alright, you're all set," Michel said, passing the stack of stamped passports back. "Have a fantastic time in the city!"

As they pulled away, James Miller let out a low whistle from the middle row. "Well," he said with a chuckle, "I finally got to use my passport!"

With their documents properly stamped and their entry into Canada officially recorded, the minivan merged onto the highway, heading north towards the glittering skyline of Montréal, ready for a new kind of adventure in a city full of history, culture, and the promise of world-famous smoked meat sandwiches.

## Montréal

The drive north from the borderlands to Montréal was a journey into a different world. The rolling hills gave way to a flat, expansive plain, and soon, the iconic, cross-topped peak of Mount Royal rose from the horizon, heralding their arrival.

Their first stop, as planned, was the mountain itself. Thomas expertly navigated the winding road up its slope, parking near the grand stone chalet. They all got out, the air cooler and fresher at this elevation. From the Belvedere Kondiaronk, the view was breathtaking. The entire downtown skyline of Montréal spread out before them, a dense forest of skyscrapers giving way to the vast, shimmering expanse of the St. Lawrence River.

"Whoa," was all Emily Miller could say, her eyes wide.

"You can see everything from up here," James added, equally impressed.

Alex, positioned at the stone balustrade by Chloé, took in the panoramic view. It was immense, a landscape of human ambition and natural grandeur all at once. Chloé pointed out landmarks her mother had described to her. IT’S SO… BIG, Alex typed, the simple words imbued with a profound sense of scale. Chloé squeezed his shoulder in agreement, the two of them sharing the moment of awe.

Next, a pilgrimage for the taste buds. Thomas drove them to the vibrant, eclectic Mile End neighborhood, a place buzzing with a creative, bohemian energy. He double-parked for just a moment in front of a small, unassuming storefront with a line spilling onto the sidewalk: "La Flamme Ronde Bagels."

"Stay here," Sophie said, hopping out. "I'll be quick!" She returned minutes later with two large paper bags, a wonderful, warm, sweet-and-savory aroma wafting from them.

"Fresh from the wood-fired oven," she announced, passing a still-warm, sesame-seed-covered bagel to each person in the van. The Millers took their first bites and their eyes widened in surprise. It was nothing like an American bagel—denser, chewier, with a distinct, subtle sweetness from being boiled in honey water.

"Oh my," Emily managed through her mouthful. "That's incredible."

For their main lunch, they headed to the bustling Plateau neighborhood and found a parking spot near "Levine's Montréal Delicatessen." The place was a glorious assault on the senses: loud, crowded, and smelling divinely of smoked meat, pickles, and rye bread. They squeezed into a booth, with Alex's wheelchair at the end of the table. The order was simple: six smoked meat sandwiches, medium-fat, on rye bread with a smear of mustard, served with giant sour pickles and bottles of crisp, cold black cherry cola.

When the sandwiches arrived, stacked impossibly high with hand-carved, spice-crusted brisket, the Millers stared in respectful silence for a moment before digging in. It was a culinary revelation. The only downside to the authentic atmosphere was the faint blue-gray haze hanging in the air, drifting from the designated smoking section near the back of the restaurant. The four non-smoking parents exchanged a brief, resigned glance—an unavoidable annoyance, the price of admission for a truly great meal.

For Alex, however, with his slight breathing difficulty, the secondhand smoke was more than just an annoyance. It started as a small, dry, persistent cough, a tickle in the back of his throat. He tried to ignore it, focusing on the amazing taste of his sandwich.

Chloé, ever attuned to his slightest discomfort, noticed immediately. She saw the subtle tension around his eyes and heard the change in his breathing between bites. She leaned closer to him, her brow furrowed with concern. "You okay, Alex? Your breathing sounds a little... tight."

Alex, who had been trying to hide his discomfort, met her gaze and gave a slight nod, bringing up his AAC. THE SMOKE. IT’S A LITTLE HARD TO BREATHE.

The moment the synthesized voice spoke, all four parents immediately focused on him. James Miller's hand was instantly on his son's shoulder, his expression shifting from relaxed to concerned. "Okay, buddy. Let's get you some fresh air."

"We can get the rest of these wrapped up," Sophie said immediately, already flagging down a busy waiter. "*On peut avoir le reste pour emporter, s'il vous plaît?*"

The rest of the meal was a blur of quick, decisive action. James helped Alex navigate his chair towards the exit while Thomas settled the bill. Within a minute, they were all back out on the bustling sidewalk. Outside on the bustling sidewalk, Alex took a deep, grateful breath of the clearer city air, the tightness in his chest already beginning to ease. Chloé stood beside him, her hand resting reassuringly on his arm. The smoked meat had been a triumph, a new favorite taste of Montréal logged away. The smoky haze they'd left behind, however, was an experience they were all happy to leave in the past as they continued their afternoon.

The afternoon was dedicated to Old Montréal. Here, the modern city fell away, replaced by the narrow, cobblestone streets and stone facades of the 18th century. James and Thomas took turns helping push Alex's chair over the bumpiest sections of Rue Saint-Paul, the old stones a charming but practical challenge. They browsed the windows of art galleries and artisan shops, soaking in the distinctly European atmosphere.

Their walk led them to a grand public square, dominated by the magnificent, twin-towered facade of the Basilique Sainte-Anne-de-Montréal. Stepping inside, they were plunged into a world of breathtaking beauty. The cavernous nave was bathed in a deep, ethereal blue light, filtering through stained glass and reflecting off a ceiling dotted with countless golden stars. The altar was a masterpiece of intricate, gilded woodwork. They walked the aisles in hushed reverence, their footsteps echoing softly. Alex tilted his head back, mesmerized by the starry ceiling, while Chloé stood beside him, her hand resting on his shoulder, both of them sharing the profound, silent beauty of the sacred space.

They ended their city tour with a stroll along the Old Port. The wide, flat promenade was perfect for Alex's wheelchair. They watched street performers juggle fire, listened to the clip-clop of the horse-drawn carriages, and looked out over the wide, flowing river. The two families, a comfortable and happy unit, found a bench. Sophie and Emily shared one of the warm bagels from La Flamme Ronde, while James and Thomas watched the boats.

Alex and Chloé sat a little apart, just watching the people go by. THIS WAS A GOOD DAY, Alex typed, a contented sigh in the rhythm of his fingers.

Chloé leaned her head on his shoulder, a familiar, comforting gesture. "The best day," she agreed softly. "I knew you'd love it." The city had been grand and exciting, but for both of them, the best part was simply being there, seeing it all, together.

## The Return Journey

After a whirlwind day spent marveling at the historic architecture of Old Montréal, exploring the bustling shops on Sainte-Catherine Street, and indulging in legendary smoked meat sandwiches that lived up to their reputation, the minivan made the two-hour drive south from the city. A comfortable, contented silence filled the vehicle, punctuated by the occasional sleepy murmur. The grand adventure had been a resounding success, but everyone was looking forward to the quiet comfort of their hotel rooms in Derby Line.

As they approached the U.S. Port of Entry, the familiar green signs of Vermont welcomed them back. James Miller leaned forward from the middle row.

"So, Tom," he asked, "I'm guessing that 'Montréal Rule' works both ways? We'll all need our passports ready for this side, too?"

"You got it, James," Thomas confirmed with a nod. "Same exact deal. Since we're coming from deep inside Canada and not just popping over from Stanstead, they'll need to check us all in properly."

The line was short, and as Thomas pulled the minivan forward, a U.S. Customs officer stepped into the booth. He was a tall man with kind eyes and a welcoming, easygoing smile.

"Evening, Tom. Sophie," the officer said, leaning on the windowsill of his booth.

"Evening, Frank," Thomas replied. "We took our friends here up to Montréal for the day."

Frank's relaxed demeanor instantly sharpened with professional focus, though his friendly tone remained. "Oh, Montréal! Sounds like a great trip. In that case, you know the drill. Let's see everyone's documents, please."

Thomas had already collected the stack. He passed the documents up to the officer: five U.S. passports for himself, Chloé, and the Millers, and for Sophie, her Canadian passport with her U.S. Permanent Resident Card tucked neatly inside. Frank took the mixed stack, a familiar sight for any officer working the northern border, and began the process. His movements were professional and unhurried as he checked each document, looking from the photo to each tired but happy face in the van.

"Anything to declare, folks?" Frank asked. "Bringing back any purchases from Montréal?"

"Just some maple candies for the kids and a couple of t-shirts," James Miller answered from the middle row.

Thomas chimed in with a wry grin, clearly for the officer's benefit. "And nope, Frank, not a single Koko-Kapsel in the car, I swear. Wouldn't want to start an international incident over some chocolate and a tiny toy."

Frank let out a hearty chuckle, shaking his head. "Appreciate you sparing me the paperwork, Tom. You know how the folks in Washington get about those things. A real threat to national security, those little plastic capsules."

In the back seat, Alex looked confused. He caught Chloé's eye, a quizzical expression on his face. *Koko-Kapsels? International incident?* Chloé just gave him a small, conspiratorial smile and a tiny shrug, as if to say, *I'll explain later. It's a long, silly story.*

"Any produce, meat products, or plants?" Frank continued, his tone returning to business.

"No, sir," Thomas replied.

"Okay then," Frank said, satisfied as he handed the stack of documents back. "Looks like you're all set. Good to see you folks. Drive safe now, and enjoy the rest of your visit."

With that, they were back in the United States. As they drove towards the hotel, Alex was already formulating the question on his AAC about the mysterious, apparently dangerous, chocolate eggs. The day continued to be full of new and confusing wonders.

## A Lesson in Chocolate and Law

Back in the quiet comfort of their adjoining hotel rooms at the Derby Line Inn, a contented exhaustion had settled over the two families. The four parents were in one room, chatting softly as they wound down from the long, exciting day. In the connecting room, Alex and Chloé were settled in for the night, the door between the rooms propped open. Chloé was already in her pajamas, sitting cross-legged on the bed, while Alex was positioned in his wheelchair beside her, having just returned from using the bathroom.

The memory of the border crossing, and the strange inside joke between her father and the customs officer, was still fresh in his mind. He turned to Chloé, his expression serious and curious, and typed on his AAC.

OKAY, CHLOÉ. EXPLAIN KOKO-KAPSELS. WHY IS A CHOCOLATE EGG AN <aac>International incident</aac>?

Chloé’s conspiratorial smile returned, but it was mixed with a hint of seriousness. She patted the spot on the bed next to her, an invitation for him to bring his chair closer.

"Okay, so," she began, lowering her voice slightly as if sharing a government secret. "A Koko-Kapsel is this awesome chocolate egg they sell in Canada and, like, the rest of the world. It's a hollow milk chocolate egg, and inside is a little plastic pod, like a capsule. And inside *that* is a surprise toy you have to put together. Some of them are really cool."

THAT SOUNDS GREAT, Alex typed. SO… WHAT’S THE PROBLEM?

"That's the crazy part," Chloé said, leaning in. "It's illegal to bring them into the United States. Totally banned."

Alex’s brow furrowed in confusion. WHY?

"It's two things, according to my dad," she explained, counting them off on her fingers. "First, there's some really old law from, like, the 1930s that says you can't sell candy that has a 'non-food object' totally hidden inside it. They were worried kids would accidentally choke on a toy or something they didn't know was in there."

BUT… THE TOY IS IN THE CAPSULE, Alex reasoned.

"Exactly!" Chloé exclaimed. "That's what everyone else in the world thinks! But the U.S. law is super strict about it. And second, the government also says the little toy parts themselves are a choking hazard for kids under three. So, it's like, double illegal."

She saw the look of baffled amusement on his face and her expression grew serious. She reached out and placed her hand on his arm.

"Here's the thing, though, Alex," she said, her voice dropping to an earnest whisper. "My dad and Frank were joking because they know each other. But it is absolutely no laughing matter if you get caught. For real."

REALLY? FOR CHOCOLATE?

"Yes," Chloé said, her eyes wide and sincere. "My parents know people who have had their cars torn apart at the border because an agent thought they were smuggling them. People have gotten hit with huge fines, like hundreds of dollars, for having just one or two eggs they forgot were in their kid's bag. And I've heard stories… people have actually been detained, arrested… 'gone to jail,' even for a little while, because they tried to bring in a whole case of them."

She shook her head. "It seems so silly, a ban on a chocolate egg. But to the U.S. government, it's a federal law. They treat it like you're smuggling contraband. It's one of those weird, serious rules you just don't ever, ever break."

Alex stared at her, processing the sheer absurdity of it. A law so strict it could land you in jail over a piece of candy with a toy inside. The casual, friendly border he had come to know over the past two days suddenly had a hidden, unyielding edge. It was another layer to this strange, wonderful, and confusing place—a world with unwritten rules of friendship and very, very serious written rules about chocolate.
