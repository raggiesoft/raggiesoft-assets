## An Attempt at Normalcy

The amber sconces in the Recreation Lounge cast a warm, low-level glow over the faux-walnut paneling. After the deafening, grease-stained chaos of the engine room and the sheer psychological terror of the afternoon's broadcasts, the heavy soundproofing of the room felt like a physical embrace.

For the first time all day, the station was entirely quiet.

Wyatt stood in front of the massive, unapologetically ugly floral sofa. He grabbed the heavy, burnt-orange seat cushions and tossed them onto the carpeted floor.

"Alright," Wyatt muttered, his voice quiet in the insulated room. "Let's see if this thing actually works."

Sarah stood beside him, her slate-blue Helios uniform shirt still tied around her waist. She grabbed the heavy metal handle of the hidden bed frame. Together, they pulled backward. With a terrifyingly loud, metallic *shriek* of neglected springs, the heavy steel frame unfolded, locking into place and revealing a surprisingly thick queen-sized mattress.

It was a stark contrast to the plush luxury of the Captain's Quarters, but right now, neither of them wanted to be in a massive room staring out a viewport at the cold void of space. They wanted a bunker. They wanted a den.

Sarah walked over to the narrow closet built into the bulkhead next to the sofa. She pulled open the louvered door and pulled out two heavy, woven wool blankets and a stack of dense, foam pillows wrapped in sterile Apex plastic.

"They smell a little like ozone and old dust," Sarah noted, tossing the pillows onto the mattress and shaking out the heavy blankets.

"Better than the engine room," Wyatt replied, pulling off his boots and leaving them by the heavy wooden television cabinet.

They crawled onto the mattress, the steel springs groaning softly under their weight. Sarah immediately shifted toward the center, pulling the thick wool blankets up over them. Wyatt slid in right behind her, his arm wrapping securely around her waist, pulling her back flush against his chest.

It was the "Anchor Rule" in its purest form. They were tangled together, entirely grounded by the physical weight and warmth of the other person. Sarah let out a long, shuddering sigh, her hands resting over Wyatt’s arm, feeling the steady, rhythmic thump of his heart against her back.

The silence of the room was heavy, but for the first time in hours, it wasn't suffocating. It was hopeful.

Before coming to the lounge, Wyatt had stood at the massive comms board in Operations, his hand hovering over the dials. He hadn't turned the receiver off—it was hardwired to stay open—but he had tuned the frequency just enough to catch the fragmented, dying echoes of military chatter bouncing off the atmosphere.

Operation Glass Wall had been executed.

The United States military had successfully detonated the subterranean nuclear payload along the Continental Divide. They had unleashed hell on their own soil, turning thousands of miles of the Rocky Mountains into a trench of radioactive glass and boiling slag.

And, incredibly, the chatter suggested it was working.

The seismic shockwaves from the anomaly's terrifying eastward sprint had slammed into the molten, radioactive barrier and stopped. The biological mass couldn't dig through liquid glass. It couldn't consume radioactive fire. The scorched-earth trench had effectively severed the continent in half, but it had starved the immediate expansion.

The anomaly was trapped on the burning western seaboard. The tide, at least for tonight, had turned.

"Do you think it's over?" Sarah whispered into the quiet dimness of the room, her voice barely carrying over the hum of the station's ventilation.

"The digging stopped," Wyatt answered softly, his chin resting against her shoulder. "The military frequencies were reporting zero seismic movement past the Rockies. The wall held, Sarah. They actually managed to trap it."

"They burned half the world to do it," she murmured, the grief still heavy in her chest.

"They saved the other half," Wyatt corrected gently, his thumb brushing a slow, comforting rhythm against her hip. "We escaped the NTTR, Sarah. Humanity is going to escape this. They just bought themselves time to figure out how to kill it."

Sarah closed her eyes, letting his steady, quiet confidence wash over her. They were from two entirely different planets, lightyears away from the agricultural fields of Wyatt's youth or the dirt gardens of hers. But lying here on a rusted, screaming metal bed frame covered in ugly floral fabric, none of that mattered.

They were alive. The lifeboat was holding. And down on Earth, humanity was fighting back.

Sarah shifted closer, burying her face into his chest, letting the exhaustion of the longest day of their lives finally pull her under. Wyatt held her tight, his eyes watching the dark screen of the 1970s console TV, standing guard in the quiet dark.

## Shedding the Armor

The heavy, mechanical reality of the Nevada Test and Training Range was designed to break you. One of the most insidious ways Apex Defense Solutions achieved this wasn't through grueling physical labor or the constant threat of violence, but through the systematic destruction of basic human dignity.

They issued every contractor exactly one uniform. There were no laundry rotations. There were no spares. You wore it, you sweat in it, you bled in it, and eventually, the sheer physical degradation made you stop caring about yourself entirely. You weren't a person; you were just a tool in a slate-blue wrapper.

Sarah only technically had two uniforms because she had scavenged an abandoned break area in Sector 4 and found a S.H.O.P. Marine jacket that was a full size too small. It was suffocatingly tight across her shoulders, but it meant she could occasionally peel off her primary Field Safety Technician gear just to let it air out.

For the last four days—through the panicked escape, the zero-gravity transit, and the desperate repairs on Delta 9—they had been living inside those same stiff, foul-smelling synthetic fabrics.

But as the deep-space morning cycled over the outpost, that nightmare finally ended.

Wyatt walked into the Recreation Lounge, his arms loaded with a massive, vacuum-sealed plastic bundle he had hauled out of the executive storage lockers in the Captain's Quarters. He dropped the heavy cube onto the foot of the pull-out "Grandma" sofa with a dull thud.

"The previous Director didn't just hoard freeze-dried food," Wyatt said, a massive, genuine grin breaking across his exhausted face. "He hoarded a wardrobe."

Sarah sat up, the heavy wool blanket falling to her waist. She stared at the plastic bundle. Wyatt pulled his heavy utility knife from his belt and sliced the vacuum seal.

With a soft *hiss* of equalizing pressure, the plastic burst open. The smell of clean, manufactured cotton wafted into the room, entirely devoid of ozone, machine grease, or fear.

It was a treasure trove of civilian normalcy. There were stacks of soft, perfectly broken-in denim jeans, heavy cotton t-shirts, and thick, pristine white socks. Beneath the denim were brand-new, sealed packages of underwear—men's boxer-briefs, women's cotton panties, and sports bras.

Sarah’s hands trembled slightly as she reached out, her fingers brushing against the soft heather-gray cotton of a t-shirt. It felt like an absolute, impossible luxury. A heavy, emotional knot formed in her throat.

"Let's get rid of them," Wyatt said quietly, his voice losing its usual gruff edge. He pulled a heavy canvas laundry bag from the bottom of the pile and snapped it open.

They didn't retreat to separate rooms. The trauma of Sector 4 had burned away any need for distance between them; right now, the only thing that mattered was shedding the corporate skin.

Sarah stood up, her fingers gripping the hem of that suffocatingly tight S.H.O.P. Marine jacket. She pulled it over her head and threw it onto the carpet with profound disgust. She unbuttoned the stiff, sweat-stained slate-blue FST shirt beneath it, peeling the synthetic fabric off her skin. She stripped down entirely, shivering slightly in the cool, recycled air of the lounge, and immediately reached for the fresh clothes.

The feeling of sliding into clean, soft cotton underwear and a sports bra that actually fit sent a wave of pure, physical relief crashing through her nervous system. She pulled on a pair of the denim jeans, the heavy fabric feeling wonderfully grounding, and slipped the heather-gray t-shirt over her head.

Beside her, Wyatt was fighting his way out of his heavy, grease-stained canvas mechanic's pants and the black undershirt he had practically lived in for a week. He kicked the heavy boots aside, pulling on a pair of clean boxer-briefs, fresh denim jeans, and a soft, fitted black t-shirt. He sat down on the edge of the mattress, letting out a long, shuddering groan of relief as he pulled a pair of thick, clean white socks onto his feet.

"Oh my god," Sarah whispered, her hands running down the soft denim of her jeans. She looked at Wyatt. Without the heavy, militarized Apex uniforms, they didn't look like runaways anymore. They looked human.

Wyatt stood up. He grabbed the S.H.O.P. Marine jacket, the FST uniform, his grease-stained pants, and the black undershirt, shoving them all aggressively into the canvas laundry bag. He pulled the thick drawstring violently tight, sealing the smell of the NTTR inside.

He tossed the bag into the dark corner of the closet and slammed the louvered door shut. It was over.

"Come on," Sarah said, a bright, radiant smile breaking across her face. She grabbed his hand, her clean fingers interlacing with his. "I raided the pantry yesterday. It's time for a real breakfast."

The narrow, brushed-steel galley kitchen was instantly filled with the greatest smell Wyatt had ever experienced.

Because of the "One-at-a-Time" rule in the cramped space, Wyatt sat at the small, square dining table securely bolted to the grated floor, sipping a mug of aggressively strong diner coffee from the heavy percolator. He watched Sarah work through the narrow doorway, looking entirely at peace in her gray t-shirt and jeans.

Whoever the previous Apex Director was, he had lived like absolute royalty while ignoring the rest of the universe.

Sarah had raided the heavy walk-in mechanical freezer and the vacuum-sealed dry stores. Now, a heavy iron skillet was sizzling on the induction stove. She cracked rehydrated eggs into the pan, tossing in thick, diced chunks of premium cured ham and tearing up slices of real, sharp Swiss cheese.

"I went down to the bay before you woke up," Sarah called over her shoulder, tossing a handful of sliced, deep-space hydroponic mushrooms into the skillet. They hit the hot butter with a loud, mouth-watering hiss. "The UV yields are incredible. The mushrooms grew almost overnight."

She expertly folded the omelets over, sliding them onto two heavy ceramic plates alongside thick slices of toasted wheat bread. She carried them out to the dining nook, setting one down in front of Wyatt before returning for two tall glasses of reconstituted orange juice.

Wyatt took a bite. The fresh, earthy snap of the mushrooms paired with the rich, melted Swiss cheese and the salty ham was staggering. It wasn't just sustenance; it was an experience.

"Sarah," Wyatt groaned, closing his eyes and leaning back against the heavy vinyl chair. "This is incredible."

Sarah sat across from him, tucking her legs up underneath her, absolutely beaming. "I told you. We aren't just surviving anymore. We're living."

Wyatt swallowed, picking up his mug of coffee. He looked out the thick, circular viewport next to the table at the slow, steady rotation of the starfield outside, his expression shifting into something quiet and deeply relieved.

"I checked the passive receiver board when I was pulling the clothes from the lockers," Wyatt said, keeping his voice calm, not wanting to ruin the warmth of the morning.

Sarah paused, her fork hovering over her plate. The memory of the Scorched Earth protocol still haunted the edges of her mind. "Did the Glass Wall hold?"

Wyatt nodded, his dark eyes meeting hers. "It held. The military intercepts confirmed it. The anomaly couldn't dig through the radioactive slag. The entire northern expansion completely stalled out."

Sarah let out a shaky breath, the tension leaving her shoulders. "Where did it stop?"

"Just south of Salem, Oregon," Wyatt answered, the relief evident in his heavy baritone. "The Pacific Northwest survived, Sarah. The military frequencies were listing the green zones. Salem, Portland, Olympia, Tacoma, Seattle... all the way up through Bellingham, Blaine, and up into Vancouver, British Columbia. The anomaly had to stop growing to heal itself from the burns. They actually saved the northern coast."

Sarah looked down at her plate, a profound, heavy gratitude washing over her. Millions of people had woken up this morning because that wall of fire had held. The monster in the earth wasn't invincible. It could be stopped. It could be contained.

She looked across the small, faux-wood table at Wyatt. He was wearing clean civilian clothes, drinking coffee, and the universe outside their little metal bubble was finally fighting back.

"Eat your omelet," Sarah said softly, reaching across the small table to rest her hand gently over his. "You have a hydroponics pump to check on later."

## The Ghost Ship

The remnants of the omelets were cleared away, the heavy ceramic plates soaking in the small galley sink. For exactly two hours, Wyatt and Sarah had allowed themselves to believe they were safe. They had sat in the Recreation Lounge, the soft cotton of their clean clothes feeling like a physical shield against the universe, and just breathed.

But Wyatt’s mechanical brain never completely shut off. The profound silence of the station was a blessing, but he needed to be absolutely sure the Glass Wall down on Earth was still holding.

"I'm just going to check the passive receiver," Wyatt told her, squeezing her hand before stepping out of the lounge. "Just to listen for the seismology reports. I'll be right back."

Sarah nodded, curling her legs up on the floral sofa, a soft smile on her face.

Wyatt walked down the quiet, curved corridor and pushed open the heavy door to the Operations center. The room was dark, bathed in the low amber glow of the massive wraparound console. The hardwired receiver was still softly hissing with deep-space static.

Wyatt stepped up to the analog board, his hand reaching for the frequency dial to tune back into the unencrypted military channels.

Before his fingers brushed the metal, the communications board violently locked up.

The low hiss of static vanished, replaced by a high-pitched, rhythmic electronic squeal. It wasn't the grating dual-tone of the EBS. It was a tight-beam, heavily encrypted data burst hitting the station's primary dish. The heavy amber CRT monitor directly in front of Wyatt flared to life, lines of raw code cascading down the phosphor screen faster than a human could read.

Then, the heavy dot-matrix printer beside the console violently jerked to life.

*ZZZT-ZZZT-ZZZT.*

Wyatt’s blood ran completely cold. He ripped the perforated paper from the feed slot the second the print head stopped moving. The text wasn't a warning to civilians. It was raw, unencrypted metadata from an approaching Apex Defense Solutions targeting computer.

- **INTERCEPT PROTOCOL: INITIATED**

- **TARGET:** OUTPOST DELTA 9 (LEGACY ASSET 44-A)

- **TELEMETRY DIAGNOSTIC:** O2 SCRUBBERS ACTIVE. THERMAL OUTPUT 72°F.

- **BIO-SIGNATURES DETECTED:** 2 (UNAUTHORIZED)

- **ASSET DEPLOYED:** CLASS-A TACTICAL DROPSHIP (CLEANER SQUADRON)

- **OBJECTIVE:** PURGE AND SECURE. NO QUARTER. **ETA:** 04 HOURS, 12 MINUTES.

"Sarah!" Wyatt roared, his voice tearing through the quiet corridors of the station.

He didn't wait. He slammed his hand down on the heavy *GENERAL QUARTERS* toggle on the analog board. A pulsing, deep-red alarm light began to spin on the ceiling, bathing the command center in the color of an execution.

Sarah burst through the door a second later, her bare feet sliding on the grated floor plates. The serene, relaxed energy she had carried just moments ago was instantly eradicated by the flashing red light.

"What happened? Did the wall fall?" she gasped, her eyes wide with terror.

Wyatt shoved the dot-matrix printout into her hands. "Apex found us. When I fixed the carbon baffles and fired up the hydroponics pumps, the reactor spiked. Their orbital grid caught the thermal bloom when they fired the kinetic strike on Nevada. They know we're here, and they're sending a cleaner crew to silence us."

Sarah stared at the blocky, printed text. Her hands started to shake. The absolute nightmare of Sector 4 had found them in the dark.

"Four hours," she whispered, looking up at him, the claustrophobia of the station suddenly crashing down on her. "Wyatt, we're in a guard tower. We don't have weapons. We don't have an escape pod. We're trapped in a metal box."

Wyatt stared at the main console, his dark eyes tracing the heavy, archaic lines of the analog dials. His mind was racing, tearing apart the blueprints of the station he had been mapping in his head for the last four days.

"No," Wyatt said, his voice dropping into a low, terrifyingly calm register. He looked up at Sarah. "We aren't in a guard tower."

He grabbed her hand and pulled her toward the massive viewport overlooking the station's outer hull. He pointed out into the dark, past the massive rotating parabolic dish, toward the heavy, cylindrical architecture of the lower rings.

"Look at the hull plating. Look at the exhaust baffles," Wyatt said, his mechanic's brain firing on all cylinders. "Apex didn't build this place from scratch. They bought it cheap and retrofitted it. Delta 9 isn't a station, Sarah. It’s an old Class-IV Heavy Deep-Space Hauler. They just parked it, welded the navigational thrusters shut, and called it an outpost."

Sarah looked out the scratched glass, her mind struggling to catch up. "You're saying this thing can move?"

"I'm saying the fission reactor in the basement isn't just a power plant. It's a primary drive core," Wyatt confirmed, a dangerous, reckless spark lighting up in his eyes. "Modern Apex dropships run on sleek, regulated fusion drives. They're safe, but they have a hard speed limit. This bucket of bolts? It runs on raw, unregulated nuclear thrust. If we can manually blow the orbital tethers and reroute the reactor output into the dead engines, we can push this thing into the red zone. We will blow the doors off that dropship."

Sarah looked from the terrifying void of space back to the dot-matrix printout in her hand. A cleaner crew was four hours away. The only option was to turn their lifeboat into a getaway car.

"What do we have to do?" Sarah asked, her voice steadying, the fierce survivor instinct locking back into place.

"We have to go back to the Boiler Room," Wyatt said, turning toward the door. "And we have to wake this ghost ship up."
