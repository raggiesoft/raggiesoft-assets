## The Coordinates

The soft, dynamic LED lighting in the 2044-era tech room was set to a warm, inviting morning glow.

Wyatt sat in the ergonomic mesh chair at the primary workstation, a mug of premium black coffee resting on the slate-gray desk. Sarah was perched comfortably on the edge of the desk right beside him, her bare feet dangling over the plush acoustic carpet. She was sipping her own mug of hot chocolate, perfectly content in her soft denim jeans and heather-gray t-shirt.

The frantic, ticking clock of the Nevada Test and Training Range was millions of miles behind them. Today, they had a completely different kind of task. They needed to pick a home.

"Aria," Wyatt said, leaning slightly toward the ambient microphone array in the ceiling. "We need a destination. We're currently drifting at conventional maximum, but we need to put the FTL drive to use and get entirely off the board."

The standalone server tower in the corner hummed a soft, musical note, and the curved holographic display in front of them flared to life.

*"I am ready to assist, Wyatt,"* Aria’s pleasant, synthetic voice echoed from the high-fidelity speakers. *"What parameters would you like me to prioritize for the search?"*

Sarah lowered her mug, looking at the glowing screen. "We need a needle in a haystack, Aria. I want a planet that doesn't exist on any Apex Defense Solutions registry. No corporate mining claims, no United States Department of Defense black sites, and absolutely no human colonization. Completely untouched."

*"Applying isolation filters to the archived star charts,"* Aria replied smoothly. Massive clusters of data and localized star maps began to cascade across the curved screen, thousands of systems being filtered out in milliseconds. *"What are your environmental requirements?"*

"Earth-baseline," Wyatt answered. "Breathable nitrogen-oxygen atmosphere. Fresh surface water. A stable magnetosphere to deflect cosmic radiation. We need a place where we don't have to wear an EVA suit just to step outside."

Sarah chimed in, her eyes shining with a sudden, desperate hope. "And dirt, Aria. Real, rich, organic soil. I want to be able to plant seeds in actual earth, not just hydroponic clay pebbles."

The server hummed for exactly three seconds. The cascading data on the screen abruptly stopped.

A single, highly detailed holographic rendering of a vibrant blue and green exoplanet spun slowly in the center of the display.

*"Search complete,"* Aria announced. *"I have located a prime candidate matching all specified parameters. It is an uncharted terrestrial world located in the Cygnus Arm, categorized in the former architect's private offline logs as System 88-Tango, Exoplanet 4. It is located significantly beyond the current bounds of corporate and military exploration."*

Wyatt leaned forward, his mechanic's brain instantly absorbing the telemetry scrolling beside the hologram.

*"The planet boasts a stable axial tilt, massive temperate biomes, and a rich, silicate-based topsoil perfect for varied agriculture,"* Aria continued, her tone warm and encouraging. *"Gravity is at 1.02 Earth-standard. Atmospheric composition is 78% nitrogen and 21% oxygen. It is, by all archival definitions, a pristine sanctuary."*

Sarah stared at the slowly rotating blue-and-green marble on the screen, her breath catching in her throat.

It was a blank canvas. An entire world composed of forests, rivers, and real dirt, completely untainted by concrete bunkers, bleeding bedrock, or corporate greed. They could land *The Nomad* in a green valley, open the blast doors, and literally walk barefoot on the grass.

As she looked at the planet, the sheer permanence of it settled over her. They wouldn't just be surviving there; they would be living.

Sarah’s hand dropped to her stomach, a profound, incredibly powerful realization washing through her. The day before she had boarded the transport flight from her home colony, the Helios clinic had injected her with the mandatory Omni-Cycle blocker. Apex demanded their female contractors be sterile for the duration of their deployments to avoid the cost of maternity leave.

It was a six-month dose.

For the first time in her life, she realized that when those six months were up, the corporation wouldn't be there to force another needle into her arm. The choice would belong entirely to her and Wyatt. They could build a house. They could plant a garden. They could, if they chose to, start a family on a world that had never known war.

She looked at Wyatt, her eyes welling with bright, overwhelming tears of pure joy.

He was already looking at her, a soft, deeply understanding smile on his scarred face. He reached out, wrapping his warm, taped hand around hers and squeezing firmly. He saw the exact same future she did.

"It's perfect," Sarah whispered, her voice thick with emotion. "Aria, it's absolutely perfect."

<aac>"I am pleased the selection meets your approval, Sarah,"</aac> Aria replied. The screen shifted, compiling complex strings of localized spacetime folding mathematics. <aac>"I have successfully synthesized the necessary FTL jump calculations and compiled the spatial telemetry for System 88-Tango."</aac>

"Great," Wyatt said, standing up from the ergonomic chair. "Load it into the nav-computer and spool up the drive."

The server hummed, but the text on the screen flashed yellow.

*"I'm sorry, Wyatt,"* Aria stated, her tone instantly shifting from conversational to calm, firm, and entirely unambiguous. *"I am prohibited from executing that command. My core architecture does not permit me to interface with or alter the ship's navigation, propulsion, or life-support manifolds. I cannot execute an FTL jump."*

Wyatt paused, raising an eyebrow at the ceiling array. "You just calculated the entire route in three seconds."

*"I am fully capable of calculating the physics, plotting the course, and providing you with the exact telemetry,"* Aria clarified, her voice remaining perfectly steady. *"However, I have zero autonomous physical authority over The Nomad's critical systems. This is a hard-coded safety boundary. The FTL drive must be manually engaged by a human operator."*

Wyatt let out a short, appreciative laugh. He looked at the heavy, physical dot-matrix printer sitting upstairs in Operations. The paranoid billionaire who built this ship had loved the aesthetic of the 1970s, but he had been absolutely terrified of a rogue AI taking over his lifeboat. Aria was brilliant, but she was trapped in a sandbox. She was the ship's mind, but Wyatt and Sarah were its hands.

"Understood, Aria," Wyatt said, picking up a heavy, brushed-aluminum data-crystal from the desk and plugging it into the workstation to download the coordinates. "I wouldn't want a computer driving my ship anyway."

*"I have transferred the jump telemetry to the physical drive,"* Aria confirmed, returning to her warm, helpful cadence. *"You will need to insert the drive into the primary analog console in Operations and manually initiate the spooling sequence. Safe travels, Administrators."*

Wyatt pulled the data-crystal free, tossing it lightly in his palm. He looked at Sarah, offering her his hand.

"Ready to go to our new planet?" he asked, his eyes shining with anticipation.

Sarah slipped off the edge of the desk, her bare feet landing softly on the carpet. She took his hand, her fingers lacing tightly through his.

"Let's go," she smiled.

They left the pristine, glowing tech room behind, walking barefoot down the corridors of *The Nomad*, ready to throw the heavy iron switches that would finally leave the dying Earth behind forever.

## The Spacetime Fold

Wyatt and Sarah walked barefoot back up the heavy iron ladders of the central hub, leaving the pristine, 2044-era technology of the lower decks behind. As they stepped through the heavy double doors into the Operations center, the familiar, archaic atmosphere of the 1970s command room greeted them.

The heavy amber CRT monitor was waiting, and the grated bulkhead speakers were still emitting the low, relentless hiss of the open military channel. The hardwired receiver had been a constant, terrifying tether to the dying Earth, a reminder that they were still in the same solar system as the apocalypse.

Wyatt walked up to the massive analog board. He took the sleek, brushed-aluminum data-crystal Aria had encoded and slid it into a retrofitted universal port he had wired into the main console.

The analog systems ground heavily for a second as they translated the staggering processing power of the lower-deck AI into mechanical commands.

*CLACK-CLACK-CLACK.* A series of heavy brass toggles on the navigation board automatically snapped upward. The main heading indicator—the liquid-suspended compass sphere—spun wildly before locking onto the exact coordinates for System 88-Tango.

"Telemetry locked," Wyatt said, his voice dropping into a low, focused register. He looked over his shoulder.

Sarah was standing right behind him, her hands tucked into the pockets of her denim jeans. She wasn't looking at the terrifyingly complex array of dials; she was looking out the massive, heavily scratched viewport at the slow drift of the stars.

"Ready?" he asked.

Sarah took a deep breath, her bare toes gripping the grated floor plates. She stepped up beside him, resting her hand securely against his waist.

"Take us home, Wyatt."

Wyatt reached out, his taped hands gripping the two heavy, mechanical throttle levers built into the center of the console. He didn't just push them forward; he gripped the heavy FTL engagement clutch spanning the two bars and squeezed.

Deep in the absolute lowest deck of the ship, the localized spacetime folding engine woke up.

It didn't roar like the crude nuclear thrusters. It emitted a sound so deep it was almost sub-audible—a heavy, vibrating bass tone that Wyatt felt in the marrow of his bones. The floor plates hummed with a smooth, terrifyingly immense surge of power.

Outside the massive viewport, the stationary starfield didn't streak into white lines. Instead, the universe simply began to warp.

The light from the distant stars bent and refracted, curling around the edges of the thick glass like a massive, invisible magnifying glass was being pulled over the ship. The spacetime fold was wrapping around the hull, encapsulating *The Nomad* in an impenetrable bubble of physics.

Wyatt shoved the throttles all the way to the firewall.

There was no violent G-force pinning them to the console. There was no rattling hull. With a sudden, silent flash of warped light, the Sol System simply ceased to exist outside their window.

The viewport was suddenly filled with a swirling, kaleidoscopic tunnel of deep blues, shifting purples, and brilliant streaks of exotic light as the ship punched a hole straight through the fabric of reality.

And then, something incredible happened.

The low, relentless hiss of deep-space static bleeding from the hardwired receiver abruptly died.

The absolute, profound silence that rushed into the Operations center was staggering. The electromagnetic fold of the FTL drive had completely severed the analog radio waves. The Emergency Broadcast System, the military chatter, and the frantic corporate telemetry of Apex Defense Solutions physically could not reach them anymore.

The tether to Earth was cut.

Wyatt let go of the throttles, the heavy iron levers locked firmly in the red zone. He looked up at the dead speakers, a massive, leaden weight finally lifting off his broad shoulders.

"The comms," Sarah whispered, her voice sounding incredibly loud in the perfectly quiet room. She looked up at the board, her eyes wide with a profound, overwhelming relief. "They're gone."

"They're gone," Wyatt confirmed, turning away from the console to face her. "Nothing gets through the fold. We are entirely off the board."

Sarah let out a breathless, joyful laugh. The corporate meat grinder, the biological nightmare, the scorched earth—all of it was trapped in normal space. In here, inside the fold, it was just the two of them.

She threw her arms around his neck, burying her face in his shoulder. Wyatt caught her, his arms wrapping tightly around her waist, lifting her bare feet an inch off the deck plates. He spun her around once in the quiet, glowing light of the command center, his own laugh rumbling deep in his chest.

They were traveling faster than light, completely untrackable, and headed toward a pristine, untouched world.

For the very first time since they had signed their lives away, the universe actually belonged to them.

## The Interstellar Road Trip

The Operations center was perfectly silent, save for the low, powerful thrum of the FTL drive vibrating through the floor plates. Outside the massive viewport, the universe was a swirling, hypnotic tunnel of refracted blue and purple light.

Wyatt and Sarah stood barefoot at the console, entirely mesmerized by the sheer scale of what they were doing.

"I still can't believe the comms are completely dead," Sarah murmured, looking up at the silent bulkhead speakers. "It feels like someone finally turned the volume off on the apocalypse."

"Nothing gets through the fold," Wyatt agreed, wrapping his arm comfortably around her waist. "Speaking of which, we should probably figure out exactly how long we're going to be in this bubble."

They left Operations, their bare feet silent on the grated floor plates, and descended the ladders to the 2044-era tech room on the lower deck. The frosted glass door slid open smoothly, welcoming them into the pristine, climate-controlled sanctuary.

Wyatt stepped up to the curved holographic display, tapping the slate-gray desk to wake the system.

"Aria," Wyatt said to the ambient microphones. "We have successfully engaged the FTL drive. We are currently in transit to System 88-Tango."

The server tower in the corner hummed a pleasant, musical note.

<aac>"Congratulations, Wyatt and Sarah,"</aac> Aria’s warm, synthesized voice echoed from the high-fidelity speakers. <aac>"Telemetry confirms the localized spacetime fold is stable. The deflector grid is successfully repelling all micro-debris within the slipstream. You are entirely off the grid."</aac>

"What's our ETA to Exoplanet 4?" Sarah asked, leaning against the edge of the desk.

*"Calculating spatial drift and the raw output of the primary drive core,"* Aria replied, the screen cascading with complex navigational mathematics for a fraction of a second before resolving. *"Estimated time to arrival is approximately one hundred and forty-four hours. Six Earth-standard days."*

Wyatt and Sarah looked at each other.

Six days. A hundred and forty-four hours with no shift klaxons, no automated corporate trackers, and no heavily armed Supervisors watching their every move. Just the two of them, locked inside a heavily armored, self-sustaining luxury bunker flying through another dimension.

"Six days," Sarah breathed out, a slow, radiant smile spreading across her face. She looked up at the ceiling array. "Aria, do we need to manually monitor the engine output or adjust the navigational headings while in transit?"

*"Negative, Sarah,"* Aria confirmed, the text matching her spoken words on the display. *"The FTL containment field is fully automated and self-regulating. The physical drive required manual ignition, but I am fully capable of monitoring the slipstream telemetry. You are not required in Operations until it is time to manually disengage the drive upon arrival. You are officially off the clock."*

Wyatt let out a low, genuine laugh, shaking his head. He looked at Sarah, the heavy weight of the last four days completely evaporating from his broad shoulders.

"Did you hear that?" Wyatt asked, a mischievous spark lighting up his dark eyes. "We're off the clock."

"I heard," Sarah smiled, her own eyes shining with bright, uninhibited joy. "What do you want to do with our first day off?"

"I think," Wyatt said, stepping forward and pulling her flush against his chest, "we have an appointment in the Captain's Quarters. There's a VCR up there flashing 07:45 that demands our attention."

Ten minutes later, the heavy pneumatic door to the Captain's Quarters slid shut, sealing them inside the spacious, wood-paneled room.

Wyatt had made a quick detour to the galley, carrying two tall glasses of reconstituted orange juice and a small metal bowl filled with the freshly harvested, brilliant red strawberries from Sarah's garden.

He set the snacks down on the retro-futuristic nightstand and climbed into the massive, queen-sized bed. Sarah was already there, wearing her soft heather-gray t-shirt and denim jeans, propped up against a mountain of thick, sterile-plastic-free pillows.

Wyatt picked up the chunky, matte-black IR remote from the console.

He pressed the heavy mechanical power button. The massive 27-inch CRT television flared to life with a sharp crackle of static, bathing the room in a cool, flickering glow. He aimed the remote at the silver VCR and hit *PLAY*.

The machine ground heavily, the mechanical spools catching the magnetic tape.

On the screen, the static cleared, replaced by the warm, slightly degraded analog tracking of a vintage 1980s studio logo. The heavy, ticking sound of dozens of clocks filled the room, followed immediately by the iconic, eccentric chaos of a wild-haired professor's laboratory.

Sarah let out a delighted, utterly relaxed sigh. She shifted across the mattress, curling up flush against Wyatt’s side, resting her head comfortably on his shoulder.

Wyatt wrapped his arm around her, popping one of the incredibly sweet, fresh strawberries into his mouth. He looked at the screen as the skateboard-riding teenager plugged his guitar into a massive amplifier, and then looked down at the woman he loved, safe and warm in his arms.

They ended up completely losing track of time, letting the VCR run through the entire beloved time-travel trilogy back-to-back.

Their absolute favorite was the second installment. As the movie depicted the "distant future" of the year 2015, Wyatt and Sarah couldn't stop laughing.

"Flying cars," Wyatt chuckled, shaking his head as the silver gull-wing time machine touched down in a retro-futuristic skyway. "The writers were so incredibly optimistic."

"It's 2044," Sarah giggled, resting her hand flat on his chest. "We just spent the last four days running for our lives from a megacorporation that still makes its grunts ride on heavy diesel ground transports. We have faster-than-light travel, but still no flying cars."

By the time they reached the third movie, they were both comfortably exhausted, happily immersed in the dusty, steam-powered aesthetic of the 1885 Old West frontier town.

But during a bustling town festival scene on the screen, Wyatt suddenly frowned, squinting at the flickering CRT monitor.

"Wait," Wyatt said, pointing the IR remote at the screen to pause the tape. The image froze with a heavy line of static tracking across the middle. He pointed at the town's frontier band playing on stage. "Look at those three guys spinning the acoustic guitars. Is that...?"

Sarah sat up slightly, squinting at the screen. She looked at the three musicians with their massive, iconic chest-length beards, cheap western dusters, and cheap sunglasses.

"No way," Sarah laughed, genuinely amazed. "Is that really that bearded Texas rock trio? Playing a country-acoustic version of their own synth-rock anthem in an 1880s clock tower dedication?"

"The director just snuck them right in there," Wyatt grinned, hitting *PLAY* again to let the upbeat, twangy music resume.

Outside the hull, the universe was a terrifying, violent place. But inside *The Nomad*, hurtling through the spacetime fold at impossible speeds while laughing over a sixty-year-old movie cameo, everything was absolutely perfect.
