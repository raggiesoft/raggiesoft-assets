# Part 4: Early Dismissal

We stepped out of the Student Services center and back into the humid Virginia afternoon. The Open House was technically scheduled to run until five o'clock, with more breakout sessions, a student life panel, and an evening mixer in the dining hall. 

Cassandra, however, had seen enough. 

"The tactical objective has been achieved," Cassandra announced, locking her brakes near the campus transit shelter. "We have verified the accessibility tolerances of the housing blocks, mapped the core academic pathways, and assessed the technological infrastructure of the Computer Science department. There is no mathematical reason to remain here for the mixer."

My father looked at her, wiping sweat from his forehead. "Are you sure, Cass? We came all the way up here. We don't have to rush off."

"I have the data I need," Cassandra replied simply. She wasn't angry or disappointed. She was just ruthlessly efficient. Atlas OS/2 was a terminal diagnosis for the department's future, but the disability counselor's intel regarding Northwest Pacific University had just opened an entirely new strategic pathway. She was already mentally moving on to Seattle. "The CCT Gold Line 1 arrives in six minutes. It loops directly through the City of Fairfax."

Mom sighed in relief, clearly thrilled to be skipping a two-hour student panel in an un-air-conditioned lecture hall. "Fairfax it is. I think we've earned a break."

Casey looked down at her meticulously highlighted itinerary, visibly distressed by this sudden deviation from the schedule. "But the student life panel! We haven't investigated the extracurricular clubs!"

"You can investigate the ice cream options in historic downtown Fairfax, Casey," Dad offered smoothly, which instantly neutralized our little sister's objections. 

When the CCT bus pulled up, the ramp lowered, and we piled on. Instead of taking the loop back toward the Vienna Orange Line station, we rode it south into the heart of the City of Fairfax. 

The campus gave way to the historic downtown district, a charming cluster of old brick buildings, wide sidewalks, and sprawling oak trees. We disembarked near the old courthouse and spent the rest of the afternoon acting like a normal family on a summer vacation, completely detached from the high-stress logistics of college hunting.

We found a classic diner with checkered floors and vinyl booths. Dad ordered a massive plate of fries for the table, and Casey spent twenty minutes aggressively debating the mathematical superiority of chocolate milkshakes over vanilla. Cassandra sat by the window, sipping a soda and watching the traffic roll through the historic intersection. 

I sat across from her, dipping a fry in ketchup. "Are you disappointed?" I asked quietly, so Casey wouldn't hear and start another lecture. "About the computers?"

Cassandra looked away from the window, adjusting her glasses. The intense, calculating edge she had carried all morning was gone, replaced by a quiet sense of clarity. 

"No, David," she said softly. "It's better to find out now. A system is only as strong as its foundation. If you build a house on an obsolete architecture, it doesn't matter how nice the dorms are. It's still going to collapse."

She looked back out the window, watching a Capital Transit bus rumble past the diner. 

"We just have to find a school that's building for the future," she added. "Not the past."
