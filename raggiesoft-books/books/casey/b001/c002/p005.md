# Part 5: The Evening Routine – 8:00 PM

By the time we navigated the CCT bus back to the motel in Vienna, the sun had set, and the oppressive Virginia heat had finally broken into a mild, humid evening. 

We retreated into our adjoining rooms. Dad collapsed onto one of the beds in my parents' room, grabbing the television remote, while Casey immediately unpacked her notebooks onto the small desk in our room, determined to mathematically graph the efficiency of the CCT bus loops we had just taken.

Cassandra wheeled herself to the center of our room, locking her brakes. The grueling transit rides and the long day of navigating the GWU campus had finally taken their toll. Her shoulders slumped slightly, and the sharp, unyielding energy that usually drove her had faded into physical exhaustion.

The adjoining door opened, and Mom stepped through. 

"Alright, Cass," Mom said softly, her tone shifting from the exasperated parent managing a college tour to the focused, quiet cadence she always used for the evening routine. "Let's get you ready for bed."

Casey immediately looked up from her notebooks, dropping her pencil. "I can assist," she volunteered eagerly, hopping off her chair. "I have analyzed the spatial dimensions of the accessible shower stall. If we utilize a pivot transfer—"

"No, Casey," Mom interrupted gently, placing a hand on our little sister's shoulder to stop her forward momentum. "Thank you, sweetheart, but Cassandra needs privacy. And this requires an adult."

Casey frowned, clearly offended that her logistical expertise was being rejected. "But I am part of the support team. I secured the breakfast perimeter. I measured the desk clearance."

"I know you did," Cassandra said, offering Casey a tired, affectionate smile. "And your data collection was flawless. But this isn't logistics, Casey. It's just my ADLs."

Activities of Daily Living. It was the clinical term Cassandra always used for the deeply personal, physically demanding reality of her condition—things like transferring, bathing, and changing clothes. 

Mom wheeled Cassandra into the large, accessible bathroom, pulling the heavy door shut behind them until it clicked, leaving Casey standing in the middle of the room, looking utterly defeated.

I walked over and pulled my eight-year-old sister into a hug. She was stiff for a second, still annoyed, before she finally sighed and leaned against me. 

"It's okay, Case," I told her quietly, resting my chin on top of her head. "You did a great job today. But sometimes, Cassandra just needs the adults to help her."

"It's inefficient," Casey mumbled against my shirt. "A properly integrated support team shouldn't have artificial age restrictions."

I smiled, letting her go so she could return to her transit graphs. But as I sat down on the edge of my Twin XL bed, listening to the muffled sounds of the running shower and Mom's quiet, encouraging voice guiding Cassandra through her transfers, I found myself staring at the closed bathroom door. 

Casey viewed our sister's life as a series of complex logistical equations to be solved. But for the first time, at ten years old, I was starting to recognize the profound physical vulnerability beneath Cassandra's brilliant, armored exterior. She relied on our parents to maintain her independence. 

A quiet thought sparked in the back of my mind. Our parents weren't going to be around forever to help her with her ADLs. What would happen when Cassandra went to college? Or when she got her own apartment? 

*What if it was me?* I thought to myself. *If it was just me and Cassandra... would I be strong enough to step in and help her?*

I didn't know the answer yet. But as I listened to the water running, I realized that I wanted to learn.
