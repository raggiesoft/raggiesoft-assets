# Part 6: The "Air" Problem

The moment we passed through the heavy revolving doors of the Finch Air and Space Museum, the brutal D.C. humidity was instantly replaced by a wall of aggressively purified, freezing air-conditioning. 

Cassandra took a deep breath, her shoulders finally dropping an inch as the thermal relief set in. 

I barely noticed the temperature drop. I was already practically vibrating with excitement. 

The main entrance emptied directly into the Milestones of Flight hall. It was a massive, soaring atrium filled with the greatest technological achievements in human history. To our left was the Wright Flyer. Hanging from the rafters above us was the bright orange Bell X-1, the bullet-shaped plane Chuck Yeager had used to shatter the sound barrier. 

And sitting dead ahead of us, encased in heavy acrylic shielding, was the Apollo 11 Command Module, *Columbia*. The actual, physical spacecraft that had carried three men to the moon and back. 

I abandoned the family completely, jogging straight for the heavy command module. I pressed my hands against the low railing, staring at the scarred, heat-scorched base of the capsule. The burn marks from its violent atmospheric reentry were still perfectly preserved on the ablative heat shield. 

"David, wait up!" Mom called out, hurrying after me with Casey trailing behind her. 

"Look at it," I whispered, completely mesmerized as I stared through the small triangular window at the impossibly cramped, dial-covered control panel inside the capsule. "They strapped themselves to the top of a controlled explosion and went into a total vacuum in this thing."

"It's a marvel of mechanical engineering," Cassandra agreed, wheeling up beside me. She looked up at the ceiling, scanning the massive exhibits suspended from the steel trusses. 

"I have a logistical grievance," Casey announced loudly, looking around the massive hall with a deep scowl on her face. "The spatial allocation of this facility is profoundly imbalanced."

"What do you mean?" Dad asked, looking up at *The Spirit of St. Louis*. 

"It's called the Air and Space Museum," I muttered, crossing my arms as I looked past the Apollo capsule. I shared the exact same frustration. "But half the building is just... airplanes."

It was a point of deep, persistent annoyance for me. Whenever I went to a museum, I wanted to see the cosmos. I wanted to see planetary rovers, orbital mechanics, and astrophysics. I wanted to see the terrifying, beautiful vacuum of deep space. 

Instead, the awe of the cosmos was constantly diluted by giant hangars filled with World War II bombers, biplanes, and commercial jetliners. To me, atmospheric flight felt mundane. It was tethered to the Earth. I didn't care about the mechanics of moving through air. 

"I wish there was just a *Space* museum," I grumbled, glaring up at a perfectly preserved P-51 Mustang hanging directly over my head. "No planes. No helicopters. Just the stars."

"Well, you have to tolerate the atmosphere to get to the vacuum, David," Cassandra pointed out dryly. She pointed across the hall. "Now, if you can temporarily suspend your hostility toward atmospheric aviation, the Lunar Module is in the next gallery."

That was all I needed to hear. 

I abandoned the airplanes entirely and practically sprinted into the massive Space Hall. Sitting right in the center of the room, wrapped in shimmering gold foil, was the Lunar Module LM-2. Behind it loomed the massive, walk-through backup model of the Skylab orbital workshop. 

Casey immediately marched up to the Skylab exhibit, her eyes wide as she began analyzing the zero-gravity toilet and the sleep stations strapped to the bulkheads. 

"The zero-gravity ADL logistics are fascinating," Casey whispered, furiously pulling out her notebook. 

I stood in front of the Lunar Module, staring up at the fragile, insect-like landing legs. I didn't care that the museum was filled with airplanes. For the next two hours, I was completely lost in the stars.
