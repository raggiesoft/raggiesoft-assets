Back at the motel, the exhaustion of the day finally caught up with us. Mom and Dad had opened the heavy adjoining door between our two rooms, allowing the cool air-conditioning and the quiet hum of the television to drift back and forth. 

While Dad was next door sorting through our luggage, Mom was in our room, sitting on the edge of Cassandra’s Queen bed to assist with her evening ADLs. 

Cassandra was propped up against the pillows, her college prospectus folders spread out across the blanket. Even after a grueling ten-hour day navigating the D.C. transit grid in a wheelchair, her brain was still moving at lightspeed. 

"This is highly amusing," Cassandra announced suddenly, adjusting her glasses as she read over a brochure from George Wythe University. 

I was sitting cross-legged on the adjacent Queen bed, trying to draw a schematic of the Lunar Module in a spiral notebook. Casey was lying on her stomach next to me, kicking her feet in the air. 

"What is it?" Mom asked gently, carefully adjusting the braces on Cassandra's legs. 

"The institutional friction," Cassandra explained, a rare, genuine smile pulling at the corner of her mouth. "George Wythe University is fiercely protective of its acronym. But there is a massive, incredibly well-funded private college right across the river in D.C. called *Greater Washington University*. They both aggressively claim the 'GWU' abbreviation in academic literature. The admissions office for George Wythe apparently spends half their annual budget just rerouting misdelivered mail meant for the other campus."

I snorted. "That's stupid. Why don't they just call one of them George Wythe and the other one Greater Washington?"

"Because of branding ego, David," Cassandra said logically. 

Casey, however, had stopped kicking her feet. She looked up from the bed, her face turning bright red as her eight-year-old brain processed the sheer logistical absurdity of two massive institutions fighting over three letters. 

A small giggle escaped her lips. Then another. 

Within seconds, Casey was laughing. Not just a normal laugh, but a full-body, gasping, wheezing laugh. She rolled onto her back, clutching her stomach as the laughter cascaded out of her. It was entirely disproportionate to the joke. 

"Casey?" Mom asked, pausing her work on Cassandra's legs, her brow furrowing in sudden concern. "Honey, are you okay?"

Casey couldn't answer. The laughter had rapidly mutated. The sheer emotional overload of the day—the transit maps, the thunderstorms, the massive crowds, the sensory overload of the museum—had all bottlenecked in her brain. The laughter fractured, turning into panicked, frustrated tears. She started to thrash slightly on the mattress, her hands curling into tight fists as she cried, completely overwhelmed by her own nervous system. 

She wasn't throwing a tantrum. She was short-circuiting. 

I didn't think. I just reacted. 

I dropped my notebook, scrambled across the mattress, and pulled my little sister into a massive, suffocatingly tight bear hug. I wrapped my arms around her shoulders and squeezed, applying as much deep, even physical pressure to her torso as my ten-year-old arms could manage. I buried my chin in her shoulder and locked my grip, refusing to let go. 

Instantly, Casey stopped thrashing. The deep, sustained physical pressure acted like a physical anchor. Her rigid muscles went completely slack, and she melted into my arms, letting out a long, shuddering sigh of relief. 

The room went completely silent, save for the hum of the AC unit. 

Mom was staring at us from the other bed. Her hands were frozen over Cassandra's braces. I saw a profound, quiet realization wash over her face. She was a mother analyzing her children, logging the data, and finally realizing that something was fundamentally, neuro-divergently different about the way Casey and I processed the world. 

Cassandra saw it, too. She looked at me, holding Casey in that tight, regulatory squeeze, and her analytical expression softened into something fiercely protective. 

"Mom," Cassandra said quietly, breaking the silence. "Can you transfer me?"

Mom blinked, pulling herself out of her thoughts. "Transfer you? To where, sweetie? You're already in bed."

"To their bed," Cassandra clarified, gesturing toward us. "I require sibling affection."

Mom smiled softly. She stood up, wrapped her arms under Cassandra's shoulders, and carefully lifted her, pivoting her across the small gap between the two beds. She settled Cassandra onto the mattress right beside us. 

I loosened my grip on Casey just enough to let Cassandra wedge herself in. The three of us curled up together in a tangled pile of limbs on the single Queen bed. 

"Sleep well, my brilliant kids," Mom whispered, pulling the heavy comforter up over us. She didn't force us to separate. She just turned off the bedside lamp and quietly retreated to the adjoining room, intentionally leaving the door wide open so she could hear us if we needed her in the dark.
