# Part 1: The Union Station Bottleneck – 12:30 PM

We woke up the next morning feeling the cumulative ache of a three-day, high-intensity transit deployment. We ate a quiet breakfast in the motel lobby, loaded up our luggage, and boarded the CCT bus one last time to make the short trip back to the Vienna surface station. 

Our departure vector was entirely different from our arrival. 

Because we were leaving the city via Washington Union Station instead of Alexandria, the route required diving into the heart of the District. We rode the Orange Line eastbound, rattling under the city until we reached Metro Center. From the lower-level Blue/Orange platform, we hauled our luggage and Cassandra's wheelchair up the elevators to the upper-level Red Line platform, catching a train heading east. 

When the automated voice finally announced Union Station, Dad looked up at the curved concrete ceiling of the platform with a nostalgic smile. 

"I still remember when they rebranded this place for the 1976 Bicentennial," he mused as we waited for the elevator doors to open. "They called it the National Visitor Center. Put a massive slide show in the main hall. It was a complete disaster."

"It's still a disaster, Dad," Cassandra muttered tightly. 

As soon as the elevator doors opened onto the main concourse, I instantly understood why Cassandra had gone out of her way to route us through the quiet, surface-level Alexandria station two days ago. 

Union Station was massive. It was a cavernous, Beaux-Arts cathedral of echoing marble, swarming with tens of thousands of commuters, tourists, and business travelers. The acoustic reflections off the vaulted ceilings created a deafening, continuous roar of overlapping voices, train announcements, and rolling luggage wheels. 

It hit me like a physical wall. I stopped dead in my tracks, my hands instinctively coming up to cover my ears as my brain desperately tried to filter out the auditory assault. 

Casey wasn't doing much better. She was clinging tightly to the back of Cassandra's wheelchair, her eyes darting frantically around the massive, overwhelming space, her breathing shallow and fast. 

Dad paused, looking back at us. The cheerful nostalgia instantly vanished from his face. He watched me pressing my hands over my ears, and then looked at Casey's white-knuckled grip on the wheelchair. He glanced at Mom, and I saw a silent, heavy look pass between them. Mom's suspicions from the previous night had just been confirmed in broad daylight. There was no denying it anymore. Casey and I were both rapidly approaching sensory overload. 

"Okay, team," Dad said softly, stepping between us and the crowd to act as a physical buffer. "Let's push through to the food court on the lower level. It's a bit more contained."

We retreated to the basement food court, grabbing quick, overpriced meals while waiting for our train. But even sitting down didn't solve Cassandra's primary logistical grievance with the station. 

"This is the fundamental flaw of WAS," Cassandra complained, aggressively stabbing a piece of lettuce in her salad. "At Alexandria, the platform is entirely accessible from the surface. I can roll directly up to the train. Here, the boarding gates are locked. We are forced to wait in the main hall, completely reliant on the ARTS Red Cap service to manually escort us down an employee elevator to the platform. It strips me of my autonomy."

She hated being forced to wait for an escort. She knew exactly which track the 3:50 PM Northeast Regional was arriving on, and she was more than capable of navigating to it herself, but the architecture of Union Station explicitly prevented her from doing so. 

Eventually, a Red Cap agent arrived, unlocked the gate, and escorted our family down the heavy freight elevator to the damp, diesel-smelling platform below. 

The moment we boarded the quiet, climate-controlled ARTS train, the tension instantly evaporated from the family. Casey and I slumped into our seats, exhausted by the sensory gauntlet of the station. Cassandra locked her wheelchair into the ADA tie-downs, looking visibly relieved to be back in a controlled, predictable environment. 

The train pulled out of Washington, D.C. at 3:50 PM. For the next four hours, we rolled smoothly down the Mid-Atlantic corridor, watching the sprawling suburbs give way to the dense forests and rivers of southern Virginia. 

By the time the train finally pulled into the Newport News (NPN) station at 8:09 PM, it was completely dark outside. We unloaded our bags and piled exhausted into the trusty family station wagon waiting in the parking lot.
