# Part 1: The Lightning Strike

I woke up coughing. 

The heavy comforter was still pulled securely over my head, and Casey was still sleeping soundly, curled up tightly against my side with Leona tucked under her chin. But the air in the room felt thick. 

I pushed the heavy blanket off my face, blinking sleep out of my eyes. The storm was still raging outside, the steady, rhythmic drumming of heavy rain hitting the roof of the house. 

But beneath the smell of the damp Virginia morning, there was something else. Acrid, harsh, and unmistakable. 

Smoke. 

I scrambled out of bed, instantly panicked. Cassandra was awake, lying against her pillows, calmly staring out her rain-streaked bedroom window. 

"Cassandra!" I gasped, coughing again. "Do you smell that? Is the house on fire?"

"Our house is not on fire, David," she said simply, pointing out the window. "Look."

I hurried over to the glass and pressed my face against the pane. Down the street, through the heavy gray sheets of rain, a massive plume of dark, oily smoke was billowing up into the sky. Thick orange flames were tearing through the roof of the old, dilapidated Victorian house sitting three lots down from ours. Two fire trucks were already parked in the street, their flashing red and white emergency lights reflecting off the flooded asphalt. 

The front door of Cassandra's bedroom opened. Mom and Dad walked in, carrying fresh clothes and a basin of warm water to begin Cassandra's morning ADLs. 

"I see everyone is awake," Mom said softly, setting the basin on the nightstand. She looked tired, her eyes lingering on Casey, who was just starting to stir under the blankets. 

"What happened?" I asked, pointing out the window at the burning house. 

"Lightning strike," Dad explained, walking over and resting a heavy hand on my shoulder. "Hit the roof right around ten o'clock last night. Fire department has been out there for hours trying to keep it from spreading to the neighboring trees."

My eyes went wide. Ten o'clock. 

*That* was the massive, earth-shaking, concussive boom that had driven Casey and me out of our room and into Cassandra's bed. It hadn't just been a loud crack of thunder. A massive bolt of electricity had literally detonated a house three doors down. 

"Is everyone okay?" I asked nervously, suddenly hyper-aware of how close we had been to the blast. 

"It's fine, David. The house has been abandoned for years," Mom reassured me, pulling the comforter back to begin stretching Cassandra's legs. "No one was inside. Just a lot of property damage."

Casey finally sat up, rubbing her eyes and clutching her stuffed lion cub. She sniffed the air, her nose wrinkling at the sharp smell of the smoke filtering through the window seams. 

"I don't like that," she muttered, pulling Leona tighter against her chest. 

"It's just an old house down the street, sweetie," Dad said gently. "The fire department is taking care of it."

Dad looked down at me, and then over at Casey. For the first time, he didn't tell us we were "too old" to be sleeping in our sister's bed. He didn't tell me to be brave about the storm, or tell Casey to stop clinging to her toy. The look in his eyes was different today—softer, more observant. He had watched us melt down in Union Station, and he had watched us sprint in absolute sensory terror into Cassandra's room last night. 

He was starting to understand what Mom had realized in the motel room. We weren't just being difficult or acting like toddlers. Our nervous systems were fundamentally wired differently. 

"Why don't you two go get washed up?" Dad suggested quietly. "I'll make some pancakes. Then we can sit down and talk about the Washington trip."

I nodded, grabbing Casey's hand. As we walked out of the room, I looked back at the window one last time. The abandoned house was still burning, the flames fighting a losing battle against the torrential coastal rain. 

The Washington scouting mission was officially over, but it felt like a completely different chapter of our lives was just beginning.
