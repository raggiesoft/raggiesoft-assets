# Part 2: The Ecology Camp Logistics

Dad's buttermilk pancakes were legendary in our house, mostly because he cooked them with absolute, clinical precision. He used a digital timer and a laser thermometer on the griddle to ensure a perfectly uniform golden-brown crust on every single one. 

Casey and I sat at the kitchen table, methodically cutting our pancakes into perfectly symmetrical grids before pouring the syrup. The residual smell of smoke from the lightning strike down the street had mostly dissipated, replaced by the comforting scent of butter and maple. 

At the far end of the table, Cassandra had already pushed her plate aside. She had Dad's heavy laptop open, the loud, screeching tones of the 14.4k dial-up modem filling the kitchen as she connected to the internet. 

"Alright," Mom said, sitting down with her coffee mug and looking at me and Casey. "Let's review the upcoming operational schedule. Summer vacation officially starts next week. Which means it is time for the Virginia Dominion University Coastal Ecology Camp."

I perked up. I had been attending the VDU summer camp for two years now, but this year was going to be fundamentally different. 

The camp accepted kids from ages eight to twelve. For my first two years, I had been in the 8-to-10-year-old junior cohort, navigating the intense schedule of coastal field trips and marine biology labs entirely on my own. But Casey had just turned eight. 

"This is a statistically unique operational window," Casey announced, tapping her fork against her plate. "I am eight. You are ten. The junior cohort caps at age ten. Therefore, this is the only year we will be placed in the exact same field group."

"Exactly," Dad said, flipping the last pancake onto a plate. "Next year, David ages into the senior cohort. But for this summer, you two are a package deal."

"Mom and Dad already spoke with the program directors," I told Casey, feeling a deep sense of relief. "They filed a formal accommodation. Whenever the cohort has to split into pairs, we are officially assigned as each other's field partners."

Casey's shoulders visibly dropped as the tension left her body. "That is highly optimal. I have already reviewed the syllabus. We are taking the VDU passenger vans for all off-campus deployments instead of massive motorcoaches."

"What's the schedule?" I asked, shoveling a perfectly square piece of pancake into my mouth. 

"Week One is Coastal Ecology," Casey recited from memory. "We take the university vans across the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel to Kiptopeake State Park. Week Two is Atmospheric Dynamics, culminating in a field trip to the NAA Langley Research Center to observe the VDU wind tunnels."

"That's just moving air," I grumbled. 

"Correct. Which leads into Week Three: Aerospace," Casey continued. "A deployment to the Dominion Air & Space Center in Hampton."

"Again with the 'Air'," I muttered, stabbing my fork into my plate. "I just want the space part. They waste too much floor space on atmospheric flight."

"And finally, Week Four," Casey finished, ignoring my complaint. "Marine Biology. We get to visit the Dominion Marine Science Museum." She pulled a small notebook out of her pocket. "I am currently calculating the logistical transit times for the university passenger vans for all four routes."

"I am officially abandoning the East Coast," Cassandra announced loudly from the other end of the table, cutting through our camp discussion. 

Dad paused, spatula in hand. "Excuse me?"

Cassandra tapped the bulky screen of the laptop. "Atlas OS/2 is dead. George Wythe and Greater Washington are locked in a useless branding war. The East Coast technology sector is stagnant." She adjusted her glasses, a sharp, determined look in her eye. "I have established a direct connection to the admissions server for Northwest Pacific University in Seattle."

"Seattle," Mom repeated slowly, taking a sip of her coffee. "Cassandra, that's three thousand miles away."

"It is three miles away from the corporate headquarters of Quantum Corporation," Cassandra corrected her instantly. "NPU's computer science department has a direct pipeline to the Quantum OS 95 development team. William Keswick and Steven Ballantine are building the future of the digital world in the Pacific Northwest. I need to be in that ecosystem."

Mom and Dad exchanged a look. They had spent three days physically hauling Cassandra's wheelchair through the punishing terrain of D.C. just to figure out local college options, only for her to instantly pivot across the entire continent. 

But looking at the sheer, unyielding determination on Cassandra's face, they knew there was no point in arguing. The decision had been made. 

"Well," Dad sighed, setting the spatula down. "I suppose I should start looking up accessible airfare to Seattle."
