# Part 1: The Junior Cohort

The VDU Coastal Ecology Camp officially commenced on a sweltering Monday morning in late June. 

Mom drove the three of us from Virginia Beach into Norfolk, pulling the station wagon into the sprawling, heavily shaded campus of Virginia Dominion University. I grabbed our shared cooler bag from the backseat while Mom unlatched Cassandra's wheelchair from the trunk. 

"Have a good first day, you two," Mom smiled, leaning down to give Casey a hug. "David, stick with your sister. Remember your accommodations."

"I know," I said, slinging the strap of the cooler over my shoulder. 

While Casey and I headed toward the massive university recreation center for camp orientation, Mom and Cassandra headed in the opposite direction. Cassandra had attended academic camps here when she was younger, but today she had a specific, tactical objective. VDU was built entirely at sea level—making it one of the flattest, most wheelchair-friendly campuses on the East Coast. Cassandra wanted to run a real-world stress test on the campus infrastructure to see exactly how easily she could navigate it without assistance. 

While plotting their route through the student union, they unexpectedly stumbled across a massive university career fair. Since they were already there, Cassandra decided they might as well roll through the exhibit hall and see what kind of technology companies were recruiting locally. 

But for Casey and me, our focus was strictly on the Junior Cohort. 

The recreation center gymnasium was an absolute sensory nightmare. Dozens of kids between the ages of eight and ten were running around, screaming, bouncing basketballs, and echoing loudly off the polished wooden floors. 

Casey immediately shrank back against my side, her hands coming up to cover her ears. She hated unstructured social environments. 

I didn't say a word. I just wrapped my arm tightly around her shoulders, shielding her from the chaotic crowd, and guided her toward the far wall of the gymnasium, away from the screaming kids. We sat down on the bottom row of the wooden bleachers, our shoulders pressed together, creating our own isolated bubble of order amidst the chaos. 

A camp counselor with a bright yellow VDU polo shirt eventually blew a whistle, corralled us into a circle, and forced everyone to play a grueling, twenty-minute icebreaker game involving throwing a tennis ball and shouting our favorite colors. 

Casey and I participated with absolute, robotic minimalism. When the ball was thrown to Casey, she caught it, deadpanned, "Teal," and instantly threw it to me. I caught it, said, "Black," and threw it to the counselor. 

We didn't care about the icebreakers. We were just waiting for the science. 

By noon, we had survived the morning orientation and a preliminary lecture on coastal tidal patterns. The camp broke for lunch, releasing us out onto the grassy campus quad. 

Casey and I immediately retreated to the shade of a massive oak tree, far away from the loud, chaotic clumps of kids trading snacks. I unzipped the shared cooler bag and pulled out our lunch. 

Mom had packed our exact, highly-specific preferences: two peanut butter and jelly sandwiches on honey wheat bread (crusts meticulously removed), a shared family-size bag of potato chips, and two cold 20-ounce bottles of soda. 

"The instructional pacing of the morning lecture was acceptable," Casey noted, taking a precise, measured bite of her sandwich. "But the social integration protocols are highly inefficient."

"Icebreakers are a waste of time," I agreed, popping the cap off my soda. "But we only have to deal with the classroom stuff until Friday. Then we get to deploy."

Casey nodded, a rare smile appearing on her face. Field trips were always on Fridays. This week, we were scheduled to take the VDU passenger vans across the bay to Kiptopeake State Park to analyze coastal erosion. 

"I am also highly anticipating Week Four," Casey said, chewing thoughtfully. "The camp director confirmed we have been granted access to the soft opening of the Owls Creek Marsh Pavilion at the Dominion Marine Science Museum. They are using our camp cohort to stress-test the new building before it opens to the general public next year."

"And the stingrays," I reminded her. 

Casey's eyes lit up. The *Hooray for Rays* stingray touch tank was the brand new, highly publicized flagship exhibit in the main building. "Yes. The tactile interaction with cartilaginous fish will be fascinating."

I smiled, leaning back against the trunk of the oak tree. The social aspects of camp were exhausting, but as long as we were partnered up, we could endure anything. We had our perfectly structured lunch, we had a clear itinerary for the week, and we had the science to look forward to.
