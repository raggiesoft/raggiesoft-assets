# Part 2: The Ecology Lab

After we finished our meticulously packed lunch under the oak tree, a counselor blew a whistle, signaling the end of free time. The Junior Cohort was herded out of the humid afternoon heat and into one of VDU's sprawling undergraduate science buildings. 

The air-conditioning hit us like a physical wall, immediately bringing my sensory stress levels down. We filed into a massive biology lab outfitted with long, black soapstone tables, stainless steel sinks, and rows of high-powered microscopes. 

"Alright, junior ecologists!" the head counselor announced, clapping his hands together. "For our afternoon session, we're going to practice cataloging marine specimens! Find your assigned partner and grab a tray from the front!"

Instantly, the room devolved into chaos. Kids were shouting, fighting over who got to be partners, and scrambling to grab the best-looking trays of shells, dried starfish, and sand dollars. 

Casey didn't even flinch. She just turned, looked at me, and nodded. 

Because Mom and Dad had filed the formal accommodation, we didn't have to participate in the frantic partner-selection ritual. I calmly walked to the front of the room, grabbed a specimen tray while the other kids were distracted, and carried it back to a quiet corner station at the very back of the lab. 

As soon as I set the tray down, we went to work. 

The other kids were treating the exercise like a toy box, loudly clattering the shells together and spilling sand all over the expensive soapstone tables. We, however, treated the tray like a crime scene. Because I had severe dysgraphia—meaning my handwriting was completely illegible—Casey was in charge of all data entry. She opened my spiral notebook, her pencil poised with perfect, rigid posture. 

I carefully removed a pair of plastic tweezers from the supply kit and began meticulously sorting the specimens into perfectly straight, symmetrical grids on the soapstone based on phylum and size. 

"Bivalvia," I announced, holding up a small, ridged shell and cross-referencing it with the laminated identification chart. "*Crassostrea virginica*." 

A kid at the next table over paused from trying to balance a starfish on his nose and stared at me like I had just spoken alien code. 

"Estimated age based on calcification?" Casey asked, not even looking up from her notebook as she wrote the Latin name down in flawless, uniform script. 

"Three years," I confirmed, placing it exactly in the center of the first grid. "Next."

For the next two hours, we completely tuned out the noise of the room. We operated like a highly efficient, closed-loop machine. I handled the tactile sorting and physical categorization, while Casey handled the data entry. We communicated exclusively in taxonomic Latin, much to the confusion of the nine-year-olds sitting around us. 

We were so hyper-fixated on the task that we didn't even notice the head counselor walking up behind us. 

"Whoa," the counselor whispered, staring down at our workstation. 

I tensed up, dropping my tweezers, afraid we were in trouble. Casey froze, her pencil hovering over the notebook. 

Our station was immaculate. The specimens were arranged in flawless geometric patterns. Casey's notebook was filled with perfectly aligned columns of taxonomic data. Meanwhile, the table next to us was covered in spilled water. 

"You guys did all this?" the counselor asked, sounding genuinely bewildered. "You didn't even need my help identifying the *Crassostrea virginica*?"

"The *Crassostrea virginica*'s ridge patterns are statistically distinct from the *Mercenaria mercenaria* population," Casey explained, completely deadpan. "The identification was elementary."

The counselor blinked, looking back and forth between us. He had clearly never dealt with neurodivergent, highly analytical children before. He slowly nodded, scribbled something down on his clipboard, and backed away. "Keep up the good work, you two."

"Social interaction concluded," I muttered, picking my tweezers back up. 

"Agreed," Casey said, seamlessly returning her focus to the notebook. "Now, hand me the *Geukensia demissa*."
