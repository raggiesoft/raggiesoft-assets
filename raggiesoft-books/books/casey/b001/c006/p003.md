# Part 3: The Catalyst – 4:00 PM

When camp officially concluded for the day, Mom and Cassandra were waiting for us at the edge of the sprawling university quad. 

Instead of heading straight back to the car to fight through the punishing rush hour traffic across the Southside, Mom steered us toward the massive VDU student union. We grabbed dinner at one of the quick-service food counters, sliding into a large vinyl booth in the back of the dining hall. 

I was exhausted. My social battery was completely drained, and all I wanted to do was stare at the wall. But Casey was operating on high alert. Her eyes were locked onto Cassandra. 

Cassandra was radiating a distinct, electric energy. She was trying to look nonchalant while peeling the foil off her sandwich, but there was an unmistakable, deeply smug smirk playing at the corners of her mouth. 

"You are suppressing highly sensitive information," Casey declared, pointing a french fry directly at our older sister. "Your micro-expressions indicate a successful tactical maneuver."

Mom laughed, taking a sip of her iced tea. "She's right, Cassandra. You might as well tell them. You've been practically vibrating for the last two hours."

Cassandra carefully set her sandwich down. The smug smirk finally broke into a wide, triumphant grin. 

"Quantum Corporation was at the career fair today," she said, her voice dropping to an intense, conspiratorial whisper. 

I blinked, my exhaustion momentarily forgotten. Quantum Corporation was the undisputed titan of the technology industry. They didn't typically waste their time recruiting at regional East Coast universities; they usually poached their talent straight out of Northwest Pacific University in Seattle or the Ivy League. 

"They were running a very small, highly selective booth," Cassandra continued, her eyes gleaming. "Looking for early-access talent. I rolled right up to the lead recruiter and started asking him deeply technical questions about the memory architecture in Quantum OS 95."

"Did he take you seriously?" I asked. I knew how dismissive adults could be when dealing with teenagers, let alone a teenage girl in a wheelchair. 

"He tried to brush me off at first," Cassandra said, her grin widening. "He asked if I even knew how to write a basic batch script. So, I started speaking fluent Q32API."

I grinned. *Quantum 32 API*. It was the absolute bleeding-edge architecture for Quantum's upcoming 32-bit operating systems. 

"I explained that I've been running Quantum OS for Workgroups 3.11 on my machine at home," Cassandra told us, leaning across the table. "Instead of standard 3.1. That means I have the Q32s extensions installed, which allows me to bypass the 16-bit limitations and write highly optimized, enterprise-level C++ code directly into the kernel."

"What did the recruiter do?" Casey asked, captivated. 

"His jaw practically hit the floor," Mom chuckled proudly. "He actually called his manager over from the other side of the booth just to listen to her talk."

"They were completely blown away," Cassandra said, leaning back in her chair and crossing her arms. "The manager started talking to me like I was an industry veteran. He asked how many years of professional enterprise experience I had, and requested my resume so they could fast-track me for a senior developer position. When I told them I was only seventeen and about to start my senior year of high school, they literally couldn't believe it."

"They thought you were already a software engineer?" I asked, my eyes wide. 

"They said I knew more about their own kernel architecture than half the people currently working in Redmond," Cassandra smirked. "Once they recovered from the shock that I was still in high school, they pivoted immediately."

Cassandra reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a heavy, glossy black envelope embossed with a silver Quantum Corporation logo. She slid it across the table. 

"They officially invited me to submit an entry for the Quantum Catalyst Initiative," she said, tapping the envelope. 

I stared at the silver logo. I had read about the Catalyst Initiative on early internet message boards. It was a brutal, uncompromising national coding challenge open exclusively to high school juniors and seniors. 

"There's only one national winner," I said quietly, looking up at her. 

"I know," Cassandra said, her voice entirely devoid of doubt. 

The stakes were astronomical. The sole winner of the Catalyst Initiative received the Vanguard Scholarship—a complete, all-expenses-paid full ride to Northwest Pacific University, a paid summer internship at the Quantum campus in Redmond, and a direct, guaranteed pipeline into the company upon graduation. 

"I have until March to write a program that pushes the Q32API architecture to its absolute breaking point," Cassandra said, looking around the table at us. Her eyes were burning with that familiar, intense neurodivergent hyperfixation. "And when I win, we are officially moving to Seattle."

Casey looked at the envelope, then looked up at Cassandra. "Your probability of failure is statistically zero."

Cassandra smiled. "I know."
