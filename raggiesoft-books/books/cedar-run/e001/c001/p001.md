## 4:30 PM — The Boundary Wire

The bottom strand of rusted barbed wire groaned under my boot heel as I held the middle wire up by a weathered tamarack post so Tessa and Sadie could duck through with Grandpa Owen's guitar case.

Down through the last fifty yards of second-growth pine behind us, the back screen door of our parents' house slammed hard enough to rattle the kitchen windowpane. My father's voice carried halfway up the dry needle slope—one final barked warning not to bother coming up the gravel drive until all three of us were ready to grow up, act like normal adults, and go our separate ways—and then the deadbolt clicked home and the afternoon woods swallowed the house whole.

Nobody cried. Nobody even looked back down the hill.

Sadie straightened up on the public-forest side of the wire, adjusted the padded strap of her canvas external-frame pack, and checked the strip of gray duct tape holding the bottom latch shut on the battered black hardshell guitar case. A stray curl of chestnut hair had worked loose over her temple above her navy-and-white striped shirt, catching the late June sun. Across from her, with the exact same gray-hazel eyes and sun-freckled nose and her hair tied half-back with a leather thong, Tessa hitched the waist belt of her tan work vest, tested the weight of the rolled blue-and-white dome tent lashed beneath her pack frame, and blew out a long, slow breath.

"Eighteen," Tessa said quietly.

"Eighteen," I answered, letting the barbed wire spring back into place between its rusted staples before stepping over it myself.

For as long as any of the three of us could remember, the crime in that house behind us had been the simplest thing in the world: we actually liked being together. When we were eight, our mother had demanded the elementary school put all three of us in separate classrooms so we would stop finishing each other's sentences at supper. When we were fourteen and spent an entire July rebuilding Grandpa Owen's cedar garden shed side by side instead of signing up for three different summer camps in three different counties, our father had lectured us for two hours at the dining table about unhealthy codependence and spreading our wings. By seventeen, every slamming cupboard door and raised voice through the drywall came back to the same ultimatum—normal brothers and sisters couldn't wait to get away from each other after graduation, and the minute we turned eighteen we were going onto separate tracks whether we liked it or not.

This morning, over cold toast on our eighteenth birthday, we had told them calmly that wherever we went next, the three of us were renting one roof and figuring it out together.

By four o'clock our packs were on the back porch steps, our birth certificates and Social Security cards were zipped inside Sadie's waterproof map pouch alongside four hundred and twelve dollars in mowed-lawn and stacked-cordwood cash, and my father was pointing down the driveway toward the county road.

Only we hadn't walked down the driveway toward the highway. Five acres behind the woodshed lay a million acres of Colville National Forest—ancient granite ridges, western larch, ponderosa pine, and nameless cold creeks climbing into the Kettle River Range with not a highway or neighbor in sight. We had walked these lower deer trails since we were ten years old whenever the shouting inside got too loud to think.

"How far in do you want to go?" Sadie asked, shifting Grandpa Owen's 1974 Clearwater guitar to her left hand so her right fingers brushed my flannel sleeve.

I looked upslope past the orange-barked ponderosas toward the high green notch where the old nineteen-sixties logging spur had washed out during the spring melt six years ago. Beyond that collapsed culvert, no pickup truck or four-wheeler could climb a hundred yards, and we had never once seen another bootprint in the silt.

"Past the washout," I said. "Deep enough that we can't hear a single engine off the county road. Let's just get camp up before dark and let our ears stop ringing."

Tessa reached out with her free hand and caught Sadie's wrist and mine together for one second—three wrists touching, leather cord against woven thread against flannel cuff—before turning uphill onto the deer trace.

"Lead on, brother," Tessa said.

## 7:45 PM — Beyond the Culvert

Five miles uphill with forty-pound canvas packs and a hardshell guitar case sorted out what mattered fast.

Around six o'clock we scrambled down and up the steep, fern-choked ravine where winter runoff had torn the old logging grade clean out to bare granite bedrock. Above the washout, the abandoned spur narrowed to twin ribbons of pinegrass and wild strawberry runners winding beneath towering western red cedars whose trunks smelled warm and spicy in the slant golden light. There were no mountain peaks shaped like cones anywhere in this corner of northeast Washington—only long, rounded granite shoulders furred green with Douglas fir and airy, bright-needled tamarack larches stretching fold after fold toward the Kettle Crest.

Half an hour before sundown, the sound of water brought us down off the bench into a hidden flat tucked inside an elbow of smooth gray granite boulders.

A stream fifteen feet wide came tumbling out of a narrow cedar gorge upstream, dropping over a knee-high ledge into a deep, slow-moving pool the color of dark bottle glass before chattering away over clean pale gravel. Twenty paces back from the water, well above the spring drift-line of bleached alder twigs, lay a dry carpet of russet pine needles sheltered between two massive ponderosas.

"Right here," Sadie breathed, setting the guitar case gently atop a flat boulder as if laying down a sleeping child. "Feel the air? Cold off the water, warm under the trees—zero mosquitoes."

We didn't have to assign jobs; eighteen years of working with six hands meant everybody already knew the rhythm. While Sadie unpacked the scarred enamel kettle and filled it where the stream poured clear and icy over clean granite lip, Tessa snapped the shock-corded fiberglass poles of our old two-person dome tent together and staked all four corners taut into the firm mineral soil beneath the pine duff. I dropped my pack, slipped Grandpa Owen's leather-sheathed hatchet and canvas tool roll out from under the top flap, and gathered dead, wrist-thick lower pine branches that snapped crisp as rifle shots—never damp wood off the ground—stacking them beside a ring of dry creek stones dug down to bare sand twenty feet downwind of the tent door.

One strike of the ferrocerium rod against the spine of my pocketknife threw a shower of white-hot sparks into a bird's nest of shredded dry cedar inner bark. A curl of blue smoke rose, caught, and licked up into clean orange flame.

By the time full twilight settled purple between the tree trunks, the kettle had come to a rolling boil for three minutes to make doubly sure of the mountain water, and Tessa was dealing out flour tortillas smeared thick with peanut butter and sprinkled with raisins from a zip-top bag while herbal peppermint tea cooled in three tin mugs.

We sat shoulder to shoulder on a fallen cedar bole facing the keyhole fire—Tessa on my right with her boots kicked off and her wool-socked feet warming near the warm stones, Sadie on my left with her mug cradled between both palms.

For three full minutes, nobody spoke a word.

Out in the dark beyond our tiny circle of amber light, the creek kept up a steady, low, braided rush over granite. High overhead, a light night breeze moved through a hundred-foot ceiling of pine needles with a sound like distant surf. Nobody slammed a bedroom door. Nobody shouted up a staircase asking why all three of us were sitting in the same room again. Nobody told Sadie to put Grandpa Owen's guitar away and go fill out housing forms five hundred miles from her brother and sister.

My shoulders dropped two full inches before I even realized I had been holding them up around my ears since breakfast.

"Listen to that," Tessa whispered against my right shoulder.

"Listen to what?" I asked softly.

"Nothing," she said, letting her head rest against my collarbone with a long, shaky exhale that turned into a quiet laugh. "Absolutely nothing. Nobody yelling at all."

Sadie set her tin mug down on a flat stone, unlatched the black guitar case, and lifted Grandpa Owen's 1974 Clearwater dreadnought onto her knee. The aged Sitka spruce soundboard glowed warm honey-gold in the firelight; up at the top of the neck, the plain dark rosewood headstock—stripped bare years ago when Grandpa Owen sanded and hand-oiled the neck himself—caught a tiny glint of orange on its six nickel tuning pegs.

She didn't strum loud. She just thumb-picked a slow, rolling pattern in open G, warm and mellow as an old nineteen-seventy-five record spinning in Grandpa's workshop while rain tapped the tin roof. After four bars, Tessa hummed a low alto third underneath the melody without needing words, and my chest vibrated as I found the baritone root note an octave down—three voices locking into place as effortlessly as breathing.

When the embers burned down to cherry-red coals under gray ash and the mountain air bit crisp through our flannels, we banked the fire ring safe with sand and crawled inside the blue-and-white dome tent together.

A tent meant for two campers took some choreography for three eighteen-year-olds, except we had slept piled together through thunderstorms since we were toddlers. We unzipped both old rectangular sleeping bags flat—one underneath us as a quilted mattress over our folded canvas packs, the second pulled wide across all three of us like a shared comforter. I stretched out on my back in the middle; Tessa curled onto her side tucked warm under my right arm with her forehead against my shoulder, and Sadie tucked right in under my left arm with her fingers resting across my ribs, both sisters breathing in slow, identical rhythm within two minutes.

Looking straight up through the mesh roof vent under the rainfly, I could see three sharp white stars framed between black ponderosa boughs. My legs ached from the climb, we had no roof thicker than nylon, and we owned four hundred and twelve dollars in the entire world.

It was, without a single close second, the happiest night of my life.
