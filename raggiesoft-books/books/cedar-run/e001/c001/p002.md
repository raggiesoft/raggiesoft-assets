## 8:45 AM — Waking in the Quiet

Sunlight woke us—not an alarm clock, not knuckles hammering on hollow-core bedroom doors, just warm gold filtering through green pine needles and painting bright dappled coins across the blue nylon tent wall.

For ten seconds I lay completely still in the middle of the unzipped sleeping bags, listening to the bright chatter of a red-breasted nuthatch somewhere up the trunk above us and the steady rush of cold water over granite. Tessa was still fast asleep with one arm slung across my chest and her chin tucked into the crook of my right shoulder; on my left, Sadie was already awake, propped slightly on one elbow watching a shaft of dusty morning sun move across the tent zipper with a look of quiet wonder on her face.

She caught me looking at her and smiled—barely a breath above a whisper so she wouldn't jolt her twin.

"Morning," Sadie murmured.

"Morning," I whispered back. "Sleep okay?"

"Nine straight hours," she whispered, resting her cheek back down against my left shoulder. "I didn't wake up once with my stomach in knots."

On my right, Tessa stirred, blinked her gray-hazel eyes against the bright canvas glow, and stretched both legs down to the foot of the tent until her toes bumped the nylon seam. She paused, listened to the forest for three heartbeats, and grinned without even lifting her head.

"Still nobody screaming," Tessa announced, her voice husky with sleep. "Happy eighteen-and-one-day, guys."

We crawled out into air that smelled of sun-warmed resin, damp moss, and crushed wild mint along the gravel bar. Down at the creek bend, thin ribbons of silver mist were rising off the deep emerald pool where sixty-degree morning sun met forty-five-degree Kettle Range snowmelt.

After yesterday's five-mile haul uphill in June heat and sleeping in our clothes, all three of us were sticky with pine pitch, trail dust, and sweat—and in twenty minutes of throwing things into backpacks yesterday afternoon, nobody had thought to pack a swimsuit. Even if we had, soaking our only pairs of denim jeans or cotton shirts in cold mountain water without a clothesline day ahead would leave us shivering by dusk.

None of us hesitated or made a production out of it. Having shared the same womb, the same nursery, and every cramped space of our lives, there had never been an ounce of awkwardness or self-consciousness between the three of us. Out on the wide, sun-baked slab of speckled gray granite overlooking the deep pool, we tugged off our boots and stacked our dry clothes in one neat, folded pile well clear of any splash—my jeans and faded olive flannel layered flat with Tessa's cream henley and Sadie's striped tee on top so the morning sun would warm the cotton while we washed.

"On three," Tessa dared from the smooth granite lip, toes curling over the edge above four feet of clear, dark-green water. "No chickening out halfway down the shin."

"One, two—go!" Sadie called, and all three of us jumped in together.

The shock of pure Kettle Crest snowmelt knocked the wind clean out of my lungs in a giant, echoing yelp as we surfaced in a burst of white spray, gasping and laughing hard enough to startle a kingfisher off an overhanging cedar bough. Beneath the dark-emerald mirror of the surface, clean gravel dropped to chest depth where the current swirled slow and gentle behind the boulder eddy. Tessa and Sadie immediately splashed two strokes inward from either side and latched onto me for body heat, laughing through chattering teeth as we huddled shoulder to shoulder with cold ripples lapping at our collarbones.

"Okay, that wakes you right up!" Tessa gasped, dunking the crown of her chestnut hair backward to rinse the trail dust clean before scrubbing pine pitch off her forearms with wet sand from the shallow shelf.

"Cleanest water in the state of Washington, though," Sadie laughed, slicking her wet hair back behind her ears and leaning warm against my left side while the sun slanted bright through the tall firs onto our faces.

Five minutes in that icy pool washed every last trace of our parents' house off our skin. We scrambled back up onto the sun-warmed granite ledge—skin tingling pink and electric in the warm mountain breeze—stretched out side by side on the hot flat rock wrapped in our one spare wool camp blanket, and huddled close together in a tight three-way tangle until the June sun dried our hair and warmed us straight to the bone before pulling our sun-toasted shirts and dry jeans back on.

## 10:30 AM — Naming the Creek

Over hot oatmeal simmered with a handful of sweet wild strawberries Tessa had spotted along the sunny edge of the clearing, Sadie spread her laminated Forest Service topographic quad flat across the cedar log and weighted its corners down with four smooth creek pebbles.

"Here's the washed-out culvert down on Spur 470-B," Sadie said, tracing a fingernail up the tight brown contour lines into a wide, unnumbered drainage crease tucked between two timbered granite spurs. "Look at the map. The main creek three miles west has a name, and the ridge north of us has an elevation marker, but this whole side fork doesn't even have a label."

I glanced upstream where two towering western red cedars leaned their feathery green boughs across the granite cascade, dropping reddish-gold bark ribbons into the clear foam.

"Cedar Run," I said.

Tessa paused with her tin spoon halfway to her mouth, looked up at the two giant cedars framing the falls, and nodded once, decisive. "Cedar Run. That's it. That's its name."

Sadie uncapped her pencil, wrote *Cedar Run* lightly in the margin of the map laminate, and then looked across the log at Tessa and me. Her voice turned quiet and steady—the voice she always used right before saying out loud what all three of us were already thinking.

"Dad thinks two nights on the hard ground with cold beans is going to break us," Sadie said. "He told Mom before we even reached the fence that we'd be back on the porch steps by Friday morning agreeing to split up."

Tessa's jaw set. She looked down at the braided brown leather cord around her wrist, then back up at the two of us. "I would rather sleep under spruce boughs in the rain until Christmas than walk back through that door and let them put three hundred miles between us."

I reached down beside my boot and unrolled Grandpa Owen's heavy waxed-canvas tool wrap across my knees. Inside their oiled pockets gleamed the tools Owen Sawyer had taught me to sharpen on Sunday afternoons when I was fifteen: a two-pound hickory camp hatchet, a curved two-handled drawknife, an L-shaped steel shingle froe for splitting cedar bolts along the grain, a twenty-four-inch Swedish bow-saw blade coiled neatly inside a strip of split garden hose, two wood chisels, a hand auger bit, a Dual-Grit whetstone, a fifty-foot hank of braided nylon cord, and an altoids-sized tin of trout hooks, lead split-shot, and monofilament line.

"Look around us," I said quietly, resting my palm over the cool steel of Grandpa Owen's froe. "We aren't going back down that hill, and we don't need a town right now either. Nobody knows we're past the washout. We have clean spring water, deadfall red cedar that splits straight as lumber, wild berries coming ripe, and trout rising in every pool down this creek. If we use our heads and start gathering food and building a real shelter right now while June is warm, we can make a camp out here that holds all three of us."

Tessa's eyes lit up with that fierce, kinetic spark she always got the second a problem turned into something she could build with her hands.

"Not just a nylon tent," Tessa said, leaning forward with both elbows on her knees. "Something off the ground. Something dry when the mountain storms roll through—something we can keep adding onto week by week."

"First rule Grandpa Owen always taught," Sadie agreed, tapping the contour lines around our nameless valley. "Before you drive the first peg, walk the whole drainage. Let's see what food is growing within a mile of this water and find the single best piece of ground in the valley to build on."
