## 9:15 AM — Four Days of Pantry Oats

On our second full morning in the woods, Tessa spread a clean cotton bandana flat on the granite hearth-slab and poured out every dry ration left in our three packs so nobody could kid themselves about the math.

Two quart bags of rolled oats. A pound and a half of pinto beans. Three cups of long-grain white rice, half a jar of peanut butter, a small tin of salt, a handful of raisins, and four packets of peppermint tea. Stretched careful, it was four days of full meals—maybe six if we went hungry by noon.

"Good," Tessa said briskly, tying the oat bags shut again before a camp jay perched in the low fir bough could get bold. "Nothing clarifies a morning schedule like seeing the bottom of the oat bag. Every calorie we bring in from the woods today stretches that salt and rice into July."

We left the dome tent staked tight with our sleeping bags aired dry inside, hoisted our food sack twenty feet out on a high tamarack limb well clear of black bears and pine martens, and took one light daypack, the bow saw I had framed that morning out of seasoned hickory saplings, Grandpa Owen's hatchet, and the tin of trout hooks.

Our mission for the day was simple: scout the entire Cedar Run basin—bottomland, sunny south slope, and upper springhead—before driving a single permanent stake.

We didn't have to walk a hundred yards from our Night One fire ring to examine our first contender.

Just above our gravel bar, where Cedar Run slowed through three linked pools of deep green water, a monarch western red cedar nearly five feet thick at the butt had blown down in some winter gale ten or fifteen years back. Its roots had tipped up clean out of the slope, leaving eighty straight, knot-free feet of rot-proof red heartwood resting dry off the damp soil along a level pine-needle terrace twenty feet above the high-water mark. Down in the emerald pool below the root wad, three spotted cutthroat trout—ten to twelve inches long, bellies flashing pale gold—hung steady in the current sipping caddisflies off the surface film.

I tapped the side of the fallen cedar trunk with the poll of Grandpa Owen's hatchet. Beneath an inch of gray weathered sapwood, the heartwood rang solid as a church bell and smelled sweet and peppery.

"Do you realize how many roof shakes and wall planks are sitting inside this one tree?" I said, running my palm along the laser-straight grain. "We wouldn't have to fell a single green tree. With the froe and a hardwood maul, we could split raised floorboards, frame a whole three-sided lean-to right against the trunk as a windbreak, and check willow fish traps in those three pools morning and night."

"And look along the gravel bar," Tessa added, crouching beside a dense thicket of flexible red-osier dogwood shoots and broad-leafed thimbleberry canes loaded with white blossoms turning to green fruit. "Straight basket shoots for fish traps right at the water's edge, wild mint, stinging nettle tops we can boil like spinach tonight, and cold water twenty steps from the hearth."

"Wait till we check the higher ground during rain runoff, though," Sadie pointed out, squinting upslope where bright sunlight blazed across an open bench half a mile north. "Creek bottoms stay ten degrees colder once September frost sinks into the canyon. Let's climb the south face."

## 1:20 PM — The Granite Overhang and the High Springhouse

Climbing out of the cool cedar shade onto the south-facing slope felt like stepping into another season altogether.

Here the dark timber gave way to five rolling acres of open mountain meadow warm with late-June sunshine and buzzing with wild bumblebees. Knee-high mountain huckleberry bushes carpeted the slope in thick green waves, their undersides already heavy with thousands of swelling berries blush-pink and turning dusty blue in the sunniest pockets, mixed with wild onions whose green tops smelled sharp and savory the instant Tessa bruised one between her thumb and forefinger.

At the top of that sunny meadow rose a forty-foot rib of pale gray Kettle Range granite—and carved into its base by ancient ice sat a natural rock overhang twelve feet deep, twenty-five feet wide, and taller than my head.

I stepped under the stone lip out of the hot noon sun and dropped to one knee to feel the floor. Bone-dry granite dust and papery pine needles that hadn't seen a single drop of rain in a hundred winters. Better still, the solid rock ceiling sloped upward toward a natural vertical cleft at the eastern corner like a ready-made chimney flue, and the massive cliff face soaked up direct southern sunshine from nine in the morning until seven at night.

"Feel the rock wall," Tessa said, pressing both palms flat against the back of the overhang. "It's like laying your hands on a warm bread oven. If we dry-stacked flat granite slabs and peeled lodgepole poles across just half of this front opening and chinked the gaps with creek clay and moss, three of our four walls are solid mountain bedrock—and all those huckleberries and wild onions are right outside our front door."

"Water's a quarter-mile downhill to the creek," I noted, measuring the haul in my head, "unless that dampfern seam at the east end of the meadow seeps into a catch-basin."

"Let's check the upper springhead on the map before we call it," Sadie said, pointing up the narrow timbered draw to the northeast where a ribbon of bright green tamaracks climbed toward a high saddle.

An hour's steady climb along a mossy trickling run brought us out onto a level, wind-sheltered saddle framed by tall golden-barked Douglas firs and feathery larches—and stopped all three of us dead in our tracks in the middle of a patch of wild red raspberries.

Half-buried beneath sixty years of emerald feather-moss and creeping twinflower stood four waist-to-shoulder-high walls of hand-fitted gray fieldstone, twelve feet by fourteen, laid straight and plumb by some forgotten nineteen-thirties forest crew long before our parents were even born. The old cedar roof poles had rotted away decades ago, letting sunshine pour straight into the stone enclosure, save for one low corner arch where crystal-clear forty-two-degree spring water bubbled straight up out of clean gravel into a hand-chiseled stone trough before spilling merrily down the draw toward Cedar Run.

"No way," Tessa whispered, stepping through the old stone doorway and dipping two fingers into the icy trough. "A stone springhouse! Look at the walls—there isn't a loose rock in the whole foundation, and we've got cold running water and a natural refrigerator right inside the stonework!"

Sadie walked the outside perimeter, parting lush fronds of lady-fern where golden chanterelle buttons were already pushing through damp fir duff after last week's rain, while two plump dusky grouse burst clucking out of the serviceberry brush thirty yards up the game trail.

"Nobody has walked up this draw in forty years," Sadie marveled, resting her hand on the warm, moss-velveted corner stone. "New Douglas fir ridgepole, peeled rafters, overlapping red-cedar bark shingles overhead—we'd have four stone walls and clean water under one roof."

## 6:40 PM — Three Visions Around the Keyhole Fire

Back down at our dome tent as golden evening slanted low through the ponderosas, we boiled a pot of tender young nettle greens with a half-cup of rice and a pinch of salt—our very first half-foraged supper—and sat close together on our cedar log while Grandpa Owen's guitar rested across Sadie's lap.

All three places we had found today stayed 100% inside the deep forest, miles from any road or town, and any one of them could keep the three of us fed and dry if we poured our backs into it starting at dawn tomorrow. Yet each foothold meant committing to a completely different way of living and building.

Down here on the Creek-Bend Cedar Bench, we had that eighty-foot fallen red-cedar giant waiting to split into straight boards and roof shakes alongside endless cutthroat trout in the deep pools right below our boots. Up on the South-Facing Granite Overhang, three indestructible walls of sun-warmed bedrock stood bone-dry above five acres of ripening huckleberries, wild onions, and meadow roots. And high on the saddle at the Forgotten Stone Springhouse, four nineteen-thirties fieldstone walls and a cold bubbling spring trough waited for new rafters beside rich mushroom duff and grouse trails.

Tessa leaned against my right shoulder; Sadie rested her knee against my left, fingers quiet on the guitar strings, both of my sisters looking at me in the warm amber glow as the creek murmured past the stones.

"Tomorrow morning we start building for real, and we start laying up food," Sadie said softly. "Where do we stake our claim, Luke?"
