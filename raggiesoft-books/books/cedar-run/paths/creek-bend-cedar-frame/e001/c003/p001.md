## 7:30 AM — The Sound of Straight Heartwood

The steel blade of Grandpa Owen's shingle froe bit into the end-grain of the thirty-inch western red cedar round, and when I struck it twice with the heavy tamarack maul I had carved before breakfast, the bolt popped open down its center with a clean, sweet *crack* that smelled like a cedar chest opened after fifty years.

"Look at that split!" Sadie called from six paces away, setting down two flat gray granite hearth slabs to run her fingertips along the freshly riven plank. Not a twist, not a knot—thirty inches long, eight inches wide, and flat enough to lay a carpenter's level across.

"Grandpa Owen wasn't exaggerating about old-growth windfall," I grinned, wiping sweat off my forehead with the back of my flannel sleeve. "That one fallen trunk is an entire lumberyard sitting twenty steps above the trout pools. It's been seasoning off the ground for ten summers and there isn't a spot of rot an inch inside the bark."

Choosing the creek-bend bench meant our mornings began to the music of rushing water and clean wood splitting in the cool canyon shade. Before we did anything else, we worked together with the bow saw to buck four stout cedar rounds off the narrower crown end of the windfall giant and rolled them into the four corners of a ten-by-eight-foot rectangle right alongside the five-foot main trunk—using the massive fallen tree itself as our solid, draft-proof north back wall.

While I notched stout peeled Douglas fir stringers across those four corner blocks to raise our sleeping deck eighteen inches clear of damp ground and evening cold air, Tessa knelt down on the sunlit gravel bar with an armful of whip-straight red-osier dogwood and willow shoots she had harvested at dawn.

Watching Tessa's hands work was like watching someone weave music out of green switches. Using thinner peeled cedar-root lashings so we wouldn't waste a single foot of our nylon cord, she wove an elongated, funnel-throated basket trap three feet long—wide cone at the upstream mouth tapering to a narrow inward-pointing throat inside a tied tail-cap—the exact passive creek weir we had once seen sketched in an old pioneer woodcraft book on Grandpa Owen's shelf.

## 1:45 PM — Silver-Gold in the Willow Trap

By early afternoon, twelve wide split-cedar planks lay lashed snug and level across our raised fir stringers—a solid, sweet-smelling wooden floor wide enough for all three of us to stretch out side by side without a single rock or root poking through—and six peeled fir rafters sloped upward from the giant cedar trunk to a sturdy front ridgepole supported on forked uprights, waiting for our overlapping cedar roof shakes.

Down at the middle emerald pool, Tessa let out a low, triumphant whistle.

Sadie and I scrambled down the granite bank right as Tessa waded thigh-deep in her rolled-up jeans to lift the willow funnel trap out of the narrow stone chute between two boulders, water cascading silver through the woven red-osier ribs. Inside the basket flashed three fat, cold-firm cutthroat trout—ten to twelve inches apiece, olive backs speckled dark and crimson slashes bright beneath their jaws—fighting the wet willow weave.

"Supper just swam into the dining room!" Tessa beamed, cradling the basket ashore onto the dry gravel bar.

She kept the two largest twelve-inchers—dispatching them swiftly and cleanly before gutting them on a flat wet stone at the tail of the riffle so the cold current carried every trace downstream away from camp—and slipped the smaller ten-incher gently back into the pool to grow for August. Up on the sunny edge of the clearing, Sadie had filled our enamel kettle half-full of tiny, intensely sweet wild mountain strawberries and tender fireweed shoots.

That evening, as blue canyon shadow climbed the eastern pines while high golden sun still gilded the treetops overhead, we christened our new raised cedar deck and our U-shaped river-stone reflector hearth.

Tessa skewered the two split cutthroat fillets flat on green peeled alder sticks leaned over a bed of glowing pine-and-alder coals until the skin blistered crisp and golden and fat sizzled into the embers. A pinch of our precious salt turned fresh snowmelt trout, boiled fireweed greens, and a shared cup of wild strawberries into the richest feast any of the three of us had ever tasted—and we hadn't touched a single grain of our dry oats or pinto beans all day.

After we scrubbed our fingers with clean creek sand and banked fresh wood against the stone reflector wall so warmth radiated straight back onto our raised cedar platform, Sadie leaned back against the giant fallen trunk between Tessa and me and lifted Grandpa Owen's guitar.

With a solid cedar floor beneath us, two dozen freshly split roof shakes stacked ready to overlap across the rafters tomorrow morning, and the three deep pools murmuring twenty feet below our toes, Sadie thumb-picked a bright, rippling chord progression that sounded exactly like sunlight dancing over creek stones—and when Tessa and I joined our voices to hers on the chorus, the giant red cedar behind our backs hummed right along with the strings.
