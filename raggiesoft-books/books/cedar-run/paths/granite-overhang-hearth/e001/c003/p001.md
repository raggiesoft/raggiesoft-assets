## 7:45 AM — Moving Upslope Into the Sun

By eight in the morning, while cool blue shadow still lingered down in the narrow creek bottom, our south-facing granite overhang half a mile upslope was already bathed in warm gold sunlight.

We struck the blue-and-white dome tent in ten minutes, packed every pot, sleeping bag, and tool up the deer switchbacks in a single trip, and set Grandpa Owen's black guitar case deep in the dry, sheltered curve at the back of the stone alcove where rain hadn't touched the dust since the glaciers melted.

Standing inside that twelve-foot-deep pocket of solid Kettle Range bedrock felt like discovering the earth itself had already done three-quarters of our heavy lifting. Overhead, a thick shelf of pale gray granite projected straight out like a fortress porch roof; behind us and along both curving sides, seamless living rock blocked every gust of wind off the northern ridge; and at the eastern corner, a natural V-shaped vertical fissure rose fifteen feet to the cliff top like a stonemason's chimney flue.

Best of all, Sadie had been right about the bright green fern seep sixty yards east across the meadow edge. When Tessa dug two feet down into the cold gravel at the base of a mossy granite outcrop with a flat digging stick and lined the basin with clean cobbles, icy groundwater cleared within twenty minutes into a brimming, crystal-pure dipping spring right on our own contour line.

"Water sixty steps east, five acres of berries out the front door, and a roof ten thousand tons heavy," Tessa grinned, wiping damp granite grit off her wrists onto her jeans. "Let's give this place a front wall."

All morning and well past noon, the three of us worked an assembly line across the talus scatter at the base of the cliff. Centuries of frost-wedging had left hundreds of flat, brick-like granite slabs lying among the pinegrass. Sadie and Tessa carried stones two-handed on a sturdy canvas sling while I fitted them dry-stack style—wide base courses two feet thick, overlapping every vertical seam two-on-one and wedging small shim stones tight until not a single block rocked under my boot heel—raising a chest-high stone wind-wall across two-thirds of the overhang's open mouth while leaving a sheltered south doorway facing the sunny meadow.

Above the stone half-wall, we wedged dead-standing peeled lodgepole pine poles upright into natural ceiling crevices and packed the chinks between the stones with stiff gray clay dug from the spring seep kneaded thick with dry pine needles and moss.

## 4:30 PM — Purple Fingers and Stored Heat

When our forearms finally shook too hard to lift one more forty-pound slab of granite, we grabbed our clean kettle, two empty zip-top bags, and Sadie's spare bandana and waded straight out into the sunlit ocean of mountain huckleberry brush below our doorway.

In the hottest south-facing pockets along the warm boulders, the early bushes were already loaded with plump, dark-purple berries the size of marbles. For two solid hours the three of us moved side by side down the rows, fingers staining deep violet-blue, eating one berry for every four that plinked into the kettle while Tessa also pried up two dozen crisp white wild-onion bulbs from the friable meadow loam with a fire-hardened digging stick.

On a wide, table-flat slab of sun-hot granite just outside our stone doorway, Sadie spread half of our berry harvest in a single thin layer to begin sun-drying into chewy winter trail fruit—covering them lightly with cheesecloth-thin pine twigs to keep the camp jays honest—while we carried the rest inside to our newly laid corner stone hearth beneath the natural rock chimney cleft.

When I touched a spark to dry pine kindling under that eastern cleft, we held our breath for five seconds to test the draft.

The curl of pale woodsmoke didn't roll back into our eyes; warmed by the sun-heated cliff face, it pulled straight up the vertical granite fissure like a chimney pipe and vanished cleanly into the open sky twenty feet overhead.

We simmered a thick, savory pot of wild-onion rice alongside warm stewed huckleberries, then spread a deep, fragrant mattress of dry western larch boughs and fern fronds beneath our sleeping bags against the curved rear rock wall.

An hour after sundown, when the open meadow air dropped twenty degrees into crisp mountain night, the magic of that south-facing cliff revealed itself. Every square foot of granite behind our backs and above our heads had soaked up ten hours of blazing June sunshine—and now it breathed gentle, steady radiant warmth back into our enclosed alcove like a tiled masonry stove.

Curled up side by side in our shirt-sleeves with Tessa tucked warm against one side of me and Sadie fingerpicking a slow, glowing nineteen-seventy-five meadow ballad on the other, watching tiny orange embers drift up the stone cleft while our rock walls held us warm, we knew we had found a fortress.
