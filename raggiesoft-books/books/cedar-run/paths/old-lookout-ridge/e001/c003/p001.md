## 8:00 AM — Reclaiming the Four Walls

Whoever had laid the four fieldstone walls on the high tamarack saddle back in the nineteen-thirties had built as if they expected the mountain itself to lean on their work.

Once Tessa, Sadie, and I cleared out the wrist-thick alder saplings, rotted old roof humus, and sixty years of fallen cones from the twelve-by-fourteen-foot interior, we hit a floor of flat, fitted gray slate flags swept clean along the northern wall by the forty-two-degree spring bubbling endlessly through its chiseled stone trough. Not a single corner course had bowed or slumped; the walls stood a solid five feet high on the side slopes and rose to seven-foot stone gables at either end, complete with a square stone lintel over the narrow south doorway.

"Somebody loved this spot once," Sadie said quietly, brushing emerald moss gently off a hand-dressed doorway block without disturbing the tiny white twinflowers rooted in the outer crevices. "Look how dry the upper nine-tenths of the flagstone floor stays—the spring trough has its own stone lip and outlet channel straight under the east wall."

"Which means drinking water, wash water, and an icebox that never warms above forty-two degrees without taking one step outside in a storm," Tessa said, testing the gable notch at the peak of the west wall with both hands. "Luke, if we drop one straight sixteen-foot Douglas fir ridgepole right into these original gable notches, we can have rafters up before supper."

High on the breezy saddle, fallen dead-standing timber cured hard and bone-dry instead of rotting into sponge. Halfway across the larch bench we found three arrow-straight, wind-snapped Douglas fir poles seasoned silver-gray and light enough for the three of us to shoulder together—Luke at the heavy butt end, Tessa and Sadie paired at the tip—marching in step through the wild raspberry canes like a three-person barn-raising crew.

Heaving that sixteen-foot ridgepole up onto the seven-foot stone gables took every ounce of leverage we had, walking it up forked push-poles inch by inch on Sadie's steady three-count until the timber dropped home into both stone pockets with a deep, satisfying *thud* that shook dust off the moss.

All afternoon, while I notched twelve peeled tamarack rafters to straddle the ridgepole down to the side walls and lashed them fast with green spruce-root withes, Tessa and Sadie peeled four-foot sheets of thick, fibrous bark off two long-dead fallen cedars down in the draw—overlapping them shingle-fashion from eaves to ridge and weighting them down with heavy pole binders so even a mountain downburst would shed clean past the stonework.

## 5:50 PM — Golden Chanterelles and the High Saddle View

With half of our roof already shingled tight over the dry western sleeping bay—and our blue-and-white tent fly stretched taut across the remaining half until tomorrow's bark haul—Tessa and Sadie disappeared into the damp, shadowy Douglas fir duff along the north rim for forty-five minutes with our clean bandana and Grandpa Owen's pocketknife.

They came back grinning ear to ear with nearly three pounds of bright, apricot-gold Pacific golden chanterelles—trumpet-shaped ridges running cleanly down firm white stems, smelling faintly of ripe apricots and damp earth—along with a quart of tart red wild raspberries and two handfuls of lemony wood sorrel leaves.

"Look at the cold trough work," Sadie said proudly, submerging our sealed jar of peanut butter and a covered tin of extra chanterelles two inches deep in the crystal stone spring channel along the north wall, where cold water purled steadily around the metal like a mountain dairy house.

Outside the south doorway on a natural stone terrace that looked out across twenty miles of rolling green Kettle River ridges—not a road, rooftop, or power line in sight, only wave after wave of pine and larch turning copper in the low sun—we built a compact stone cooking hearth and sautéed sliced golden chanterelles in a spoonful of peanut-butter oil with wood sorrel and a cup of boiled rice.

Nothing in any restaurant on earth could have touched hot golden mushrooms and cold spring water on that ridge at eight o'clock on a June evening.

When twilight cooled the high saddle, we carried a flat stone pan of glowing hardwood coals inside to warm the slate flags, pulled a rough-woven cedar-bough curtain across the low doorway, and stretched our sleeping bags side by side under the fresh-peeled tamarack rafters in the dry bay. Listening to the quiet, musical trickle of our indoor stone spring in the dark while Sadie hummed a soft harmony line between Tessa and me, it felt less like camping and more like coming home to a place that had been waiting sixty years for the three of us to put its roof back on.
