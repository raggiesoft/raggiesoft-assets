The hospital hallway was too bright, and it smelled like sharp cleaning spray and cold plastic. 

Maggie held tightly onto Mrs. Bea's hand, her small sneakers squeaking against the shiny white floor. She was wearing her favorite yellow sundress, the one with the little daisies along the hem. Her fever was completely gone, but her stomach felt fluttery and nervous. 

She hadn't seen Drew since Thursday evening. The last time she saw her twin brother, he was wearing his brand-new, stiff white karate uniform, looking so proud as Mom took a picture of him on the front steps. But when Mom finally came home on Friday to get some clothes, she was crying. She told Maggie that a bad man had hurt Drew very badly, and that Drew had to stay in the hospital for a long time. 

"Okay, Maggie, my sweet girl," Mrs. Bea said softly, stopping outside a large glass door with a big number '4' painted on it. Mrs. Bea kneeled down so she was right at Maggie's eye level. "We're going to go inside and see your brother now. But we have to remember the rules we talked about in the car. Do you remember?"

Maggie nodded her head solemnly. "I have to be super quiet. And I can't touch the wires."

"That's right," Mrs. Bea smiled sadly, brushing a stray lock of brown hair behind Maggie's ear. "Drew has a lot of boo-boos on his tummy and his back. It hurts him a lot right now. So we can't jump on the bed, and we can't give him a big squeeze hug like we normally do. We have to be very, very gentle."

"I can be gentle," Maggie promised.

Mrs. Bea squeezed her hand, stood up, and pushed the heavy glass door open. 

Maggie stepped into the room. It was filled with loud beeping noises and a strange, steady rushing sound. In the very middle of the room was a giant metal bed, surrounded by tall poles with bags of clear water hanging from them. 

And in the middle of the giant bed was Drew. 

Maggie's breath hitched in her throat. He looked so small. He wasn't wearing his white uniform anymore; he was wearing a loose hospital gown covered in tiny blue bears. There were thin, clear tubes taped to his nose, and wires with sticky pads attached to his chest. His arms rested on top of the blankets, and there was a plastic tube sticking right into his hand with medical tape holding it down. 

His face was very pale, and his brown hair was messy against the stark white pillow. 

Mom was sitting in a chair right next to the bed. When she saw Maggie, she stood up quickly, wiping her eyes, and walked over to give Maggie a gentle, careful hug. 

"Hi, sweetie," Mom whispered, kissing the top of her head. "He's awake. He's been waiting for you."

Maggie looked past Mom. Drew’s head slowly turned on the pillow, and his eyes found hers. Behind his small glasses, his eyes looked tired and sad, but a tiny, wobbly smile appeared on his lips. 

"Hi, Mags," Drew's voice was very quiet and scratchy. 

Maggie’s immediate instinct, wired into her since the day they were born, was to run and dive onto the mattress, throwing her arms around him to protect him from the scary room and the loud machines. She took a quick, eager step forward—

"Ah, gentle, Maggie," Mom warned softly, reaching out to catch her shoulder. 

Maggie stopped instantly. She remembered the rules. She remembered Mrs. Bea saying he had bad boo-boos on his tummy. 

Instead of running, Maggie walked very slowly and carefully to the side of the bed. The metal rails were pulled up high, like the sides of a crib, but there was enough space between the bars for her to reach through. 

She stood on her tiptoes, reaching her hand through the metal rails. 

Drew shifted his hand across the blanket, his fingers moving weakly until they met hers. Maggie laced her small fingers through his, holding his hand as gently as she could, just like she would hold a fragile butterfly. His hand felt cold, but his grip tightened around her fingers. 

The physical connection—the simple, unbroken bond between twins—finally settled the nervous fluttering in Maggie’s stomach. She was here. She was with him. 

"Does it hurt?" Maggie asked softly, staring at the tube taped to his hand. 

"Yeah," Drew whispered. "My tummy hurts. And my legs feel weird."

"Mom said a bad man did it," Maggie said, her six-year-old mind struggling to understand why an adult would ever want to hurt her brother. Her lower lip trembled, and a sudden, fierce rush of anger bubbled up in her chest. "He's a mean bully."

"The police took him away," Drew said, his eyes dropping to their joined hands. "But Mom says I can't walk anymore, Mags. My legs don't work right."

Maggie stared at him. Her brain tried to process what that meant. They rode bikes together. They ran around the courtyard catching fireflies. If his legs didn't work, he couldn't do those things. He couldn't run away from mean people. 

She looked at her twin brother, looking so fragile and broken in the giant hospital bed. She didn't know how to fix his legs, and she couldn't take the boo-boos away. But as she squeezed his fingers, a profound, unbreakable determination settled into her heart. 

"That's okay," Maggie said firmly, her voice entirely devoid of hesitation. "I'll help you."

Drew looked up at her, his eyes shining with unshed tears. "You will?"

"Uh-huh," Maggie promised, nodding her head with absolute six-year-old certainty. "When we go to school, I'll carry your backpack for you. I'll carry both of them. And if you have a wheelchair, I'll push it really fast so we can still race the other kids."

A small, genuine smile broke through the sadness on Drew’s face. "Like a racecar?"

"Like a super-fast racecar," Maggie agreed. She leaned closer to the metal bars, lowering her voice like they were sharing a top-secret plan. "I'm not going to let you be by yourself. I'm going to sit next to you in every class. If a bully tries to be mean to you, I'll yell at them really loud. I'm going to take care of you, Drew. Forever."

"Okay," Drew whispered, his grip on her hand solidifying, anchoring himself to her promise. "Okay, Mags."

Mom and Mrs. Bea stood quietly in the corner of the room, both of them crying silently as they watched the two six-year-olds holding hands through the bed rails. The world had irrevocably shattered their innocence, but in the center of the sterile, terrifying hospital room, the bond between the twins had just forged itself into steel.
