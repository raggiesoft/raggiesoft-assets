Thanksgiving Day on the pediatric spinal cord injury ward possessed a strange, hollow quiet. The usual bustle of physical therapists and morning rounds was scaled back to a skeleton crew. For the children stuck in their beds or their newly assigned wheelchairs, the holiday felt less like a celebration and more like a test of endurance.

Around lunchtime, the quiet was broken by the joyful, echoing sounds of a family packing up. 

Across the hall, a sixteen-year-old girl named Chloe—who had been recovering from a severe car accident for the last three months—was finally being discharged. Drew watched from his doorway, seated in his small, motorized wheelchair. He saw Chloe’s father carrying a stack of board games and a deflating "Get Well Soon" balloon. He saw the orderly bring in the transport wheelchair, and he heard the bubbly, tearful laughter as Chloe hugged Nurse Brenda goodbye.

"Happy Thanksgiving, everyone!" Chloe’s mother called out to the nurses' station, her voice thick with happy tears. "We’re finally going home!"

Drew sat perfectly still as the elevator doors closed behind the family. The silence rushed back into the hallway, heavier than before. 

Then, the tears came.

It wasn't a tantrum. It was a profound, suffocating wave of grief. Drew buried his face in his small hands, his narrow shoulders shaking with violent, silent sobs. 

Claire, who had been organizing his closet, rushed out to the hallway and fell to her knees beside his wheelchair. "Oh, buddy," she whispered, wrapping her arms around his small frame. "I know. I know."

"Why did she get to go home?" Drew gasped, his face red and slick with tears. He gripped his mother’s shirt, burying his face in her shoulder. "It’s Thanksgiving! I’m supposed to go home too! I want to go home, Mom! I want to go *home*!"

He cried harder than he had in weeks. The reality of his permanent residency on the ward, stretching out into an unforeseeable future, felt unbearable. Maggie, who had been coloring on Drew's bed, dropped her markers and ran out. She scrambled to wrap her arms around her brother from behind. She didn't say anything; she just pressed her cheek against his back and let her own tears fall, hating the hospital for keeping him captive.

The grief hung over Drew’s room for hours, an oppressive cloud that stubbornly refused to lift. 

By late afternoon, however, the familiar, booming voice of Mrs. Bea echoed down the corridor. 

"Now, Brenda, I did exactly what you said," Mrs. Bea was saying as she pushed a hospital utility cart loaded with insulated bags. "Everything is double-sealed, cooked to one-hundred and sixty-five degrees, and I even brought the receipt for the commercial-grade Tupperware."

Nurse Brenda walked alongside her, smiling warmly but maintaining her professional rigor. The hospital’s infection control protocol for bringing outside food onto a surgical recovery ward was incredibly strict. Brenda systematically pulled each thick, heavy-duty plastic container from the bags, ensuring the airtight seals hadn't been compromised during transit. She took a container of antibacterial hospital wipes and meticulously wiped down the exterior of every single dish before placing them on a sanitized steel tray. 

"You did perfectly, Bea," Brenda said softly. "The dayroom is all yours. I wiped down the big table an hour ago. Let's just review Drew's chart real quick... alright, he's cleared for regular solids, so we are good to go."

When Claire wheeled Drew into the family dayroom, the harsh fluorescent lights and the lingering smell of clinical antiseptic couldn't completely mask the warm aroma that escaped when Mrs. Bea finally cracked open the seals.

The table was set with sterile paper plates and individually wrapped plastic cutlery, but the food was a small miracle. There was sliced turkey breast, heavily steamed green beans, and right in the center, Mrs. Bea’s famous sweet potato casserole. 

"Happy Thanksgiving, my sweet boy," Mrs. Bea said, her voice gentle as she bent down to kiss the top of Drew's head. 

Drew sniffled, wiping his eyes beneath his glasses. He looked at the sterile room, the paper plates, and the plastic forks. It wasn't their dining room table. It wasn't the house. But as Maggie pulled her chair as close to his wheelchair as physically possible, slipping her hand into his under the table, he realized that he wasn't alone. 

He picked up his plastic fork, took a tiny bite of the sweet potatoes, and leaned his head against his sister's shoulder.
