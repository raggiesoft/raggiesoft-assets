The dayroom at the end of the pediatric rehabilitation hallway was normally a place for kids to watch cartoons or play board games, but every morning at nine o'clock, the television was turned off, and it officially became a classroom. 

Drew sat at one of the low plastic tables, the brakes on his red wheelchair securely locked. In front of him was a thick manila envelope that Mom brought in every Monday morning. It was filled with reading packets, spelling lists, and math worksheets straight from his first-grade teacher back at Bayside Elementary in Virginia Beach. 

Across the table from him, the teenager with the massive cast on his leg was hunched over a heavy high school history textbook. At the next table over, the little girl with no hair was using a set of wooden blocks to practice counting. 

"Alright, Drew, let's take a look at your spelling words for this week," Mr. Davis said, pulling up a small blue plastic chair next to the wheelchair. 

Mr. Davis wasn't a doctor or a therapist. He didn't wear scrubs, and he never poked anyone with needles. He wore a quiet cardigan and a tie. Because the Children's Hospital was located in Norfolk, the Virginia Beach school district paid the Norfolk school district to send Mr. Davis to the ward every single weekday. His entire job was to make sure that the kids who were trapped inside the hospital didn't get left behind by the world outside. 

Drew pulled the lined paper out of the Bayside Elementary envelope. He picked up his pencil, gripping it tightly. 

"I have to learn all ten of them by Friday," Drew stated seriously, looking up at the teacher. 

"Ten words is a lot," Mr. Davis noted gently. "If you're feeling tired from physical therapy this morning, we can always ask your school to reduce the list. Under your hospital instruction plan, you don't have to do the exact same amount of work as the kids in the regular classroom."

"No," Drew said instantly, shaking his head. His grip on the pencil tightened. "I have to do the same amount."

"Are you sure?" 

"Maggie is doing these exact same ten words," Drew explained, the fierce, unwavering logic of a six-year-old twin driving his decision. "If I don't do them, I won't be as smart as her. And if I don't finish first grade with her, they won't let me be in her class next year."

Drew looked down at the blank lines on the paper. The idea of Maggie walking into a second-grade classroom without him made his chest feel tight. He remembered how hard she cried when the doctors first explained how badly he was hurt. If he failed first grade and got left behind, she would cry all over again. He had promised to let her take care of him, and he wasn't going to let a hospital stay break their timeline. 

Mr. Davis smiled, recognizing the sheer determination in the little boy's eyes. The physical therapists worked on Drew's paralyzed legs, but his academic drive was entirely fueled by his sister. 

"Okay, then," Mr. Davis said, tapping the top of the spelling list. "Let's get to work. First word: *Together*."

Drew carefully touched his pencil to the paper and started tracing out the letters. It was hard to focus sometimes. His lower back ached constantly from sitting in the chair, and the heavy medication he took for the nerve pain sometimes made his brain feel fuzzy. But he pushed through it, carefully sounding out the vowels just like Mr. Davis taught him. 

The dayroom was quiet, filled only with the scratch of pencils, the soft turning of textbook pages, and the low murmur of Mr. Davis moving from table to table to check on the different patients. 

It wasn't a normal first-grade classroom. There was no recess bell, no messy cafeteria lunches, and no running around on the playground. But as Drew finished spelling out his final word and carefully slid the paper back into his manila envelope, he felt a profound sense of accomplishment. 

He was holding the line. His body was broken, and his life was completely confined to the pediatric ward, but his mind was keeping perfect pace with his twin sister across the city. He was going to be ready for second grade, no matter what it took.
