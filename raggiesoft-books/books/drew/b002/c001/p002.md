They had always just called her Mrs. Bea. It was a title of politeness that had stuck since before the twins could even walk. Her actual name was Beatrice Armstrong. She had immigrated from Newcastle upon Tyne back in the late 1930s, and even after sixty years in America, her voice still carried the warm, thick musical lilt of northern England. 

But to Drew and Maggie, she was simply Mrs. Bea—the ultimate protector, the woman who had fostered Maggie during the darkest months of the hospital void, and the woman who was now taking them in when they had absolutely nowhere else to go.

Mrs. Bea’s house was a modest, single-story ranch slightly closer to the oceanfront. To Drew's immediate relief, a sturdy wooden wheelchair ramp had already been installed over the front steps. Mrs. Bea had paid a local handyman to put it in years ago for her late husband. 

"Come inside, come inside. Out of the heat," Mrs. Bea urged, holding the front door wide open. 

Drew pushed the joystick forward, his chair humming as he rolled up the ramp and over the threshold. For the first time in his life, he didn't have to wait for his mother to drag his 150-pound chair up the stairs. 

"I've got the guest room all cleared out," Mrs. Bea said, leading the way down the hall. "And Claire, I had Jim check the bathroom this morning. The grab bars in the tub are solid. They'll hold."

"Thank you, Beatrice," Claire whispered, carrying two heavy cardboard boxes. "You have no idea what this means to us."

Mrs. Bea pushed open the door at the end of the hallway. 

It was a surprisingly large bedroom. The walls were painted a pale, soothing yellow, but the best part was the floor. There was no thick, plush carpet to bog down his heavy pneumatic tires. It was smooth, hard linoleum. 

"I claim this one!" Maggie announced, pushing past Mrs. Bea and dropping her trash bag of stuffed animals onto the floor. "For me and Drew."

Claire sighed, setting the boxes down. "Maggie, no. Mrs. Bea has the sewing room down the hall with a cot in it. That's where you'll sleep. You and your brother are nine years old now. You need your own space."

"But the floor in here is perfect for his chair!" Maggie argued, her hands planted firmly on her hips. She glared protectively around the room. "And it's close to the bathroom with the grab bars. He has to be in here."

"He will be in here," Claire said, rubbing her temples. The exhaustion of the move and the bankruptcy was clearly catching up to her. "But you can't share a room with a boy. And there's only one bed."

Drew looked at the center of the room. There was a single, heavy wooden bed frame holding a queen-size mattress. 

"We shared a room when we were babies," Maggie pointed out stubbornly. "And I'm not sleeping down the hall. What if he needs something in the middle of the night and he can't yell loud enough? I'm staying."

Claire opened her mouth to argue, but Mrs. Bea gently placed a hand on Claire's arm. 

"Leave it be, Claire," Mrs. Bea said softly, her Geordie accent wrapping around the words like a warm blanket. "They've been through a war today. Let them stick together."

Claire looked from Mrs. Bea to the stubborn set of Maggie's jaw, and finally down to Drew, who was silently hoping Maggie would win the argument. The trauma of the hospital had left him terrified of the dark, and Maggie was the only one who truly understood how to calm him down during his night terrors. He didn't want to sleep alone in a strange room. 

"Fine," Claire finally relented, letting out a defeated breath. "But you're nine. We have to have some boundaries."

For the next two hours, Claire and Mrs. Bea turned into makeshift engineers. They found a heavy coil of clothesline in the garage and strung it tightly across the ceiling, anchoring it into the drywall on opposite sides of the room. Then, using safety pins and three large bedsheets, they created a solid fabric wall that divided the room exactly in half, cutting right across the center of the queen-size mattress. 

"There," Claire said, wiping her hands together as she stepped back to admire their work. "Separate spaces. You change clothes on your own side of the curtain. Privacy."

Maggie immediately ducked under the hanging bedsheet to test her side of the mattress. "This is awesome. It's like a fort."

Drew drove his chair up to his side of the bed. It wasn't their house. It wasn't the life they were supposed to have. But the linoleum was smooth beneath his wheels, the ramp was outside the front door, and his sister was just on the other side of the curtain. 

For the first time since the bills had started arriving three years ago, things felt like they were going to be okay.
