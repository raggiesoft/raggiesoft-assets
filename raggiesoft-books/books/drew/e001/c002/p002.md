## 11:00 AM • Maggie — The Sofa

The morning sun filtered through the lace curtains of Mrs. Bea's living room. 

Six-year-old Maggie lay curled up on the plush floral sofa. She was buried under a heavy knit afghan blanket, her head resting on a soft pillow. Tucked tightly under her arm was Barnaby, her favorite brown teddy bear with the slightly crooked bowtie. 

Her forehead finally felt cool. The terrible, hot fever that had kept her home from her very first day of karate class had broken a few hours ago, leaving her feeling hollow and tired, but much better. 

The television was playing a cartoon at a very low volume, but Maggie wasn't really watching it. She kept looking at the front door. 

She had slept through the entire night on Mrs. Bea's sofa. When she woke up, her mom and Drew still weren't here. They had only gone to a one-hour karate class yesterday afternoon. They should have been back for dinner. 

Mrs. Bea walked into the living room carrying a small tray. She set a glass of apple juice and a few plain crackers on the coffee table. Her kind, wrinkled face looked very tired, and her eyes were a little red. 

"How are we feeling this morning, poppet?" Mrs. Bea asked gently, her thick Scottish accent making the words sound soft and musical. She sat down on the edge of the coffee table, right facing the sofa. 

"Better," Maggie said, her voice small. She sat up a little bit, pulling Barnaby closer to her chest. "Where's my mom? Where's Drew?"

Mrs. Bea took a deep breath. She reached out and gently smoothed Maggie's long brown hair away from her face. 

"Well, sweetheart, I need to tell you something, and I need you to be a brave big sister," Mrs. Bea said softly. "Your brother got hurt at his karate class yesterday."

Maggie's eyes widened. "Is he okay? Did he scrape his knee?"

"He got hurt a bit worse than a scraped knee," Mrs. Bea explained, keeping her voice incredibly calm and steady so Maggie wouldn't be frightened. "He had a bad accident, so your mom took him to the hospital. The doctors there are very smart, and they are taking excellent care of him. They fixed his tummy, and he's resting right now."

Maggie’s lower lip quivered. The hospital was a scary place. "I want to go see him. I want my mom."

"I know you do, pet," Mrs. Bea said, her heart aching for the little girl. "And your mom wants to see you so badly. But we have a very important rule right now. Because you just had a fever, you still have some germs. Hospitals have strict rules about germs, especially where Drew is resting. If we bring you in, it could make the other kids sick."

Maggie sniffled, burying her face into her teddy bear. "But Drew needs me. We do everything together."

"He does need you," Mrs. Bea agreed, rubbing Maggie's arm. "But he needs you to be fully better. Your mom and I talked on the telephone this morning. She said the best thing you can do for Drew right now is to rest on this sofa, drink your juice, and get all your strength back. Can you do that for him?"

Maggie looked down at her apple juice, then back at the front door. The house felt entirely too quiet without her twin brother running around and making noise. 

"Will he come home soon?" Maggie whispered. 

"He has to stay with the doctors for a little while until he's strong enough," Mrs. Bea said honestly, offering a warm, reassuring smile. "But he is safe, and your mom is sitting right next to his bed holding his hand. And as soon as the doctors say you don't have any more germs, I will drive you straight there so you can give him the biggest hug in the world."

Maggie nodded slowly, sniffling once more. She picked up the glass of apple juice with two hands and took a small sip. She had to get better. Drew was waiting for her.

## 3:15 PM • Claire — The Coalition

The harsh fluorescent lighting of the hospital corridor buzzed with a low, grating frequency that seemed to vibrate directly into Claire’s skull. She stood alone near the main elevator bank on the surgical floor, holding a styrofoam cup of black coffee she hadn't taken a single sip from. 

Drew was asleep again, finally resting peacefully under the heavy blanket of intravenous painkillers. The PICU nurses had gently, but firmly, insisted she step out of the unit for a few minutes to stretch her legs and get a drink. She didn't want to leave. The thought of being more than a hundred feet away while her six-year-old son lay permanently paralyzed from a spinal stroke with half his internal organs rearranged was physically repulsive. 

But she also couldn't afford to collapse from exhaustion. 

As she stared blankly at the floor indicator numbers above the elevator doors, a woman stepped out of the adjacent stairwell, heading straight for the bank of public payphones on the wall. 

Claire’s eyes tracked the movement out of pure, exhausted habit. The woman was sharply dressed, her posture rigid, but there was a frantic, terrifying exhaustion pulling at her features. She picked up the payphone receiver, her hands trembling so badly she fumbled the quarters before finally shoving them into the slot. 

Claire stared at her, a jolt of recognition slicing through her brain fog. 

She knew this woman. She had seen her just yesterday evening. 

It was the mother from the Dojang lobby. The one who had walked out the front doors with the two brown-belt children—the boy who had been limping, and the little girl who was crying and clutching her ribs. 

Without thinking, Claire stepped away from the elevators. Her legs felt heavy and detached as she crossed the polished linoleum, stopping a few feet away from the payphones just as the woman hung up the receiver with a heavy sigh.

"Excuse me," Claire said softly, her voice raspy from crying and disuse. 

The woman jumped slightly, spinning around. Her eyes, red-rimmed and wide, locked onto Claire. A flash of confusion crossed her face before settling into the same, sudden recognition. 

"You were in the lobby yesterday," the woman breathed, her hand still resting on the metal phone cord. "You were sitting on the couch."

"Claire Hayes," Claire said, extending a trembling hand. "My son... my son Drew was in the beginner's class."

The woman took Claire’s hand. Her grip was tight, desperate. "Ellen Ragsdale. My children, Michael and Rachel, they..." Ellen’s voice cracked, and she squeezed her eyes shut, fighting back a fresh wave of tears. "They're downstairs. In the emergency trauma bay."

Claire felt the floor drop out from underneath her. Officer Miller's words from last night echoed in her mind: *'If we have multiple victims in one session... Chesapeake PD needs to lock down that entire roster immediately.'*

"He hurt them too," Claire whispered, the horrifying reality solidifying between them. It wasn't an accident. It was a massacre. 

"He stepped on them," Ellen said, her voice dropping into a harsh, tear-choked whisper. "He deliberately pressed his weight onto my babies' stomachs. Michael has torn fascia. Rachel has a massive hematoma on her spleen. They can't move. They have to lie perfectly flat for four months." 

"Drew is in the PICU," Claire said, the words spilling out of her in a rush of shared, devastating trauma. "He had to have his spleen removed last night. He almost bled to death internally. And the doctors... they said the pressure damaged his pituitary gland. And his spine." Claire covered her mouth with her hand to stifle a sob. "He had a spinal stroke. He's never going to walk again, Ellen. My baby boy is paralyzed."

Ellen gasped, her hands flying up to cover her mouth. For a long, silent moment, the two mothers stood in the harsh lighting of the hospital corridor, entirely shattered by the magnitude of what had been stolen from them in a single, one-hour martial arts class.

But as the silence stretched, the profound, weeping grief in Ellen’s eyes began to shift. The sorrow didn't vanish, but it hardened. It crystallized into a cold, absolute, terrifying fury. 

"I took them to our pediatrician this morning," Ellen stated, her voice losing its tremble and dropping into a rigid, tactical register. "He called the police. The detective told us they arrested Curtis an hour ago."

"I spoke to the police last night," Claire confirmed, her own anger mirroring Ellen's. "I told them everything I saw in the lobby. I told them your kids were hurt, too."

"They are going to lock him in a cage," Ellen said fiercely, stepping closer to Claire. "And when they are done with him, I am going to destroy the man who owns that facility. Ray knew what was happening in his gym, and he let it happen."

Ellen dug into her purse, pulling out a small notepad and a pen. She quickly scribbled down a phone number, tore the sheet off, and pressed it firmly into Claire’s hand. 

"This is my home number," Ellen said, her eyes locked onto Claire’s with absolute, unyielding intent. "When the criminal trial starts, they are going to try to divide us. They are going to try to say these were isolated accidents. We cannot let them. We are going to form a coalition, Claire. We are going to take everything they have."

Claire looked down at the slip of paper in her hand. The overwhelming, crushing helplessness she had felt for the last eighteen hours receded just a fraction, replaced by the stabilizing force of an ally. She wasn't fighting this alone. 

"Give me your number," Ellen instructed, poising her pen over the notepad. "I will call you on Monday."

Claire dictated her home phone number, watching as Ellen wrote it down with sharp, precise strokes. 

"I have to get back down to the trauma bay," Ellen said, slipping the notepad back into her purse. She reached out and gripped Claire’s forearm tightly, a promise passed from one mother to another. "Tell Drew to hold on. We are going to make them pay."

"We will," Claire vowed, her voice steadying. "Tell your kids the same."

Ellen gave a single, sharp nod, turning and heading back toward the stairwell. 

Claire stood alone by the payphones, clutching the small piece of paper in her hand. The buzz of the fluorescent lights didn't feel quite so maddening anymore. She turned and walked back toward the PICU doors, ready to return to her son's side, armed with the first piece of ammunition for the war to come.
