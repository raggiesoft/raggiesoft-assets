The pediatric inpatient rehabilitation wing was much brighter and louder than the PICU. The walls were painted with cheerful murals of jungle animals, and the nurses’ station was plastered with crayon drawings. But for Claire, the transition out of intensive care came with a harsh, unyielding dose of reality. 

In the PICU, the goal was simply survival. Up here in rehab, the goal was learning how to live with the permanent damage. 

Claire stood near the doorway of Room 412, her arms crossed tightly over her chest as she watched the physical therapist lock the brakes on a brand-new, bright red pediatric wheelchair. It was incredibly small, perfectly scaled for a six-year-old, with anti-tip wheels on the back and thick, padded armrests. 

It was a piece of medical equipment designed to give her son his mobility back. But to Claire, it was a heavy, mechanical symbol that her little boy would never run across a playground again. 

"Alright, Drew," the therapist, a kind woman named Sarah, said gently. "We've got the transfer board lined up. Just like we practiced. Use your arms to push up, and I'll help guide your hips."

Drew was sitting on the edge of the hospital bed, wearing gray sweatpants and a loose t-shirt. He looked nervous, his eyes darting between the gap from the mattress to the wheelchair cushion. Slowly, he pressed his hands flat against the bed and pushed. Sarah gripped the heavy medical gait belt securely fastened around his waist and smoothly lifted and pivoted his lower body. 

A second later, Drew dropped securely onto the cushion of the wheelchair. Sarah quickly adjusted his feet onto the footrests and strapped a thin safety belt across his lap. 

"Perfect," Sarah praised, stepping back. "How does it feel?"

Drew gripped the padded armrests. He looked down at the large, rubber-treaded wheels on either side of him, then back up at his mother. "It feels okay. It's kind of big."

"You'll grow into it, sweetheart," Claire said, forcing a warm, encouraging smile onto her face despite the knot tightening in her throat. She stepped forward, intending to show him how the wheel rims worked. 

But a small, yellow-clad blur beat her to it. 

"My turn!" Maggie announced, dropping her school backpack onto the linoleum floor with a heavy thud. She marched straight up to the back of the wheelchair, reaching up to wrap her small hands securely around the black rubber push-handles. 

She had just been dropped off by Mrs. Bea after school, and she was clearly ready to fulfill her duties. 

"I promised I'm the driver," Maggie told the physical therapist with absolute, unshakeable authority. 

Sarah chuckled, exchanging a fond look with Claire. "Well, every driver needs to do a test lap. Do you know where the brakes are?"

"Right here," Maggie said, pointing to the silver levers near the wheels. She looked down at the top of Drew's head. "Ready, racecar?"

"Ready," Drew grinned, his nervous hesitation instantly evaporating the second his twin sister took the wheel. 

"Hold on tight!" Maggie warned. 

She leaned her weight into the handles and shoved forward. The wheelchair rolled smoothly over the polished linoleum, much faster and easier than Maggie had anticipated. 

"Vroom!" Drew yelled, throwing his hands up in the air as they cleared the doorway and merged into the wide hospital corridor. 

Maggie giggled, her tiny sneakers squeaking rapidly against the floor as she broke into a light jog to keep the momentum going. "Super-fast racecar coming through! Beep beep!"

"Maggie, wait!" Claire gasped, her heart instantly leaping into her throat. Drew was only four weeks out from a massive abdominal surgery. His spleen had been removed, his fascia was still healing, and he was strapped into a moving piece of heavy equipment. 

Claire rushed out into the hallway, catching up to them just as they neared the nurses' station. She reached out and firmly grabbed one of the push-handles, smoothly bringing the wheelchair to a halt. 

"Whoa, slow down, Mario Andretti," Claire breathed, dropping to one knee so she was at eye level with both of them. 

Maggie frowned, looking slightly deflated. "But it's a racecar, Mom. Racecars go fast."

"I know, baby," Claire said gently, smoothing Maggie’s brown hair back from her forehead. "But this is a special hospital racecar. And Drew's tummy is still healing. If we go too fast and hit a bump, or if we have to stop suddenly, it could hurt him really badly."

Drew looked down at his lap, then back up at his sister. "Maybe we should be a slow racecar today, Mags."

Maggie considered this for a moment. Her protective instincts instantly overrode her desire for speed. She nodded solemnly. 

"Okay," Maggie agreed. "We will be a stealth racecar. Super quiet and super careful."

"I like the sound of a stealth racecar," Claire smiled, the panic in her chest finally receding. She stood back up and released the handle. "Let's see your stealth mode."

Maggie gripped the handles again, but this time, she walked at a measured, deliberate pace, carefully scanning the floor ahead of them for any obstacles. Drew kept his hands resting safely on the armrests, looking around the hallway from his new, seated vantage point. 

As Claire watched her six-year-old daughter carefully navigate her twin brother down the pediatric ward, the heavy, crushing sorrow of the wheelchair faded just a fraction. It wasn't the life she had envisioned for him, and the road ahead was going to be incredibly difficult. 

But looking at the two of them, Claire knew one thing for certain: Drew was never going to be navigating that road alone.
