The pediatric rehab wing was usually strictly dark and silent by eight o'clock, but tonight was different. The fluorescent lights in the hallway were dimmed, replaced by the soft, flickering glow of the television screens mounted in the corners of every room. 

It was almost midnight on New Year's Eve. 

Drew lay in his hospital bed, propped up against a mountain of pillows so he could see the small CRT TV. His eyelids felt incredibly heavy, but he was determined not to fall asleep. 

In the bed next to his, an eight-year-old boy named Sam was staring blankly at the screen. Sam had been in the rehab wing since a terrible car accident in early November, his left leg suspended in an intimidating metal traction rig. Sam usually talked a lot during the day, but tonight, he had been quiet and sullen.

"Almost time, buddy," Claire whispered. She was sitting in the hard plastic visitor's chair beside Drew's bed, looking exhausted but smiling warmly. Maggie was curled up on the small pull-out cot by the window, completely dead to the world, snoring softly with her favorite blanket pulled over her head. 

A few minutes later, Nurse Sarah pushed the door open, carrying a plastic tray. She had draped a shiny silver tinsel garland around her neck like a feather boa. 

"Alright, gentlemen," Sarah whispered cheerfully, stepping into the room. "I have the good stuff."

She handed Claire a small plastic cup, then carefully handed one to Drew and one to Sam. It was sparkling apple cider. The bubbles tickled Drew's nose as he brought it close to his face. 

On the television, a massive crowd in New York City was cheering, huddled together in the freezing cold. The bright numbers on the screen flashed. 

*11:58 PM.*

Drew looked down at his legs, hidden beneath the white hospital blankets. He couldn't feel them. He hadn't felt them since that terrible Thursday afternoon in August when Master Curtis had stepped on him. 

In Drew's six-year-old mind, 1995 was a monster. It was the year of the Dojang, the year of the sirens, the surgeries, the needles, and the terrifying realization that he was never going to walk again. It was the year his mother cried in the hallway when she thought he was asleep. 

He hated 1995. He couldn't wait for it to be over.

"Ten!" the television announcer yelled. 

Claire leaned forward, resting her hand gently on Drew's arm. "Nine," she counted along softly.

"Eight! Seven! Six!"

Drew looked over at Sam. Sam was holding his little plastic cup, but he wasn't counting. He just looked sad. Drew remembered feeling that exact same way a week ago, watching the other kids pack up their bags to go home for Christmas while he was stuck in this bed. 

"Three! Two! One!" 

"Happy New Year!" Claire whispered, leaning in and kissing Drew firmly on the forehead. 

Nurse Sarah blew a quiet, muted party horn from the doorway and smiled. "Happy 1996, boys."

"Happy New Year, Mommy," Drew said. He reached over and gently clinked his small plastic cup against hers. 

He took a sip of the sweet, fizzy cider. 1996 was finally here. The doctors had already told his mom the plan. Sometime this month, once the bathroom at the house was finished, Drew was finally going to be discharged. 

1996 was the year he was going home.
