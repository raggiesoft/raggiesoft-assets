The bluejeans felt stiff and heavy against Drew's legs, the thick denim rubbing strangely against his skin. After five solid months of thin cotton hospital gowns and soft pajama bottoms, actual street clothes felt almost alien. 

Claire knelt on the linoleum floor next to his hospital bed, carefully tying the laces of his brand-new sneakers. She smoothed the cuffs of his jeans over the tops of the shoes, then looked up at him with a tired, glowing smile. 

"How does that feel, buddy?" she asked softly.

"Weird," Drew admitted, tugging at the collar of his red sweater. "My legs feel heavy."

"That's just the jeans," she promised, standing up. She took a deep breath, looking past him to the doorway. "Okay. Ready for your new ride?"

A physical therapist rolled it into the room. It wasn't the clunky, oversized manual chair the hospital usually used to transport patients down to radiology. This was his. A custom pediatric power wheelchair. The frame was painted a deep, glossy black, and the seat was heavily padded to support his spine. On the right armrest sat a black joystick.

The reality of it hit Drew like a physical weight. The hospital bed had been a temporary prison. But this chair was permanent. This was how he was going to move through the world from now on.

With the therapist's help, Claire gently lifted Drew from the edge of the bed and lowered him into the power chair. It felt significantly different than sitting on a mattress. It felt solid. Engineered. The therapist showed him how to rest his hand on the joystick and nudged it forward just a millimeter. The chair hummed quietly and rolled forward an inch.

Drew’s heart skipped a beat. For the first time since August, he was moving under his own power.

"Let's go say goodbye," Claire smiled, grabbing their duffel bags.

Drew drove the chair slowly out into the hallway. The nurses at the station—Sarah, Michelle, and the others who had taken care of him for almost half a year—were waiting. They clapped and cheered as he drove out, some of them wiping tears from their eyes as they bent down to hug him.

But before they headed for the elevators, Drew turned the joystick and rolled back toward his room. 

Sam was sitting in his bed, his leg still suspended in the heavy traction rig. The television was on, but Sam wasn't watching it. He was looking at Drew’s new chair. The eight-year-old looked incredibly small, and incredibly sad. 

Drew remembered the suffocating weight of watching other kids leave before Christmas. He drove his chair right up to the side of Sam's bed. 

"I'm gonna beat Gorilla King Land for you," Drew said seriously, looking up at his roommate. 

Sam offered a weak, crooked smile. "You better."

"I will," Drew promised. "See ya, Sam."

The trip down the elevator and out through the automatic sliding doors felt like a dream. The freezing January air hit Drew's face like a shockwave. He hadn't felt real, unfiltered outdoor wind since summer. 

Getting into the family minivan was a jarring new reality. There was no easy way to do it. Claire had to physically lift him out of the heavy power chair and carefully maneuver him into the passenger seat, buckling him in before she and the therapist wrestled the heavy motorized chair into the back of the van. 

The drive through the city was a blur of bare trees and gray skies. Drew stared out the window, his six-year-old mind struggling to process how big the world looked when you weren't confined to a single room. 

When Claire finally turned the blinker on and pulled into their driveway, Drew's breath caught in his throat. 

It was his house. The same brick front, the same front yard. But as Claire wrestled his heavy power chair out of the back of the van, the brutal reality of their situation became instantly clear. 

There was no ramp. Claire didn't have the money or the time to have one built. 

Claire had to physically lift Drew out of the car, carry him up the concrete front steps, and sit him down on the porch. Then, she had to go back to the driveway, grab the impossibly heavy power chair, and drag it backward, groaning with exertion, bumping it up step by step until it cleared the threshold. By the time she helped Drew back into the seat, she was sweating and breathing heavily.

Drew rolled into the foyer. 

The house smelled exactly the same—a mix of laundry detergent and Claire’s coffee—but it was suddenly hostile to him. The hallway felt entirely too narrow. The thick living room carpet caught the casters of his chair, making the motors whine in protest as he tried to turn. 

"Drew!"

A small blur of motion launched from the living room. Maggie threw her arms around his neck, burying her face into his shoulder. She squeezed him so tightly he could barely breathe, and for the first time all morning, Drew started to cry. He wrapped his arms around his twin sister, holding onto her like she was the only thing keeping him anchored to the earth. 

"You're home," Maggie sobbed into his sweater. "You're finally home."

Drew looked past her into the house. Down the hall, the door to the main bathroom was open. It was just a standard, narrow bathroom with a high-walled porcelain tub. There was no roll-in shower. There were no grab bars. 

It was a stark, terrifying reminder of how hard their lives were going to be from now on. The house wasn't built for him, and his mother was going to have to physically lift and carry him through all of its obstacles every single day. 

But as Maggie squeezed him tighter and Claire rested a warm, exhausted hand on the back of his neck, the fear began to recede. The hospital was behind him. 

He was finally home.
