The late January air bit at Drew’s cheeks as he sat in his power chair at the bottom of the concrete driveway. Claire stood beside him, her hands jammed into her coat pockets, exhaling a long, visible plume of breath. 

Standing stubbornly in front of the chair, refusing to move, was Maggie. 

"It's stupid," Maggie said, her voice trembling with a mix of cold and anger. "I want to ride with Drew."

"I know, Mags," Claire said, her tone carrying the heavy exhaustion of a woman who had spent the last five months arguing with insurance companies and school administrators. "But we talked about this. You can't."

It was a strict liability policy for Virginia Beach City Public Schools. The special education buses—the "short buses"—were funded and regulated entirely differently than the standard fleet. Only students whose Individualized Education Programs explicitly mandated accessible transportation were legally permitted to step foot on them. There were no exceptions for siblings. 

"We sat in the exact same seat every single day in Kindergarten," Maggie argued, her eyes welling up behind her glasses. "I can just sit next to him. I won't get in the way."

"It's not about getting in the way," Claire said gently, kneeling down to look Maggie in the eye. "The driver isn't allowed to let you on. If he does, he could lose his job. You have to take the regular bus."

Down at the corner of the street, the familiar squeal and hiss of air brakes echoed through the neighborhood. The big yellow school bus had arrived. 

Maggie looked at the bus, then back at Drew. The pain on her face was sharp and devastating. They had done everything together. They were supposed to do first grade together. 

"Go on," Drew told her quietly, his hand resting on his joystick. 

Maggie gave him a tight, desperate hug, burying her face in his coat for a second before pulling away. She turned and broke into a heavy jog down the street, her oversized backpack bouncing against her shoulders. Drew watched her climb the steps of the big bus alone. 

Ten minutes later, Drew’s bus arrived. 

There was nothing normal about it. It pulled up to the curb and the driver threw a switch, causing the side doors to fold open. A hydraulic lift deployed with a loud, whining, mechanical groan that echoed off the neighboring houses. Drew had to drive his chair onto the metal platform, wait as it slowly hoisted him into the air, and then back into the aisle. 

Inside, there were no rows of green vinyl seats to share with a sister. Instead, the driver spent three minutes hooking heavy, ratcheting canvas straps to the metal D-rings on the bottom of Drew’s chair, locking him to the floor tracks so the 150-pound chair wouldn't become a projectile in an accident. 

When the bus finally arrived at the elementary school, it didn't drop him off at the front doors where the hundreds of other kids were swarming the entrance. It drove around to a side door near the cafeteria. 

Waiting for him on the concrete ramp was Mrs. Higgins. 

"There's my special guy!" she chirped as Drew rolled off the hydraulic lift. She was an older paraprofessional, wearing a floral sweater and a lanyard heavy with keys. She meant well, but her voice had that high-pitched, patronizing lilt usually reserved for toddlers. "Let's get you out of the cold, sweetie."

She hovered over him the entire way down the hallway, rushing ahead to pull open the heavy fire doors and shooing away younger kids who didn't move fast enough. By the time they reached his first-grade classroom, Drew felt entirely like a spectacle. 

Mrs. Albright's classroom looked exactly like Kindergarten had. The walls were covered in brightly colored alphabet banners, and the standard wooden desks were pushed together into "pods" of four so the kids could work in groups. 

Except for Drew. 

The heavy base of his power chair meant he couldn't slide his legs under the low cubbies of the standard first-grade desks. The school's solution had been to scrounge up a standalone, adjustable-height gray folding table. They had placed it near the chalkboard, separated from the desk pods by a wide, awkward path of empty carpet so Drew could navigate without hitting anything. 

He was completely isolated. 

As Mrs. Higgins awkwardly helped him take his winter coat off, the bell rang. The classroom door opened, and a flood of first-graders poured in, shaking off the cold and tossing their backpacks into their cubbies. 

It didn't take long for them to notice the massive black machine sitting in the front of the room. 

First-graders had zero filter. A boy in a striped shirt dropped his backpack and walked straight up to Drew's table, staring wide-eyed at the joystick and the heavy wheels. 

"Whoa," the boy said. "Is that a robot?"

A girl with pigtails leaned over from the closest desk pod. "Why are you in that? What's wrong with your legs?"

"Okay, class, let's find our seats!" Mrs. Albright called out nervously from her desk, while Mrs. Higgins stepped forward, waving her hands. 

"Now, now, we don't ask rude questions," Mrs. Higgins scolded the children. 

Drew's hand tightened around the joystick. Instinctively, he looked toward the classroom door. 

In Kindergarten, Maggie would have already stepped between him and the boy. She would have told the girl with the pigtails to mind her own business. She would have buffered the stares and absorbed the attention. 

But Maggie wasn't there. She was in a different classroom down the hall, sitting in a normal pod of four desks, probably looking at the door, too. 

For the first time since August, Drew was completely on his own. 

"They don't work," Drew said quietly, looking the boy in the eye over Mrs. Higgins' hovering arm. "I can't walk." 

The boy blinked, processed the information, and then shrugged. "My dad has a truck with big wheels like that."

Drew didn't smile, but he let go of the joystick. It was going to be a very long day.
