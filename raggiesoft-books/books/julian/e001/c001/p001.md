The fluorescent lights in the James City County Middle School guidance office buzzed with a low, grating electrical hum.

Julian sat completely rigid in his manual wheelchair, his hands clamped tightly over his ears. He hated this room. It smelled faintly of stale coffee and industrial floor wax, and the sheer volume of paperwork piled on the counselor's desk was visually overwhelming.

Clara and Maria sat on either side of him in hard plastic guest chairs. They weren't officially part of the Rowlands family, but when it came to Julian's Individualized Education Program (IEP) meetings, the identical twins' presence was legally mandated as a necessary medical accommodation. It was a highly unusual, virtually unheard-of directive that Eira had fought ruthlessly to secure. Clara leaned her shoulder firmly against his left side, and Maria mirrored her on the right, offering a steady, symmetrical grounding pressure.

Across the desk sat Mr. Caldwell, an exhausted guidance counselor who had spent his entire career trying to fit square pegs into round administrative holes.

"Alright," Mr. Caldwell sighed, adjusting his glasses as he looked over the high school transition paperwork. "Moving into the freshman year, Julian needs to select a foreign language track to stay on the advanced diploma path. And girls, your schedules need to mirror his exactly, per the IEP."

"That's correct," Eira stated. She sat perfectly straight in her chair, projecting an aura of absolute, uncompromising authority.

Internally, Eira was running on fumes. It had only been a few months since she had finally managed to force Tom out of the house. Because of Virginia’s strict family laws, she was trapped in the mandatory, agonizing one-year legal separation period before she could officially file for divorce. Tom’s ghost was still legally attached to her, and despite the strict no-contact order keeping him away from the kids, her tolerance for bureaucratic pushback regarding her children was absolutely zero.

Richard Harrison sat in the chair next to her, a quiet, unyielding sentry.

Mr. Caldwell tapped his pen against the file. "Well, this seems like an easy win. The file notes that Julian is already completely bilingual. He speaks French at home. And Clara, Maria, you two have picked it up from being around the family?"

"Yes, sir," the twins answered in perfect, identical unison.

"Perfect," Mr. Caldwell smiled, writing a note on the schedule. "I'll put all three of you down for French I. It'll be an easy 'A' for everyone, and it satisfies the graduation requirement. The high school French teacher, Madame Dupont, is wonderful."

"No," Eira said flatly. The single syllable cut through the buzzing office like a knife.

Mr. Caldwell blinked, his pen freezing over the paper. "Mrs. Cross, I assure you, it's the most logical—"

"My son speaks Québécois French, Mr. Caldwell," Eira interrupted, her Welsh accent clipping with cold precision. "The high school curriculum teaches Metropolitan, Parisian French. The vocabulary, the cadence, and the grammatical structures are vastly different."

"Well, French is French, isn't it?" Mr. Caldwell chuckled weakly, clearly out of his depth. "Madame Dupont will just correct his pronunciation."

Richard leaned forward, resting his forearms on his knees. He didn't raise his voice, but the weight of his tone instantly erased the counselor's smile.

"If a high school teacher stands at the front of a classroom and repeatedly tells an autistic teenager that his native language—the language he uses to feel safe in his own home—is wrong, it will trigger a catastrophic meltdown," Richard explained, laying out the psychological reality with brutal clarity. "French is strictly off the table."

Mr. Caldwell swallowed hard, crossing 'French' off the list. "Alright. Spanish? German? They are highly conversational classes..."

"Exactly the problem," Eira countered. "Modern language curriculums rely heavily on spontaneous oral exams and auditory comprehension tapes. When Julian is overwhelmed, his verbal abilities shut down. He relies on his AAC device. You cannot force him into a highly conversational, unpredictable auditory environment."

Julian dropped his hands from his ears, resting them on his AAC device. He hated the idea of a conversational class. He hated the idea of standing up in front of thirty teenagers and being forced to speak a language he didn't know.

He looked at Clara and Maria. They already knew exactly what he was thinking.

"What about Latin?" Clara asked quietly.

Mr. Caldwell looked up, surprised. "Latin? It's a dead language. Nobody speaks it."

"Exactly," Maria finished, a small, brilliant smile touching the corners of her mouth. She looked at Julian. "No oral exams. No listening to crackling cassette tapes. It's just reading and translating. It's all written down."

Julian’s eyes brightened. He pulled his AAC device onto his lap, his fingers flying across the keys. A language that didn't require talking? A language built entirely on rigid, mathematical grammatical rules? It wasn't a social exercise; it was a puzzle.

<aac>LATIN. IT IS A PUZZLE. I WANT THE PUZZLE.</aac> the robotic voice announced.

Richard smiled proudly, looking at his daughters and the boy they protected. "It satisfies the language requirement for the diploma, doesn't it?"

"Well... yes," Mr. Caldwell admitted, completely outmaneuvered. "The high school offers Latin I through IV."

"Then it's settled," Eira declared, standing up and ending the meeting entirely on her own terms. "Enroll them in Latin I. And Mr. Caldwell? Make sure their desks are placed in the back of the room, three in a row, with Julian in the center."
