## 9:30 AM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The Reinforcements

The gravel driveway of the Cross property crunched loudly under the heavy, worn tires of a battered transit van.

Gabriel stood by the frosted front window, his jaw clenched, watching the vehicle throw the transmission into park. The doors swung open before the engine even fully cut out.

Marcus stepped out of the driver’s side, the collar of his denim jacket popped against the biting Virginia wind. He hauled a massive, heavy-duty canvas bag filled with steel drum hardware over his shoulder. From the passenger side, Sarah emerged. She didn't have her usual bright, easygoing smile; her face was set in a hard, serious line as she hoisted her thick, padded electric cello case onto her back like a shield.

They didn't bother knocking. They pushed through the front door, bringing a gust of freezing air with them into the tense, suffocating atmosphere of the living room.

The moment they crossed the threshold, the sheer gravity of the situation hit them. They saw Eira huddled on the floral sofa, a heavy blanket wrapped tightly around her shaking shoulders. And then, their eyes snapped to the corner of the room.

The empty space. The missing manual wheelchair.

"Gabriel," Marcus said, his voice dropping into a low, completely stunned register as he dropped his hardware bag onto the carpet with a heavy metallic clank. "Man... what happened? Where's Julian?"

"Tom," Gabriel stated, the name sounding like a curse in the quiet room. He didn't sugarcoat it, and he didn't use neurotypical filler to soften the blow. "He ambushed Mom at the door at eight o'clock this morning. He grabbed Julian right out of his seat, threw him in his car, and threw the chair in the trunk. He took him."

Sarah’s breath hitched. Her hands flew to her mouth in absolute horror. She knew exactly what that meant. Julian wasn't just missing; he was entirely unmoored.

"He doesn't have his weighted blanket," Sarah whispered, her eyes wide as she looked at Gwendolyn, who was standing rigidly by the piano. "He doesn't have his routine. He's trapped in a moving car with a man who doesn't know how to calm his sensory spikes."

"We called the police," Gwendolyn explained, her tactical mind stepping forward to manage the briefing. "They put out an alert on the interstate. But they are treating it as a domestic custody dispute while they process the paperwork. They aren't treating it as an immediate medical emergency. We are losing hours."

"So we aren't waiting for them," Gabriel growled, his fierce, protective energy radiating off him in waves. He looked at his two bandmates—his friends, his garrison. "We wrote a track. A beacon. If we can get it recorded, mixed, and rushed to the local radio stations, Julian might hear it. We need it to be loud. We need it to be massive."

Marcus didn't offer empty platitudes. He didn't tell them everything was going to be okay, and he didn't question the logic of trying to fight a kidnapping with a rock song. He understood the absolute, primal desperation of an older brother backed into a corner.

Marcus simply nodded, his expression hardening into stone. He reached down and grabbed the handles of his hardware bag.

"Then let's go to war," Marcus said firmly. "Where are we setting up?"

"The spare room," Gwendolyn directed, pointing down the narrow hallway. "We need to soundproof it as much as we can. We are going to push the limits of every amplifier we have."

They moved as a synchronized, completely focused unit. For the next thirty minutes, the hallway was a blur of frantic, heavy lifting. They carried the snare, the heavy floor toms, the kick drum, and the cymbals into the small, carpeted spare room.

Sarah unzipped her padded case, pulling out her sleek, solid-body electric cello. She plugged the heavy quarter-inch cable directly into an oversized bass amplifier, bypassing the delicate, classical acoustic tones for something capable of tearing the paint off the walls.

Gwendolyn dragged her heavy synthesizer keyboard into the corner, booting up the digital strings and choral patches, while Gabriel hauled his heavy amplifier and his solid-body electric guitar into the center of the cramped space.

The room was suffocatingly tight, smelling of hot dust from the warming amplifiers and the frantic sweat of sheer panic. Wires snaked across the floor like a chaotic nervous system.

Gabriel strapped his electric guitar across his chest. He turned the distortion knob on his amplifier all the way up, completely bypassing any attempt at restraint. He looked around the tiny, crowded room at Marcus, Sarah, and Gwendolyn.

"The band is called The Winter Palace," Gabriel announced over the low, vibrating hum of the live amplifiers. "The track is called Upon This Winter's Night. Gwen is going to start us off with his music box melody to establish the baseline. But when I give the signal... I want you to hit it so hard the foundation shakes."

Sarah tightened her grip on her cello bow, her eyes blazing with unwavering solidarity. Marcus settled onto his drum throne, raising his heavy wooden sticks.

The garrison was armed. The recording light clicked to a bright, violent red.

## 9:45 AM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Wall of Sound

The spare room was suffocatingly hot, smelling of ozone, dust, and raw, unfiltered panic.

Gabriel stood in the center of the tangled mess of heavy black cables, his solid-body electric guitar strapped tight across his chest. He adjusted the heavy metal stand of the dynamic microphone, pulling it close to his mouth. Across the cramped room, Gwendolyn gave him a sharp, confirming nod from behind her synthesizer.

"Roll it," Gabriel commanded, his voice a low, gravelly rasp.

Marcus hit the record button on the archaic, four-track tape machine they had scavenged from a local pawn shop.

Gwendolyn’s fingers dropped onto the keys, replicating the fragile, pristine melody of Julian's music box. It was the anchor—the sound of their home, designed specifically to reach a boy who was autistic, non-verbal, and currently trapped in a sensory nightmare.

Gabriel closed his eyes. He pictured Julian in the back of Tom's car, miles away on I-64, terrified, freezing, and entirely without his perimeter. Gabriel’s chest heaved. He brought his pick down hard against the steel strings of his guitar, dialing the distortion up to a violent, jagged roar just as Marcus slammed his heavy boot onto the kick drum.

Sarah leaned into her electric cello, dragging her bow across the lowest string with punishing force. It wasn't classical music; it was a heavy, driving riff that hit Gabriel right in the sternum. It was the dark, relentless foundation of a massive, theatrical piece of symphonic rock.

Gabriel leaned into the microphone. He didn't just sing the lyrics; he weaponized them.

"The clock upon the mantel chimes... Reminding me of better times..."

He poured every ounce of his protective, older-brother fury into the vocal. He was the frontline shield, and right now, his voice was the only perimeter he had left to throw over his brother.

"The window pane is frosted white... I'm searching for a sign tonight."

They slammed into the pre-chorus. The strings built the tension, winding tighter and tighter. And then, they hit the drop on the word Candle. The small spare room physically shook from the sheer volume of the amplifiers.

But as they pushed through the chorus and reached the bridge, Gabriel abruptly slapped his palm flat against his guitar strings, killing the sound in a screech of harsh feedback.

Marcus stopped playing, his drumsticks hovering in the air. Sarah lowered her bow, breathing heavily.

"It's not enough," Gabriel growled, running a frustrated, trembling hand through his hair. He looked at the four of them crammed into the tiny space. "It's loud, but it's not massive. It sounds like four teenagers in a bedroom. If this is going to be a beacon, it needs to sound like an army. It needs to command the radio dial."

Gwendolyn stared at her synthesizer, her tactical mind instantly processing the deficit. "The bridge," she said, her voice sharp with realization. "When you sing 'I'm sending out a prayer', my voice isn't enough to answer you. We wrote it for a haunting choir. And we need a glockenspiel to pierce through the heavy bass tones."

"We need an orchestra," Sarah agreed, wiping sweat from her forehead. "We need it to sound like a rock opera."

Gabriel unstrapped his guitar, his jaw setting into absolute stone. The desperation was clawing at his throat, but he channeled it entirely into logistics. The 1996 single wasn't just a song anymore; it was a desperate, musical plea for his brother's life.

"I'm going back to the wall phone," Gabriel announced, turning toward the hallway. He looked at the drummer. "Marcus. Richard Harrison's girls. We need them here now."

"Maria and Clara," Marcus nodded instantly, catching Gabriel's wavelength. "Maria has the brass glockenspiel from the marching band. And Clara has perfect pitch. She basically runs the vocal arrangements for the youth choir."

To anyone else at the high school, the fourteen-year-old identical twins were just Richard Harrison's daughters. But Gabriel knew exactly what they were to his brother. They were Julian's absolute best friends.

Maria was the fierce, physical shield. Clara was the flawless auditory anchor. Because they looked and sounded exactly alike, down to the same dusting of freckles, Julian's easily overwhelmed brain registered them as a perfectly unified constant. Outside of Gwendolyn, Maria and Clara were the only girls in the world allowed into Julian's physical space. When Julian was overloaded, his absolute favorite safe harbor was being sandwiched between the twins on the sofa, anchored by a symmetrical, dual-sided deep-pressure cuddle.

If Julian was out there in the dark, he needed to hear his best friends calling him home.

"Call them," Gabriel ordered, stepping out into the hallway. "Call Richard at his office. Call the high school and get Maria and Clara on the line. I don't care if they're sitting in third period right now. Tell them to walk out. Tell them Julep was taken, and we need every single voice and instrument they can fit into this house."

Gwendolyn stood up from her keyboard, a fierce, desperate fire burning in her eyes.

"Tell them to park on the lawn if they have to," Gwendolyn added, her voice echoing down the hall. "We are going to build a wall of sound so thick that Tom won't be able to turn the dial without hearing us coming."

Gabriel grabbed the plastic receiver of the kitchen phone. He was eighteen, broke, and absolutely terrified. But as he began dialing numbers, preparing to turn their quiet Virginia home into a chaotic, crowded war room, he knew one thing for certain: the Harrison twins were going to drop everything and tear the world apart to save their best friend, and Gabriel was going to get his brother back, no matter how loud he had to scream to do it.

## 10:15 AM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The Makeshift Orchestra

Gabriel stood by the frosted front window, his chest tight, watching the driveway. The quiet, residential street in Ewell was suddenly transforming into a chaotic staging ground.

A heavy, reliable station wagon pulled aggressively onto the frost-covered grass of the front lawn, entirely disregarding the driveway. Two battered sedans full of high school band students pulled up right behind it. Car doors slammed in rapid succession.

Richard Harrison stepped out of the station wagon, his expression carved from absolute stone. Right behind him were his fourteen-year-old identical twin daughters. Clara and Maria marched up the walkway in perfect synchronization, their faces flushed from the freezing wind and blazing with a terrifying, fiercely protective rage. Clara was already organizing the sheet music in her hands, while Maria carefully balanced the heavy wooden case holding her marching-band glockenspiel.

Gabriel pulled the front door open, waving them inside. The freezing Virginia wind swept in with them, but the sheer, focused energy they brought instantly warmed the house. They didn't ask questions, and there was no casual small talk. When Gabriel had called Richard's office, the former music therapy teacher hadn't hesitated; he had driven straight to the high school, signed his daughters out of third period, and mobilized the frontline.

"We're here," Richard announced, shedding his heavy coat and dropping it onto the floor. He looked at Gabriel, projecting the quiet, unyielding authority of a man ready to go to war for his students. "Tell us what you need."

"We need a wall of sound," Gabriel said, his voice a low, gravelly rumble. He pointed toward the cramped spare room down the hall, where distorted guitar feedback was already humming like a live wire. "We need Clara's choir for the bridge, and we need Maria's glockenspiel to pierce through the distortion. We are recording a beacon."

Before Richard could respond, a sharp, defeated sob echoed from the kitchen.

Gabriel turned. His mother, Eira, was hanging up the heavy plastic wall phone, her hand trembling so violently she missed the cradle twice before it finally clicked into place.

"Mom?" Gabriel asked, closing the distance between them in three long strides. "Was that the precinct? Did they find the car?"

Eira shook her head, burying her face in her hands. "They haven't even issued the alert yet, Gabriel."

"What?!" Gabriel roared, his protective energy violently flaring. "It's been over two hours! He's non-verbal and he doesn't have his chair! He is critically endangered!"

"It's the bureaucracy," Eira wept, her voice muffled against her palms. "Because Tom is his biological father, the dispatcher said it's technically a civil custody dispute until they can physically verify the court orders. The archives are at the county courthouse. Everything is on paper. A clerk has to pull the physical divorce decree, verify that Tom has zero visitation rights, and get a judge to sign off on the breach before they can legally flag Julian as a kidnapping victim in the state system."

"How long?" Gwendolyn demanded, stepping out of the spare room. Her tactical mind was instantly calculating the horrifying delay.

"They said it could take until mid-afternoon," Eira choked out. "Gabriel... Tom could have him across the state line by then."

Gabriel’s vision swam with red. Mid-afternoon. Julian would be trapped in the back of a car for hours, subjected to unpredictable sensory inputs, unable to shift his own body weight, completely terrified and wondering why his family hadn't come for him.

"Then we don't wait for the police," Gabriel snarled, turning back to the makeshift orchestra crowding his living room. The desperation in his chest hardened into absolute, unyielding steel. "We finish this track right now."

Clara stepped forward, looking exactly like her twin sister but carrying a completely different auditory command. She looked at the hastily scribbled lyrics Gwendolyn had handed her. "Gabriel, even if we record a masterpiece in the next hour... how are you getting it out there? Local radio stations have corporate playlists. They don't just take cassette tapes from teenagers off the street."

"I don't know," Gabriel admitted, his voice cracking with sheer frustration. "I'll break into the broadcast booth if I have to."

"You won't have to break in," Richard Harrison spoke up quietly from the center of the room.

Gabriel looked at him.

Richard stepped forward, his eyes locking onto Eira's devastated face before shifting to Gabriel. "I left teaching to work corporate for a broadcasting group," Richard explained, his voice calm, steady, and entirely absolute. "We own a massive block of FM rock stations across Virginia and down into the Carolinas. And I am the regional program director who controls the live feed."

Gwendolyn’s eyes widened, the tactical puzzle instantly snapping together.

"I know Julian," Richard continued, his voice thick with emotion. "I know how he communicates, and I know how much he relies on his routine. If you record this beacon, I will bypass the corporate playlist. I will push the track live over the airwaves across the entire state. If Tom Cross turns on a radio today, I will make sure this is the only thing he hears."

Gabriel’s heart hammered a frantic, heavy rhythm against his ribs. It wasn't just a desperate shot in the dark anymore. They had a direct, viable pipeline to the state's broadcast towers. And Eira looked at Richard, seeing the exact, unwavering shield her family so desperately needed.

"Set up the microphones," Gabriel commanded, turning to Gwendolyn and the twins. "We push the vocals in the living room, keep the heavy rhythm section in the spare room. Clara, I need your choir to sound like an absolute army answering my prayer on the bridge. Maria, the glockenspiel has to be perfect."

The identical twins shared a single, synchronized nod, their best friend's life entirely on the line.

The house immediately exploded into chaotic, focused motion. Microphones were dragged into the living room, cables snaking across the carpets.

Gabriel strapped his electric guitar back across his chest, the heavy weight of it feeling like armor. He looked at the sheet of lyrics, picturing the freezing, desolate miles of the interstate. He was going to sing so loudly that the sheer force of it would shatter the windows of his father's car.
