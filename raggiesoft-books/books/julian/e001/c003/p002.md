The spare room and the adjoining hallway were packed wall-to-wall. The air was thick with the heat of glowing amplifiers, frantic breath, and absolute, unified desperation.

Gabriel stood in the center of the living room, the dynamic microphone gripped tightly in his calloused hands. Down the hall, Marcus counted off the tempo with a heavy clack-clack-clack of his drumsticks.

They tore through the first verse and the chorus in a single, breathless take. Gabriel’s voice was a raw, bleeding instrument, carrying the weight of a brother who had failed to block the front door. Behind him, Sarah’s electric cello carved a dark, relentless groove, while the brass section—crammed near the kitchen archway—swelled to build a massive, theatrical wall of sound.

"The bridge!" Gwendolyn yelled from her synthesizer, her fingers flying across the keys to shift the tempo into a driving, frantic rock-opera march. "Clara, go!"

Clara stepped up to the secondary microphone, leading the youth choir vocalists. They didn't just sing; they chanted with the perfect, flawless pitch of a marching army.

(Gabriel) "I’m sending out a prayer!" (Choir) "Bring him home! Bring him home!" (Gabriel) "To vanish all despair!" (Choir) "You are not alone!" In the center of the room, Gabriel closed his eyes. The tears he had been fighting all morning finally broke, tracking hot and fast down his cheeks, but his voice didn't waver. He leaned his entire body weight into the microphone stand.

"No matter where you are... no matter how far! Just look to the Northern Star!" Right on cue, Maria struck the metal keys of her glockenspiel. The bright, piercing, crystalline notes shattered through the heavy distortion of the guitars and the booming brass. It sounded exactly like stars bursting in the dark—a pristine, undeniable beacon cutting through the noise.

Gabriel ripped into his guitar solo, his fingers flying across the fretboard in a soaring, wailing plea. It was loud enough to rattle the picture frames in the hallway. It was loud enough to shake the foundation of the house.

As the final, massive orchestral hit echoed through the drywall, the heavy instruments abruptly cut out.

Gwendolyn let her hands drop to the lowest volume on the synthesizer, mimicking the delicate, fragile plinking of Julian’s wind-up toy. The track ended exactly how it began: with the quiet, safe, familiar lullaby of their home, fading slowly into total silence.

For five seconds, nobody in the house breathed.

Down the hall, Marcus hit the heavy, mechanical STOP button on the four-track tape machine. With a loud clack, he popped the plastic cassette tape out of the deck. He walked into the living room and pressed the warm plastic cassette directly into Gabriel’s hand.

"It's rough, and it's bleeding," Marcus said, his chest heaving. "But it's done."

Gabriel looked down at the tape. It wasn't just a song. It was a lifeline.

But as the adrenaline in the room finally crashed, the horrifying reality of the silence rushed back in. Clara dropped her sheet music. Her hands began to shake, and a sharp, ragged sob tore from her throat.

"He's overloading," Clara wept, the flawless control of her voice completely shattering as she looked at her twin sister and Gwendolyn. The agonizing intuition of a best friend was tearing her apart. "It's been hours. He's trapped in a moving car with that monster, and he doesn't have his weighted blanket. He's terrified. He needs us, Maria. He needs the pressure. He needs us to cuddle him right now."

Maria abandoned her glockenspiel instantly, crossing the room and pulling Clara into a fierce, desperate hug. Gwendolyn stepped out from behind the synthesizer and wrapped her arms tightly around both of the identical twins—forming a protective, heavy, three-person embrace that was a cruel, empty echo of the exact sensory hold Julian desperately needed.

Gabriel’s jaw clenched. He looked at Richard Harrison, who was already picking up the kitchen wall phone.

Richard dialed the direct line to his broadcasting control room. He didn't ask for permission; he pulled rank. "This is Harrison," Richard ordered his staff, his voice echoing with corporate, unyielding authority. "Pull the mid-day programming on the flagship rock station immediately. I have a priority track coming in. Clear the airwaves and keep the channel open."

Richard hung up the phone and pointed a sharp finger at Gabriel. "The board is yours. Get there as fast as you can."

Gabriel shoved the cassette tape into the pocket of his denim jacket. He grabbed his car keys off the kitchen counter.

"Gwen, stay here with Mom and the girls," Gabriel ordered, his frontline protector instincts shifting from the microphone to the asphalt. "If the police finally issue the alert, or if they find the car, I need to know immediately."

"Go," Gwendolyn urged, her arms still wrapped securely around the sobbing twins. "Get him on the air, Gabriel."

Gabriel bolted out the front door, the freezing December wind biting at his face. Marcus was right behind him. They sprinted across the frost-covered lawn to Gabriel's battered sedan, throwing themselves into the freezing interior. The engine roared to life, the tires spinning on the frosted driveway before catching traction.

Gabriel slammed the car into drive and tore out of the neighborhood, heading straight for the regional broadcast towers.

He gripped the steering wheel, his knuckles white. The cassette tape burned like a hot coal in his pocket. He didn't care about FCC regulations, corporate playlists, or broadcast fidelity. He just knew that his fourteen-year-old brother was trapped in a moving car somewhere out in the freezing expanse of the state, terrified, overstimulated, and desperately waiting for his family.

Turn on the radio, Tom, Gabriel prayed silently, his foot pressing harder on the accelerator. Turn on the radio, and let him hear us coming.
