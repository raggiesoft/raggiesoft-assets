The spare room was suffocatingly hot, smelling of ozone, dust, and raw, unfiltered panic.

Gabriel stood in the center of the tangled mess of heavy black cables, his solid-body electric guitar strapped tight across his chest. He adjusted the heavy metal stand of the dynamic microphone, pulling it close to his mouth. Across the cramped room, Gwendolyn gave him a sharp, confirming nod from behind her synthesizer.

"Roll it," Gabriel commanded, his voice a low, gravelly rasp.

Marcus hit the record button on the archaic, four-track tape machine they had scavenged from a local pawn shop.

Gwendolyn’s fingers dropped onto the keys, replicating the fragile, pristine melody of Julian's music box. It was the anchor—the sound of their home, designed specifically to reach a boy who was autistic, non-verbal, and currently trapped in a sensory nightmare.

Gabriel closed his eyes. He pictured Julian in the back of Tom's car, miles away on I-64, terrified, freezing, and entirely without his perimeter. Gabriel’s chest heaved. He brought his pick down hard against the steel strings of his guitar, dialing the distortion up to a violent, jagged roar just as Marcus slammed his heavy boot onto the kick drum.

Sarah leaned into her electric cello, dragging her bow across the lowest string with punishing force. It wasn't classical music; it was a heavy, driving riff that hit Gabriel right in the sternum. It was the dark, relentless foundation of a massive, theatrical piece of symphonic rock.

Gabriel leaned into the microphone. He didn't just sing the lyrics; he weaponized them.

"The clock upon the mantel chimes... Reminding me of better times..."

He poured every ounce of his protective, older-brother fury into the vocal. He was the frontline shield, and right now, his voice was the only perimeter he had left to throw over his brother.

"The window pane is frosted white... I'm searching for a sign tonight."

They slammed into the pre-chorus. The strings built the tension, winding tighter and tighter. And then, they hit the drop on the word Candle. The small spare room physically shook from the sheer volume of the amplifiers.

But as they pushed through the chorus and reached the bridge, Gabriel abruptly slapped his palm flat against his guitar strings, killing the sound in a screech of harsh feedback.

Marcus stopped playing, his drumsticks hovering in the air. Sarah lowered her bow, breathing heavily.

"It's not enough," Gabriel growled, running a frustrated, trembling hand through his hair. He looked at the four of them crammed into the tiny space. "It's loud, but it's not massive. It sounds like four teenagers in a bedroom. If this is going to be a beacon, it needs to sound like an army. It needs to command the radio dial."

Gwendolyn stared at her synthesizer, her tactical mind instantly processing the deficit. "The bridge," she said, her voice sharp with realization. "When you sing 'I'm sending out a prayer', my voice isn't enough to answer you. We wrote it for a haunting choir. And we need a glockenspiel to pierce through the heavy bass tones."

"We need an orchestra," Sarah agreed, wiping sweat from her forehead. "We need it to sound like a rock opera."

Gabriel unstrapped his guitar, his jaw setting into absolute stone. The desperation was clawing at his throat, but he channeled it entirely into logistics. The 1996 single wasn't just a song anymore; it was a desperate, musical plea for his brother's life.

"I'm going back to the wall phone," Gabriel announced, turning toward the hallway. He looked at the drummer. "Marcus. Richard Harrison's girls. We need them here now."

"Maria and Clara," Marcus nodded instantly, catching Gabriel's wavelength. "Maria has the brass glockenspiel from the marching band. And Clara has perfect pitch. She basically runs the vocal arrangements for the youth choir."

To anyone else at the high school, the fourteen-year-old identical twins were just Richard Harrison's daughters. But Gabriel knew exactly what they were to his brother. They were Julian's absolute best friends.

Maria was the fierce, physical shield. Clara was the flawless auditory anchor. Because they looked and sounded exactly alike, down to the same dusting of freckles, Julian's easily overwhelmed brain registered them as a perfectly unified constant. Outside of Gwendolyn, Maria and Clara were the only girls in the world allowed into Julian's physical space. When Julian was overloaded, his absolute favorite safe harbor was being sandwiched between the twins on the sofa, anchored by a symmetrical, dual-sided deep-pressure cuddle.

If Julian was out there in the dark, he needed to hear his best friends calling him home.

"Call them," Gabriel ordered, stepping out into the hallway. "Call Richard at his office. Call the high school and get Maria and Clara on the line. I don't care if they're sitting in third period right now. Tell them to walk out. Tell them Julep was taken, and we need every single voice and instrument they can fit into this house."

Gwendolyn stood up from her keyboard, a fierce, desperate fire burning in her eyes.

"Tell them to park on the lawn if they have to," Gwendolyn added, her voice echoing down the hall. "We are going to build a wall of sound so thick that Tom won't be able to turn the dial without hearing us coming."

Gabriel grabbed the plastic receiver of the kitchen phone. He was eighteen, broke, and absolutely terrified. But as he began dialing numbers, preparing to turn their quiet Virginia home into a chaotic, crowded war room, he knew one thing for certain: the Harrison twins were going to drop everything and tear the world apart to save their best friend, and Gabriel was going to get his brother back, no matter how loud he had to scream to do it.
