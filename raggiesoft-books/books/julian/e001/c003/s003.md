Gabriel stood by the frosted front window, his chest tight, watching the driveway. The quiet, residential street in Ewell was suddenly transforming into a chaotic staging ground.

A heavy, reliable station wagon pulled aggressively onto the frost-covered grass of the front lawn, entirely disregarding the driveway. Two battered sedans full of high school band students pulled up right behind it. Car doors slammed in rapid succession.

Richard Harrison stepped out of the station wagon, his expression carved from absolute stone. Right behind him were his fourteen-year-old identical twin daughters. Clara and Maria marched up the walkway in perfect synchronization, their faces flushed from the freezing wind and blazing with a terrifying, fiercely protective rage. Clara was already organizing the sheet music in her hands, while Maria carefully balanced the heavy wooden case holding her marching-band glockenspiel.

Gabriel pulled the front door open, waving them inside. The freezing Virginia wind swept in with them, but the sheer, focused energy they brought instantly warmed the house. They didn't ask questions, and there was no casual small talk. When Gabriel had called Richard's office, the former music therapy teacher hadn't hesitated; he had driven straight to the high school, signed his daughters out of third period, and mobilized the frontline.

"We're here," Richard announced, shedding his heavy coat and dropping it onto the floor. He looked at Gabriel, projecting the quiet, unyielding authority of a man ready to go to war for his students. "Tell us what you need."

"We need a wall of sound," Gabriel said, his voice a low, gravelly rumble. He pointed toward the cramped spare room down the hall, where distorted guitar feedback was already humming like a live wire. "We need Clara's choir for the bridge, and we need Maria's glockenspiel to pierce through the distortion. We are recording a beacon."

Before Richard could respond, a sharp, defeated sob echoed from the kitchen.

Gabriel turned. His mother, Eira, was hanging up the heavy plastic wall phone, her hand trembling so violently she missed the cradle twice before it finally clicked into place.

"Mom?" Gabriel asked, closing the distance between them in three long strides. "Was that the precinct? Did they find the car?"

Eira shook her head, burying her face in her hands. "They haven't even issued the alert yet, Gabriel."

"What?!" Gabriel roared, his protective energy violently flaring. "It's been over two hours! He's non-verbal and he doesn't have his chair! He is critically endangered!"

"It's the bureaucracy," Eira wept, her voice muffled against her palms. "Because Tom is his biological father, the dispatcher said it's technically a civil custody dispute until they can physically verify the court orders. The archives are at the county courthouse. Everything is on paper. A clerk has to pull the physical divorce decree, verify that Tom has zero visitation rights, and get a judge to sign off on the breach before they can legally flag Julian as a kidnapping victim in the state system."

"How long?" Gwendolyn demanded, stepping out of the spare room. Her tactical mind was instantly calculating the horrifying delay.

"They said it could take until mid-afternoon," Eira choked out. "Gabriel... Tom could have him across the state line by then."

Gabriel’s vision swam with red. Mid-afternoon. Julian would be trapped in the back of a car for hours, subjected to unpredictable sensory inputs, unable to shift his own body weight, completely terrified and wondering why his family hadn't come for him.

"Then we don't wait for the police," Gabriel snarled, turning back to the makeshift orchestra crowding his living room. The desperation in his chest hardened into absolute, unyielding steel. "We finish this track right now."

Clara stepped forward, looking exactly like her twin sister but carrying a completely different auditory command. She looked at the hastily scribbled lyrics Gwendolyn had handed her. "Gabriel, even if we record a masterpiece in the next hour... how are you getting it out there? Local radio stations have corporate playlists. They don't just take cassette tapes from teenagers off the street."

"I don't know," Gabriel admitted, his voice cracking with sheer frustration. "I'll break into the broadcast booth if I have to."

"You won't have to break in," Richard Harrison spoke up quietly from the center of the room.

Gabriel looked at him.

Richard stepped forward, his eyes locking onto Eira's devastated face before shifting to Gabriel. "I left teaching to work corporate for a broadcasting group," Richard explained, his voice calm, steady, and entirely absolute. "We own a massive block of FM rock stations across Virginia and down into the Carolinas. And I am the regional program director who controls the live feed."

Gwendolyn’s eyes widened, the tactical puzzle instantly snapping together.

"I know Julian," Richard continued, his voice thick with emotion. "I know how he communicates, and I know how much he relies on his routine. If you record this beacon, I will bypass the corporate playlist. I will push the track live over the airwaves across the entire state. If Tom Cross turns on a radio today, I will make sure this is the only thing he hears."

Gabriel’s heart hammered a frantic, heavy rhythm against his ribs. It wasn't just a desperate shot in the dark anymore. They had a direct, viable pipeline to the state's broadcast towers. And Eira looked at Richard, seeing the exact, unwavering shield her family so desperately needed.

"Set up the microphones," Gabriel commanded, turning to Gwendolyn and the twins. "We push the vocals in the living room, keep the heavy rhythm section in the spare room. Clara, I need your choir to sound like an absolute army answering my prayer on the bridge. Maria, the glockenspiel has to be perfect."

The identical twins shared a single, synchronized nod, their best friend's life entirely on the line.

The house immediately exploded into chaotic, focused motion. Microphones were dragged into the living room, cables snaking across the carpets.

Gabriel strapped his electric guitar back across his chest, the heavy weight of it feeling like armor. He looked at the sheet of lyrics, picturing the freezing, desolate miles of the interstate. He was going to sing so loudly that the sheer force of it would shatter the windows of his father's car.
