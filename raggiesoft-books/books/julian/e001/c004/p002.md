## 12:20 PM • Julian Rowlands — The Hijack

The heavy tires of the sedan crunched over the frozen gravel of the shoulder, throwing a spray of dirty slush as Tom aggressively merged back onto the two-lane West Virginia highway.

He snatched the yellow paper citation from his lap and tossed it violently onto the dashboard. He let out a harsh, ragged breath, his heart hammering against his ribs. It had been entirely too close. The state trooper had shined his heavy flashlight right into the back seat. But Tom had played the part perfectly—the exhausted, patient father dealing with a disabled son having a "bad day."

Because the James City County clerk hadn't pushed the custody breach to the national wire yet, the computer in the trooper's cruiser had returned a completely clean record.

Tom gripped the steering wheel, his knuckles turning white as he accelerated back up to seventy miles an hour, pushing deeper into the isolating, jagged terrain of the Appalachian Mountains.

In the back seat, Julian sat completely rigid. His unmedicated muscles were locked into a terrifying, painful state of spasticity. The freezing draft from the broken heater, combined with the sheer, crushing terror of the traffic stop, was pushing his sensory system to the absolute brink. When the police officer had looked at him, Julian had tried to make a sound, to signal his distress, but his vocal cords had completely frozen in panic.

He was trapped. The coastal flatlands of his home were gone, replaced by towering, skeletal trees and walls of gray rock.

Irritated by the heavy, suffocating silence in the car and the quiet, strained hitching of Julian's breathing, Tom reached a hand out and twisted the volume knob on the dashboard radio.

The speakers crackled to life, fighting through a layer of mountain static. A polished, pre-recorded radio jingle echoed through the cabin.

“You’re locked into the Valley’s Rock... 97.3 Winchester, your Shenandoah connection.”

A generic grunge track started to play, the heavy bass vibrating through the floorboards. Tom settled back into his seat, the noise providing a distraction from the reality of what he had just done.

But less than thirty seconds later, the music abruptly, violently cut out.

It wasn't a fade. It was a hard, digital kill-switch. A sharp pulse of static hissed through the car speakers, followed immediately by five seconds of absolute dead air.

Tom frowned, reaching a hand out to smack the side of the plastic dashboard, assuming the mountain terrain had killed the signal.

Then, a voice pierced the silence. It didn't belong to a polished radio DJ. It was raw, breathless, and trembling with a terrifying, protective fury.

"Julian."

In the back seat, Julian violently gasped. His wide, terrified eyes snapped toward the front dashboard. His chest heaved.

"It's Gabriel," the voice crackled over the FM frequency, bouncing off the Winchester broadcast towers and bleeding directly into the freezing West Virginia air. "Mom is safe. Gwen is safe. I know it's loud right now, and I know you're scared. But you need to listen to me. We are looking for you. We are not going to stop. I need you to listen to this song, Julian. Close your eyes, and hold onto it."

Tom slammed his foot on the brake pedal, the sedan swerving slightly on the icy asphalt.

"What the hell?" Tom hissed, staring at the radio interface in absolute, uncomprehending shock.

A moment later, the pristine, metallic, delicate plinking of a wind-up toy began to play through the cheap car speakers.

It was Wiegenlied. It was the exact melody of the plush music box that sat on Julian’s nightstand in Ewell.

For Julian, the sound was an instant, physical tether. The crushing, suffocating terror of the mountains completely evaporated. The music box was the sound of safety. It was the sound of his brother sitting on the edge of his bed, holding the perimeter.

Julian let out a loud, breathless vocalization of pure recognition. He wasn't lost anymore. They knew where he was.

"The clock upon the mantel chimes..." Gabriel’s deep, dramatic baritone blasted through the speakers, immediately followed by the massive, concussive drop of a heavily distorted electric guitar and a driving, relentless cello.

"Reminding me of better times!"

"How?!" Tom roared, his face flushing a violent, panicked red. He reached out and violently twisted the tuning dial, trying to escape the auditory assault.

The frequency jumped from 97.3 to 98.1.

"...The window pane is frosted white!" Gabriel’s voice continued seamlessly on the new station.

Tom spun the dial again, pushing it to 101.5.

"...I'm searching for a sign tonight!"

Mr. Harrison’s corporate override was absolute. The coastal broadcasting group owned half the frequencies in the Shenandoah Valley, and every single one of them was currently transmitting the exact same desperate, symphonic war cry.

Julian’s hands uncurled. The rigid tension in his legs began to melt. He didn't cover his ears against the loud, distorted guitars, because the noise wasn't chaotic anymore. It was a massive, impenetrable wall of armor built entirely for him.

Tom slammed his fist against the radio, finally hitting the power button and killing the audio completely. The car plunged back into a suffocating, ringing silence.

Tom was breathing heavily, his eyes darting frantically to the rearview mirror. The delusion that he had gotten away with it cleanly was entirely dead. His eighteen-year-old son hadn't just filed a police report; he had hijacked the regional commercial airwaves.

And miles behind them, in a dispatch center across the Virginia state line, the agonizing bureaucratic delay was finally breaking. A county clerk hit Enter on a keyboard, linking the custody breach to the state database.

Tom didn't know it yet as he sped deeper into the West Virginia interior, but the speeding ticket in his lap was no longer his biggest problem. The Critical Missing Child Bulletin had just officially hit the NCIC wire, and the net was rapidly closing.

## 1:15 PM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Escalation

Gabriel’s battered sedan slid into the driveway of the Ewell house, the engine ticking as he cut the ignition. He didn't move immediately. He just sat behind the steering wheel, his hands still gripping the worn plastic rim, listening to the car speakers.

Through the static, the haunting, massive choir of Upon This Winter's Night was echoing across the regional broadcast.

It was surreal. His own raw, desperate prayer was bleeding out of the dashboard, carried by invisible waves into the freezing December sky. In the backseat, Marcus let out a long, heavy exhale, staring at the radio. In the passenger seat, Richard Harrison sat in quiet, unyielding solidarity, the corporate program director having successfully torn up his own network’s rulebook.

"It's out there, Gabriel," Richard said softly, his voice carrying the steady, grounding weight of a father. "We did everything we could do."

Gabriel nodded, a sharp ache settling deep in his chest. He pulled the keys from the ignition, killing the audio, and the three of them stepped out into the biting wind.

When Gabriel pushed through the front door, the atmosphere inside the house had fundamentally shifted. The chaotic, frantic energy of the makeshift recording studio was gone. The instruments were abandoned in the spare room, the amplifiers cooling off.

In the living room, Eira was no longer collapsed on the floor. She was sitting rigidly on the edge of the floral sofa, her hands clasped tightly in her lap. Sitting shoulder-to-shoulder on either side of her were Maria and Clara, the identical twins flanking Julian's mother like a fiercely protective honor guard.

Standing across from them, wearing a heavy wool trench coat and holding a leather-bound notepad, was a county detective. Gwendolyn stood right behind the sofa, her arms crossed, her eyes locked onto the investigator with a cold, calculating intensity.

Gabriel’s heavy boots thudded against the hardwood, drawing the detective's attention.

"Gabriel," Gwendolyn said, her voice tight but remarkably steady. "This is Detective Miller. The county clerk finally processed the paperwork."

Gabriel stepped directly into the living room, his broad shoulders squared, instinctively placing himself between the detective and his family. Richard stepped up right beside him, reinforcing the perimeter.

"Did you find him? We know about the traffic stop," Gabriel demanded.

Detective Miller let out a slow, measured breath, closing his notepad. He looked at the eighteen-year-old boy, recognizing the absolute, volatile protectiveness radiating from him.

"We did," Detective Miller confirmed, his tone carrying the grave, heavy weight of a massive bureaucratic failure. "The state police in West Virginia forwarded the incident report a few minutes ago. The traffic stop occurred in a jurisdiction called Lost City."

Gabriel flinched. Lost City. The name hit his chest like a physical blow. It wasn't just a town; it was a devastating reflection of his brother's reality. Julian was entirely unmoored, dragged deep into the isolating, jagged terrain of the Appalachian Mountains. Clara let out a sharp, devastated breath, her hand instantly finding Maria's and gripping it tight.

"A state trooper pulled your father over for excessive speed," the detective explained, maintaining direct eye contact with Gabriel. "Because the custody breach hadn't hit the national wire yet, the plates came back clean. Your father claimed it was a standard family trip. The trooper wrote the citation and let them merge back onto the highway."

"He looked right at my brother," Gabriel snarled, his voice trembling with a dark, violent fury. "Julian is fourteen. He's completely non-verbal. He has Cerebral Palsy. His muscles lock up when he's terrified! How does a trained officer look at a boy in that condition and just let the car drive away?!"

"Gabriel," Eira whispered, her voice cracking as she reached out.

"He didn't have the context, son," Detective Miller said calmly, refusing to be baited into an argument. "Your father lied, and the system was too slow to catch him. But the system is fully online now."

The detective stepped forward, his expression hardening into absolute, uncompromising authority.

"The moment that citation hit the regional dispatch, it triggered the interstate net," Miller stated. "Your father crossed a state line with a child he has absolutely no legal right to possess. This is no longer a local civil dispute. It is a federal kidnapping."

Gwendolyn’s breath hitched. The tactical shift was monumental.

"We have officially issued a critical missing child bulletin across all neighboring states," the detective continued, laying out the arsenal they were finally deploying. "Every highway patrol unit, every weigh station, and every toll booth from here to the Ohio border has the license plate and the vehicle description. And because Julian is without his manual wheelchair and his required medication, the state police have officially classified him as critically endangered."

Critically endangered. The words echoed in the quiet living room. It was the absolute worst-case scenario, printed in black and white on a police report, but it was also the exact leverage they needed. The authorities were finally treating Julian's disappearance with the sheer, life-or-death urgency it demanded.

"He's in the mountains," Gabriel said, his voice dropping into a hollow, terrified rasp. "It's freezing out there. He doesn't have a coat. He doesn't have his weighted blanket to stop the sensory overloads."

"Which is why we are tearing the highway apart looking for him," Detective Miller promised. "We have units sweeping the major routes leading out of Lost City right now."

The detective offered a small, empathetic nod to Eira and Richard before turning toward the front door. "Stay by the phone. The second we have a visual on that vehicle, I will call you."

As the heavy front door clicked shut behind the investigator, the suffocating reality of the wait settled over the house.

Gabriel looked at Gwendolyn, and then down at Clara and Maria. They had mobilized an army. They had hijacked the regional radio towers. They had forced the federal authorities to launch a massive dragnet.

But as Gabriel sank into the dining chair, staring blindly out the frosted window toward the west, he knew that none of the police cruisers mattered right now.

Julian was alone in the freezing, jagged wilderness of West Virginia. And until the flashing lights found Tom's car, the only thing keeping his little brother tethered to the world was the sound of a fragile music box playing through the radio static.

## 6:00 PM • Eira Rowlands — The Longest Shadow

By six o'clock, the winter sun had completely surrendered, plunging James City County into a bitter, freezing night.

Inside the Cross household, the suffocating tension had settled into the drywall like frost. The living room was crowded, yet entirely devoid of the chaotic, mobilized energy from the morning. The makeshift garrison—Marcus, Sarah, Clara, and Maria—hadn't gone home. They were camped out on the floor and the edges of the armchairs, exhausted but refusing to abandon their post.

The silence in the room was absolute, broken only by the rhythmic, agonizing ticking of the mantel clock and the low hum of a portable radio sitting on the coffee table.

Every ten minutes, the local broadcast interrupted its regular programming. A harsh, electronic tone would pierce the airwaves, followed by the automated, chilling cadence of the Emergency Alert System.

"The Virginia State Police and the West Virginia State Police have issued a Critical Missing Child Bulletin for a critically endangered fourteen-year-old boy..."

Gabriel sat on the floor, his back pressed hard against the base of the sofa where his mother was resting. He had his knees pulled up, his heavy boots scuffing the carpet. His calloused fingers were completely still. He had spent all of his protective, frontline energy screaming into the studio microphone, and now, there was nothing left to do but wait.

The phone on the kitchen wall remained brutally, tauntingly silent.

Detective Miller had promised to call the second they had a visual on Tom’s car. But hours had bled away since the traffic stop in Lost City. Tom was a ghost, swallowed entirely by the jagged, desolate expanse of the Appalachian Mountains in the dark.

"They're going to find him, Gabriel," Marcus said quietly from the armchair, leaning forward and resting his elbows on his knees. "Every state trooper on the eastern seaboard is looking for that license plate right now."

Gabriel gave a slow, hollow nod, staring blankly at the radio. "It's freezing out there, Marcus. If he turns the car off to sleep... Julian doesn't have his winter coat. He can't generate enough body heat if his muscles are locked up."

On the sofa, Eira let out a quiet, shuddering breath, pulling her sweater tighter around her chest.

Gwendolyn was sitting at the dining table, surrounded by a sprawl of physical road maps she had dug out of the glove compartment. Her tactical mind was frantically trying to calculate vectors and travel speeds, desperately trying to predict the unpredictable.

"Mr. Harrison called while you were outside checking the driveway," Maria spoke up softly from the corner, her voice breaking the heavy gloom.

Gabriel lifted his head, his eyes finding the young glockenspiel player. "Did he get fired?"

Maria shook her head, a faint, resilient smile touching her lips. "No. The corporate executives in New York were furious at first. They threatened to pull his broadcasting license for the override. But then the federal bulletin hit the wire, and the precinct publicly confirmed the kidnapping."

"The community rallied," Clara added, leaning against the archway, her voice a flawless, comforting echo of her sister's. "The station's switchboard has been lighting up all afternoon. People aren't mad about the dead air or the rogue broadcast. They're calling in to ask how they can help. Mr. Harrison said the station manager completely backed off. They put Upon This Winter's Night into the official, top-of-the-hour rotation. It's playing on every station they own, completely sanctioned, until Julian comes home."

Gabriel closed his eyes, a profound, crushing weight pressing down on his chest. He had built an auditory fortress. He had unleashed an army. The entire region was standing at his back, projecting their war cry into the freezing night.

But as the mantel clock ticked forward into the seventh hour of the abduction, the terrifying reality remained: the music could only reach Julian if Tom kept the radio on. And right now, Gabriel had absolutely no way of knowing if his brother was listening, or if he was suffering entirely in the dark.
