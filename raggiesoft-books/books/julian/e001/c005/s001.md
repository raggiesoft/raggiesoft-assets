By seven o'clock in the evening, the sun had been dead and gone for hours, plunging the Virginia peninsula into a bitter, freezing dark.

Inside the house in Ewell, the adrenaline that had fueled the frantic recording session and the desperate hijack of the broadcast towers had completely evaporated. In its place was a heavy, suffocating silence, broken only by the low, ambient hiss of the living room radiator and the quiet static of a battery-powered radio sitting on the coffee table.

Gabriel stood by the frosted front window, his forehead pressed lightly against the icy glass. He stared out into the blackness of the driveway, his broad shoulders slumped under the crushing weight of the night. Every time a pair of headlights swept past the neighborhood cross street, his heart would violently seize, praying to see the silhouette of a county police cruiser turning in. But the lights always faded, leaving him back in the dark.

It had been nearly seven hours since the speeding ticket in Lost City, West Virginia. Seven hours of absolute, agonizing dead air. The federal dragnet was active, the highway signs were flashing Julian's description, and their song was looping across the regional stations, but Tom had simply vanished into the jagged, desolate terrain of the Appalachian Mountains.

"Gabriel," Marcus said softly, stepping up beside him. He held out a steaming ceramic mug of plain, black coffee.

Gabriel took the mug, his calloused fingers wrapping around the hot ceramic, though he couldn't feel the warmth. "It's freezing out there, Marcus," Gabriel whispered, his voice cracking into a hollow, devastated rasp. "Julian doesn't have his coat. If Tom pulled off the highway to hide the car... if he turned the engine off..."

"Don't do that to yourself, man," Marcus urged, keeping his voice a low, steady rumble of solidarity. "The state troopers have infrared. They have helicopters. They aren't going to stop looking."

Behind them, the living room was a quiet, makeshift command center of moral support. Nobody had gone home.

Sarah was sitting on the carpet, her back resting against the floral sofa, methodically tuning and re-tuning the strings of her electric cello just to keep her hands moving. On the sofa, Eira was curled into a tight, trembling ball under a heavy knitted blanket. Gwendolyn sat perfectly still right beside her, holding their mother's hand.

Standing near the archway, holding a cup of coffee he hadn't touched, was Richard Harrison. He hadn't returned to the corporate office. He had stayed exactly where he was needed most.

"Did they fire you, Richard?" Gwendolyn asked quietly, her tactical mind finally surfacing from the terrifying void.

Richard offered a tired, unyielding smile. "The corporate executives in New York were furious at first. They threatened to pull my broadcasting license for the override. But then the federal bulletin hit the wire, and the precinct publicly confirmed the kidnapping."

Richard looked down at his two daughters, Clara and Maria, who were sitting cross-legged on the floor near the radiator. "The community rallied. The station's switchboard has been lighting up all afternoon. People aren't mad about the dead air or the rogue broadcast. They're calling in to ask how they can help. The station manager completely backed off. I told them we are playing Upon This Winter's Night at the top of every single hour, right after the emergency police bulletin, and they agreed to sanction it."

"Thank you," Eira whispered, her voice trembling. "Richard, thank you."

As the exhaustion of the day began to set in, Clara and Maria stood up from the floor. They didn't put their coats on to leave. Instead, they walked quietly down the hall toward Julian's bedroom.

They had made their choice. They were spending the night, and as always, they were sleeping in Julian's bed. It was a strict, highly specific boundary in the Rowlands household: outside of Gwendolyn, the identical twins were the only people Julian allowed into his sleeping space. For Julian, falling asleep sandwiched between his two best friends was the ultimate, symmetrical sensory comfort. Tonight, the girls were going to sleep under his weighted blanket, keeping his sanctuary warm and anchored so it would be exactly as he left it when Gabriel finally brought him home.

The battery-powered radio on the coffee table hissed, a low bed of static filling the suffocating tension of the living room. It was the top of the hour.

Gabriel turned away from the window, his eyes locking onto the small plastic speaker.

The syndicated rock track faded out abruptly. There was no upbeat station jingle. When the regional DJ spoke, his voice was stripped of all its usual polished, corporate enthusiasm. It was somber, urgent, and deadly serious.

"We are interrupting our scheduled programming to bring you a federal Critical Missing Child Bulletin," the DJ announced, the weight of the broadcast echoing through the quiet house. "The FBI and state authorities across Virginia and West Virginia are searching for fourteen-year-old Julian Cross. Julian was abducted early this morning from his home in James City County by his non-custodial father, Thomas Cross."

Gabriel’s jaw clenched, his pulse hammering a violent rhythm against his ribs as he heard his brother's legal name pushed out over the commercial airwaves.

"Julian is classified as critically endangered," the DJ continued, reading the NCIC wire verbatim. "He has cerebral palsy, is entirely non-verbal, and requires daily medication which he does not have. He was taken without his winter coat and without his manual wheelchair, leaving him completely unable to assist himself. The suspect is driving a dark blue sedan. Authorities have confirmed the vehicle was last spotted earlier this afternoon crossing the state line into Lost City, West Virginia, heading west into the Appalachian interior."

Eira let out a sharp, ragged sob, hiding her face against Gwendolyn's shoulder. West Virginia. He was so far away from the coastal flatlands.

"If you see this dark blue sedan, do not approach the vehicle. Contact highway patrol or dial 9-1-1 immediately," the DJ instructed. Then, the broadcast took a sudden, deeply personal shift. The DJ took a breath, his voice losing its rigid broadcaster cadence. "Julian’s older brother and sister just delivered a raw cassette tape to our regional control room. They asked us to play it across the entire network, hoping that if that dark blue sedan has its radio on, Julian might hear it. They want him to know that his family is coming for him. So, if you're out there listening in the dark, Julian... this is for you."

Two seconds of absolute, heavy dead air followed.

And then, cutting cleanly through the radio static, the pristine, fragile, metallic plinking of Julian’s wind-up music box spilled out of the speaker.

Gabriel closed his eyes as the delicate notes of Wiegenlied filled the living room, immediately followed by the massive, concussive drop of his own distorted electric guitar and Sarah's relentless cello. Their desperate, symphonic war cry was officially blanketing the mountains.

All he could do was sit in the heavy, agonizing silence with his family, desperately waiting for the phone to ring, praying that the Northern Star they had broadcasted into the dark was bright enough to guide his brother home.
