The dashboard clock glowed a faint, sickly green in the pitch-black cabin of the sedan. It was exactly seven o'clock in the evening, and the rugged, jagged spine of West Virginia had finally given way to the dense, freezing forests of southern Ohio.

Tom stared through the frosted windshield, his hands gripping the steering wheel so tightly his knuckles ached. He kept his right foot feather-light on the accelerator, his eyes darting neurotically between the dark, two-lane highway and the speedometer. The needle hovered exactly on the posted speed limit. Not a single mile-per-hour over.

The yellow traffic citation he had received in Lost City sat crumpled in the center console, a glowing, radioactive reminder of how close he had come to losing everything.

He didn't know about the federal dragnet. He didn't know about the critical missing child bulletin, or that his license plate was now sitting in the NCIC database of every highway patrol cruiser from the East Coast to the Midwest. Because he had violently killed the radio power the exact second Gabriel’s voice had invaded the car, Tom was flying entirely blind, operating on pure, arrogant paranoia.

But if the silence of the dead radio was supposed to bring him peace, it had failed spectacularly.

The back seat was a chorus of absolute, brokenhearted devastation.

Julian was crying. It wasn't a quiet, suppressed whimper; it was a breathless, ragged, continuous sob that shook his entire fragile frame.

For the first four hours of the abduction, the shock and the freezing temperature had forced Julian’s autistic nervous system into a severe, non-responsive freeze state. But the radio broadcast had shattered that dissociative wall. Hearing Gabriel’s desperate promise, and hearing the pristine, delicate plinking of Gwendolyn playing his music box, had violently pulled Julian back to reality.

He didn't just miss his home; he was in a state of profound, agonizing sensory mourning.

His unmedicated muscles were spasming, locking his legs into painful, rigid extensions against the back of Tom's seat. The tears streamed hot and fast down his freezing cheeks. In his mind, he was desperately reaching out for Gwendolyn. When the world became too loud, or when his body fought against him, Gwendolyn was the one who would sit beside his manual wheelchair, wrap her arms securely around his chest, and apply the firm, unyielding deep-pressure cuddle that grounded his nervous system.

He wanted his sister. He wanted the heavy, weighted blanket. He wanted the safe, predictable lights of the holidays.

"I said, be quiet!" Tom roared, slapping his right hand violently against the vinyl of the passenger seat.

Julian flinched, a sharp, terrified whine catching in his throat, but he couldn't stop the tears. His body was completely overloaded.

"Stop crying!" Tom snapped, glaring at his son through the rearview mirror. His face was twisted in an ugly, selfish fury. He didn't see a disabled boy in the throes of a medical and emotional crisis; he just saw an inconvenience that was shredding his nerves. "You're giving me a headache! I'm your father, and you're going to sit there and be quiet!"

Julian squeezed his eyes shut, his hands pulling up to cover his ears, rocking his torso slightly as the sobs continued to tear through his chest.

Tom gritted his teeth, letting out a harsh, frustrated breath. He looked down at the dashboard. The fuel needle was dipping dangerously close to the empty red line.

He couldn't just keep driving. His own bladder was aching, his stomach was completely empty, and basic, irritating logic dictated that the boy in the back seat had the exact same biological needs. He had to stop. But pulling into a brightly lit, populated rest stop felt like walking into a trap. He was terrified of seeing another state trooper.

Through the dark, a tall, flickering sign approached on the right shoulder, advertising a solitary, independent gas station off a desolate rural exit.

"Fine," Tom hissed, hitting his turn signal and taking the exit ramp entirely too slow, paranoid about his speed. "We're stopping. But you are going to dry it up before I open that door, do you understand me? You are not making a scene."

Tom pulled the sedan into the empty, poorly lit lot of the dilapidated gas station, killing the engine under the flickering canopy. He had no idea that the total silence of his car radio was the only thing keeping him ignorant of the massive net rapidly closing in around him.
