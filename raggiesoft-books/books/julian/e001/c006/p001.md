## 12:15 AM • Richard Harrison — The Midnight Relay

The eight-hour drive from the Virginia coast to the jagged, frozen foothills of Athens County, Ohio, was not a road trip. It was a grueling, desperately mobilized convoy.

They took two vehicles. Gabriel, Gwendolyn, and Eira packed into the family’s heavy station wagon, the trunk loaded with Julian’s heavy winter coat, his weighted sensory blanket, and the physical folder of original, court-stamped custody documents.

Following close behind them, its headlights a constant, reassuring presence in the rearview mirror, was Mr. Harrison’s vehicle. The program director hadn’t just hijacked the regional airwaves for them; he had immediately offered to drive his fourteen-year-old identical twin daughters, Clara and Maria, across three state lines the second he heard Julian had been found.

Gabriel understood exactly why they needed to be there. Clara and Maria weren't just girls from the high school music department. They were the only people entirely outside of the Cross family who existed inside Julian’s deep, heavily guarded inner circle. They didn't pity Julian, and they never spoke down to him. They would sit cross-legged on the floor of his room for hours, perfectly content in the silence, communicating with him on a quiet, empathetic frequency that Gabriel still marveled at. Clara's flawless, perfect pitch had been the vocal anchor, and Maria's bright, piercing glockenspiel note had been the star that guided Julian through the static. Together, the identical twins were his ultimate, symmetrical safe harbor, and they absolutely refused to let him wake up in a strange place without them.

Gabriel took the first driving shift.

From midnight until three in the morning, he white-knuckled the steering wheel as the station wagon swallowed the dark, winding miles of the Appalachian passes. The adrenaline that had fueled his frantic recording session was entirely gone, replaced by a hollow, burning exhaustion and the sheer, gravitational pull of his brother’s terror. He kept the dashboard heater blasting, thinking about Julian shivering in the back of a crashed sedan.

At a desolate, brilliantly lit rest area near the West Virginia border, they executed the first relay.

Nobody spoke. Gabriel put the car in park, his muscles stiff and aching as he stepped out into the biting wind. Eira slid into the driver's seat without a word, her face set in a mask of absolute, uncompromising maternal grit.

From three until six in the morning, Eira drove. Gabriel sat in the back seat, staring blankly at the dark tree line rushing past the frost-covered window. Up front, Gwendolyn rode shotgun, a small, battery-powered flashlight clamped between her teeth as her fingers traced the highlighted routes over a sprawling paper map. They didn't listen to the radio. The silence in the car was heavy with the terrifying knowledge that Julian was currently trapped in a sterile hospital room, entirely incapable of understanding why his family wasn't there to hold him.

As the first, weak gray light of dawn began to bleed over the horizon, they crossed the Ohio River.

Gwendolyn took the final shift. She drove with tactical, ruthless precision, pushing the station wagon exactly to the edge of the speed limit down the two-lane rural highways of southern Ohio. Her jaw was locked. She knew she was Julian's primary physical anchor. She knew exactly what a sensory overload felt like for him—the rigid, agonizing muscle spasms, the blinding lights, the terrifying inability to filter out the noise. Every minute she spent behind the wheel was another minute Julian was suffering without his deep-pressure cuddle.

"Next exit," Gwendolyn announced, her voice raspy and dry. "Athens County limits."

Gabriel sat forward, resting his forearms on the back of the front seats. The landscape was unfamiliar and desolate—a mix of dense, skeletal winter forests and faded industrial towns.

At 8:15 AM, the convoy pulled off the highway and navigated through the sleepy, frozen streets of Athens.

## 8:30 AM • Eira Rowlands — The Red Tape

The Athens County Regional Medical Center was a sprawling, brutalist concrete structure sitting on the edge of town.

Gwendolyn threw the station wagon into park in the visitor lot, aggressively cutting the engine. Mr. Harrison pulled in right beside them.

The doors opened simultaneously. Maria and Clara stepped out of their father's car, clutching their heavy winter coats tightly around themselves. The identical twins looked exhausted, pale from the overnight drive, but their eyes were fiercely focused. They fell into step right beside Gabriel as the group marched rapidly toward the glowing red letters of the Emergency entrance.

The automatic glass doors slid open, blasting them with the sharp, clinical smell of antiseptic and floor wax.

Gabriel immediately hated the building. It was entirely too bright. The fluorescent lights buzzed with a high-pitched, electrical hum, and the lobby echoed with the chaotic noise of rolling stretchers, beeping monitors, and ringing phones. It was the absolute worst possible environment for a boy with severe cerebral palsy and autism.

Eira marched directly up to the triage desk. The receptionist, a tired-looking woman in faded scrubs, looked up.

"My name is Eira Cross," she stated, her voice carrying the unyielding authority of a mother who had just driven eight hours through the night. She slammed the heavy, manila folder of legal documents onto the counter. "My son, Julian Cross, was brought here last night by the State Highway Patrol. He was the victim of a federal abduction. I am his sole legal guardian, and I need to see him right now."

The receptionist’s eyes widened slightly in recognition. "Mrs. Cross. Please, wait right here. I need to page the county social worker."

"I don't need a social worker," Eira countered, her voice dropping into a dangerous, icy register. "I need my son's room number."

"Ma'am, because he crossed state lines with his biological father, he is technically in the temporary emergency custody of Ohio Child Protective Services," the receptionist explained apologetically, picking up her desk phone. "It's standard legal protocol. I'm paging the caseworker assigned to him now. She's been with him all night."

Gabriel felt his chest tighten, his protective frontline energy violently flaring. He stepped up beside his mother, his broad shoulders squared.

A moment later, a set of heavy double doors down the hall pushed open. A woman in a wrinkled professional blouse and a security badge hurried into the lobby. She looked incredibly stressed, holding a clipboard tightly to her chest.

"Mrs. Cross?" the social worker asked, quickly closing the distance. "I'm Brenda, the county caseworker assigned to Julian."

"Where is he?" Gwendolyn demanded, stepping out from behind Gabriel. "Is he hurt?"

"He has some deep bruising from the seatbelt, and a mild concussion from the airbag deployment, but no broken bones," Brenda explained rapidly, keeping her voice low. "But... we are having a very difficult time, Mrs. Cross. He has been screaming and crying non-stop for hours. He is completely uncooperative. We've been trying to ask him what hurts, to get him to just talk to us, but he absolutely won't answer. Every time anyone approaches the bed, his muscles lock up completely. The doctors are talking about administering a heavy chemical sedative just to stop the spasticity so they can run an MRI."

Gwendolyn’s breath hitched, a horrifying realization dawning on her. "You're trying to make him talk?" she asked, her voice trembling with absolute outrage. "He doesn't have his AAC tablet! He has Cerebral Palsy and he is severely autistic. He is entirely non-verbal!"

Gabriel felt his heart shatter. He knew exactly what was happening in Trauma Room Four. Julian wasn't just crying from the physical pain. In his mind, he was screaming in frantic, desperate Cymraeg, begging for his identical twin anchors to come and save him. But without his communication device, the hospital just saw a combative, uncooperative teenager who was refusing to follow directions.

"Absolutely not!" Gwendolyn snapped, the tactical, calculating part of her brain instantly shifting into absolute command. "Do not sedate him. It's a sensory meltdown. The lights in his room are too bright, the monitors are too loud, and you're surrounding him with strangers who are demanding he speak when he physically cannot. He doesn't need a sedative. He needs his anchors."

Brenda blinked, taken aback by the fierce, absolute certainty of the eighteen-year-old girl. "His anchors?"

"Us," Clara and Maria stated simultaneously, stepping forward. Their flawless, identical voices rang with an absolute, uncompromising clarity that demanded immediate attention.

"Take us to his room," Gwendolyn ordered, grabbing the heavy weighted blanket from Gabriel's arms. "Right now."

Brenda looked at the legal folder sitting on the desk, then at the exhausted, uncompromising faces of the Cross family and the two fourteen-year-old identical girls standing fiercely beside them.

"Follow me," Brenda said, swiping her badge against the secure double doors. "He's in Trauma Room Four."
