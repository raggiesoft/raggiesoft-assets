The squeak of the manual wheelchair tires against the highly polished hospital linoleum was the best sound Gabriel had ever heard.

It was a steady, mechanical rhythm of forward momentum. They were leaving.

Gabriel walked behind the chair, his large, calloused hands resting easily on the rubber push-grips. The heavy, volatile frontline energy that had possessed him for the last twenty-six hours was finally beginning to bleed out of his shoulders. He didn't need to be a human shield right now, because the perimeter in front of him was absolutely unbreakable.

Julian sat in his chair, wearing his own heavy winter coat and wrapped tightly in his weighted blanket. His face was pale and exhausted, but the absolute, primal terror was gone. He reached his left hand out, wrapping his fingers tightly around Clara’s hand. He reached his right hand out, holding firmly onto Maria’s.

Clara and Maria walked in perfect tandem on either side of the wheelchair, matching Gabriel’s pace. They didn't mind the awkward angle of walking while holding hands with someone in a moving chair. For Julian, the continuous, symmetrical deep-pressure squeeze of the identical twins' grips was the ultimate tactile anchor. It was his proof that he wasn't alone in the dark anymore.

A few paces ahead, Eira, Gwendolyn, and Mr. Harrison led the way toward the main lobby, the heavy glass of the emergency exit doors finally in sight.

But as they approached the triage desk, the heavy double doors of the surgical ward swung open. Trooper Vance stepped out, his expression grim and exhausted, his campaign hat held respectfully in his hands. Beside him was a surgeon in green scrubs.

"Ms. Rowlands," Vance called out softly, using the name Eira had declared just twenty minutes prior.

Eira stopped, instinctively turning her body to block the hallway, shielding her children from whatever complication was coming next. Gabriel’s hands tightened on the push-grips.

"Is there a problem with the release paperwork?" Eira asked, her tone flat and uncompromising.

"No, ma'am," Vance assured her quickly. He looked at the surgeon, offering a slight nod.

The surgeon stepped forward, clasping his hands in front of him. "Ms. Rowlands, I am the trauma surgeon who attended to Thomas Cross when he was brought in from the crash site."

Gabriel felt the air in the hallway turn to ice. He looked down at Julian. Julian’s grip on Clara and Maria’s hands visibly tightened, his shoulders pulling up toward his ears at the mention of the monster's name.

"The impact with the concrete retaining wall caused the steering column to collapse completely inward," the surgeon explained, his voice maintaining a quiet, clinical detachment. "He suffered massive internal trauma and catastrophic hemorrhaging. We did everything we could in the operating room, but the damage was simply too severe."

The surgeon paused, letting the heavy, sterile silence of the hospital hallway hold the weight of his next words.

"He succumbed to his injuries a few minutes ago. I'm sorry for your loss."

Gabriel stopped breathing.

He stared at the surgeon, the words echoing in his mind. He succumbed. Gabriel had spent his entire life waiting for the other shoe to drop. He had spent his childhood learning to read the heavy, aggressive footsteps in the hallway, bracing himself for the violent, unpredictable explosions of a selfish, cruel man. Even after the divorce, Tom had always been a shadow lurking at the edge of their lives, a constant, low-level threat that required Gabriel to keep his guard up.

But the shadow was gone.

The monster who had ripped a disabled fourteen-year-old boy out of his safe, predictable world and dragged him into the freezing mountains was dead. He could never show up at the house in Ewell again. He could never file another frivolous custody motion. He could never, ever hurt them again.

Gabriel looked at Gwendolyn. She let out a long, shuddering exhale, a single tear of absolute, staggering relief escaping down her cheek.

On the right side of the chair, Maria leaned down slightly. "He's gone, Julian," she whispered gently.

On the left side, Clara leaned in, pressing a soft kiss to his temple. "You're safe," Clara echoed, her flawless pitch wrapping around him like a warm blanket.

Julian didn't cry. He didn't type on his AAC device. He just let his shoulders drop, completely releasing the residual tension he had been carrying.

Eira stood perfectly still. She didn't offer a polite, socially acceptable performance of grief. She didn't cry for the man who had terrorized her family. She just looked at the surgeon with eyes made of absolute steel.

"I see," Eira said smoothly. "What is the procedure now?"

"The county coroner will take possession of the remains," the surgeon explained, slightly taken aback by the family's stoic, tearless reception of the news. "Because you are his legal next of kin, the hospital administration will need you to sign the release forms to claim the body and arrange for a funeral home to coordinate the transfer."

Eira didn't even blink.

"I am not his next of kin," Eira stated, her voice echoing with absolute, glacial finality. "We are the Rowlands family. Thomas Cross has no family here. We will not be claiming the body. We will not be making any arrangements."

The surgeon looked momentarily stunned. "Ms. Rowlands, if you refuse to claim the remains, he becomes a ward of the state. He will be buried by the county in an unmarked, indigent grave."

"Then let the county bury him," Eira replied without a fraction of hesitation. She stepped forward, closing the distance between herself and the surgeon. "The only piece of paperwork I want from this hospital is a certified copy of his death certificate. I will need it for the family court in Virginia to prove, definitively, that he is dead and can never make another legal claim on my children. And Doctor? Save your condolences. I am not sorry."

She turned her back on the surgeon and the triage desk, looking at Gabriel.

"Push the chair, Gabriel. We're going home."

Gabriel felt a massive, triumphant grin break across his exhausted face. His mother was a force of nature.

"Yes, ma'am," Gabriel said.

He pushed the manual wheelchair forward. The automatic glass doors slid open with a quiet mechanical hum.

The freezing Ohio morning air hit them instantly, sharp and biting, but for the first time in twenty-six hours, Gabriel wasn't cold. He stepped out onto the concrete pavement, flanked by his mother, his sister, and the identical twins who had helped light the beacon in the dark.

Gabriel looked down at his brother, safely wrapped in his coat and blanket, holding tightly to his best friends as they moved forward into the sunlight. The long dark was officially over.
