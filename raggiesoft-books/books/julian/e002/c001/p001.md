## 2:00 PM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Echo

The house in Ewell had finally, fundamentally, exhaled.

It had been forty-eight hours since the wheelchair van pulled into the driveway, carrying the Rowlands family out of the freezing Ohio nightmare and back into the coastal Virginia flatlands. The heavy, suffocating dread that had frosted the windows and silenced the halls was entirely gone. In its place was a fierce, protective warmth.

In the living room, the radiator hummed its familiar, ambient rhythm. Julian was stationed perfectly in the center of the sofa, wrapped so thoroughly in his heavy weighted blanket that only his head and his hands were visible. His AAC device rested securely on his lap. The dark purple bruising along his collarbone was still visible, but the terrifying, rigid spasticity in his legs had completely melted away the second he crossed the threshold of his home.

He was safe. The perimeter was absolute.

From the spare room down the hall, the muffled, driving rhythm of a bass guitar and the dark, sweeping notes of an electric cello reverberated through the drywall.

Gabriel, Sarah, and Marcus were packed into the makeshift recording space. They weren't frantically screaming into a cheap microphone anymore; they were methodically, carefully fulfilling the promise Gabriel had made in Trauma Room 4. They were polishing the mix.

Sitting cross-legged on the floor just outside the spare room’s open door, safely away from the loudest amplifiers but perfectly in the line of sight, were Gwendolyn and Maria. Maria had her glockenspiel resting across her knees, striking the metal keys with a soft, bright precision, while Gwendolyn scribbled tempo adjustments into a spiral-bound notebook.

They were officially The Winter Palace. It was the name Gwendolyn had quietly written at the top of the lyric sheet days ago, a tribute to the safe, illuminated fortress they had built for Julian.

The heavy brass knocker on the front door suddenly clacked twice.

Eira emerged from the kitchen, wiping her hands on a dish towel. She didn't flinch at the sound of the door anymore. The ghost of Thomas Cross was buried in a county file hundreds of miles away.

She pulled the door open, a genuine, welcoming smile breaking across her face. "Mr. Harrison. Come in out of the wind."

The music therapy teacher stepped into the foyer, unwrapping a thick wool scarf from his neck. He looked entirely different than the fiercely focused program director who had hijacked the regional control board on Wednesday. Today, he was practically vibrating with a restless, electric energy. He carried a sleek, black leather portfolio under his arm.

"Eira," Mr. Harrison said, his voice dropping into a conspiratorial, excited register. "Is Gabriel in the back? I need to talk to the band."

Eira raised an eyebrow, gesturing down the hall. "They're fixing the bass line for Julian. Go on back."

Mr. Harrison walked down the hallway, leaning his head into the spare room. "Hold the track, guys. Kill the amps."

Gabriel hit the heavy metal switch on his amplifier, cutting the distorted guitar with a sharp pop. Marcus silenced the bass strings with his palm, and Sarah lowered her cello bow. Gwendolyn and Maria looked up from the hallway floor.

"What's going on, Mr. Harrison?" Gabriel asked, wiping a layer of sweat from his forehead. "Did the station executives finally fire you?"

"Quite the opposite, Gabriel," Mr. Harrison laughed, stepping fully into the room and opening his leather portfolio. "The corporate office is currently pretending they sanctioned the broadcast from the very beginning. The community response has been staggering."

Mr. Harrison pulled out a stack of pink message slips, holding them up like a winning lottery ticket.

"When we pushed that raw cassette tape out over the regional repeaters, we didn't just blanket the mountains," Mr. Harrison explained, his eyes shining with absolute pride. "We blanketed the entire mid-Atlantic coastal grid. And people didn't just hear the critical missing child bulletin. They heard the music. They heard a massive, cinematic rock anthem with a classical cello and a glockenspiel, completely shredding the commercial airwaves."

Gwendolyn stood up, her tactical mind instantly catching the shift in his tone. "Who called the station?"

Mr. Harrison looked directly at Gwendolyn, recognizing the brilliant, calculating composer who had actually built the song from the ground up.

"An Artists and Repertoire executive from an independent label up in Richmond," Mr. Harrison said, dropping the bombshell with absolute precision. "He was driving down the interstate when the override hit. He called the station manager yesterday morning. He doesn't just want to know who hijacked the signal. He wants to know who wrote the track."

Gabriel stared at his former teacher, his jaw dropping slightly. The heavy, calloused fingers of his left hand hovered entirely frozen over the fretboard of his guitar.

"A record label," Gabriel repeated, the words sounding completely alien in the cramped, foam-lined spare room. "They want to meet us?"

"He wants to meet The Winter Palace," Mr. Harrison corrected, tapping the pink slips. "He's driving down to the peninsula on Tuesday. He wants to sit down, hear the polished mix, and talk about a development deal. You didn't just build a rescue beacon, Gabriel. You built a hit."

From the living room, a sharp, digitized, synthetic voice suddenly barked through the hallway.

<aac>TOLD YOU IT WAS GOOD.</aac> Gabriel let out a loud, breathless laugh, the sheer, staggering reality of the moment finally crashing over him. He looked at Sarah, who was beaming behind her cello, and Marcus, who was spinning a drumstick in pure shock. Then, Gabriel looked down at Gwendolyn and Maria.

They had fought a monster in the dark, and they had won. And now, the rest of the world was listening.

"Tuesday," Gabriel said, a fierce, confident spark igniting in his chest. He reached over and flipped the power switch on his amplifier back on, the heavy electric hum filling the room once again. "Alright, Palace. We have three days to get this mix perfect for the boss."

## 4:00 PM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The Hearth

The living room of the house in Ewell had been completely reconfigured. The armchairs were pushed back, and the heavy coffee table was shifted to the side, opening up the sightlines across the entire first floor.

Nobody was willing to let Julian out of their direct line of sight. The terrifying, agonizing hours he had spent trapped in the freezing Appalachian mountains had fundamentally altered the family's perimeter; the physical walls of the house weren't enough anymore. They needed to see him breathing.

Julian was stationed safely in the dead center of the floral sofa. He was buried under the heavy, comforting weight of his sensory blanket, his muscles completely relaxed in the warmth of the radiator. Tucked securely against his right side, sharing the warmth of the heavy blanket, was Maria.

She had her glockenspiel case resting on the floor nearby, but right now, her only focus was her best friend. Since they were seven years old, Maria had existed in a space entirely her own within Julian’s heavily guarded inner circle. She didn't just understand his non-verbal cues; she anticipated them. She was the only person outside of his immediate family who had earned the absolute trust of both Eira and Mr. Harrison to sleep in the same bed as Julian. When the night terrors or sensory overloads hit, her quiet, steady presence and deep-pressure holds were as vital as his medication.

A few feet away at the heavy wooden dining table, Eira sat surrounded by a sprawling spread of legal documents. Mr. Harrison sat across from her, reading glasses perched on his nose, methodically helping her sort through the bureaucratic red tape of completely erasing Thomas Cross from their lives.

"<lang="cy" trans="The forms are all here, Richard,">Mae'r ffurflenni i gyd yma, Richard,</lang>" Eira said, her voice dropping into the fluid, rhythmic cadence of Welsh. She tapped a pen against the top page. "<lang="cy" trans="I will file on Monday to return to Rowlands, and to change Julian's name. Tom is dead. There is nothing to stop me now.">Fe wna i ffeilio ddydd Llun i ddychwelyd i Rowlands, ac i newid enw Julian. Mae Tom wedi marw. Does dim byd i'm rhwystro i nawr.</lang>"

"<lang="cy" trans="It's about time, Eira,">Roedd hi'n hen bryd, Eira,</lang>" Mr. Harrison replied flawlessly in the same language, handing her a highlighted affidavit.

The fact that the music therapy teacher and his daughter spoke Welsh and Québécois French so perfectly was a testament to the profound, chosen-family bond they shared. When Maria and Julian became inseparable at age seven, the Harrisons hadn't just welcomed Julian; they had fully embraced the family's heritage, eagerly learning the languages.

It was a stark, bitter contrast to Tom. Tom had violently refused to learn a single word of Welsh or French, completely rejecting the cultural bedrock of Eira's family and the extended relatives in Stanstead, Québec. His stubborn, xenophobic refusal had been one of the first, heaviest wedges that ultimately shattered their marriage.

Gabriel leaned against the archway of the kitchen, nursing a mug of coffee. He hadn't taken his eyes off the sofa, but he was tracking the conversation at the table perfectly.

"<lang="cy" trans="We are going with you on Monday, Mom,">Rydyn ni'n mynd gyda chi ddydd Llun, Mam,</lang>" Gabriel rumbled in Welsh, his deep voice carrying a fierce, uncompromising loyalty. He looked at his twin sister, who was sitting cross-legged on the carpet near the sofa, sorting through their chaotic studio notes. "<lang="cy" trans="We are dropping the name Cross too. We are all Rowlands now.">Rydyn ni'n gollwng yr enw Cross hefyd. Rydyn ni i gyd yn Rowlands nawr.</lang>"

Eira looked up from the paperwork, a fiercely proud, exhausted smile breaking across her face. "<lang="cy" trans="All of us,">I gyd ohonom,</lang>" she agreed softly.

Julian let out a happy, rhythmic vocalization from the sofa, his head resting comfortably against Maria's shoulder. He didn't need his AAC device to communicate his absolute approval of the plan.

Gabriel smiled, taking a sip of his coffee. The suffocating weight of the abduction was finally giving way to the massive, impossible future Vanguard Horizon had just laid at their feet. His mind started spinning with the logistics of the multi-million dollar recording contract.

"You know," Gabriel started, seamlessly switching back to Welsh as he looked at Gwendolyn and Maria. "<lang="cy" trans="We should record the song in Welsh for the album.">Dylen ni recordio'r gân yn Gymraeg i'r albwm.</lang>" "If Elias wants a massive, theatrical winter epic, a Welsh choir on the bridge would sound absolutely incredible."

Gwendolyn didn't miss a beat. Her tactical mind instantly expanded the perimeter of the idea, effortlessly pivoting the linguistic gears.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="We should record it in Welsh, yes,">On devrait l'enregistrer en gallois, oui,</lang>" Gwendolyn replied in crisp Québécois French, her eyes lighting up with brilliant, composer-level inspiration. "<lang="fr-CA" trans="But why not a French version too?">Mais pourquoi pas une version en français aussi?</lang>"

Gabriel’s eyes widened, the sheer scale of the concept hitting him all at once. An English release for the radio, a Welsh release honoring their mother's roots, and a Québécois French release for their family up in Stanstead, Québec.

"Yes," Gabriel agreed, switching back to English so the sheer enthusiasm could ring out clearly across the room. "That is a brilliant idea, Gwen. We don't just give them the English track. Let's share all three of our languages with the world."

On the sofa, Maria translated the rapid-fire idea to Julian with a gentle squeeze of his hand. Julian’s face lit up. He reached out from under the heavy blanket, aggressively tapping the armrest of the sofa in a drumbeat rhythm.

The dark road in West Virginia was entirely behind them. They were safe, they were together, and The Winter Palace was officially preparing to go global.

## 6:00 PM • Eira Rowlands — The Perimeter Holds

By six o'clock, the frantic, mobilized energy of the afternoon had completely burned off, leaving behind the heavy, bone-deep exhaustion of a family that had spent the last three days running on pure adrenaline.

Outside, the freezing Virginia wind rattled the windowpanes, but inside the Ewell house, the atmosphere was incredibly subdued.

On the floral sofa, the reality of Julian’s trauma was manifesting in a quiet, desperate need for physical grounding. He was terrified. The logical part of his brain knew he was home, but his autistic nervous system was still completely shattered by the memory of the isolating, freezing back seat of Tom's car.

Julian had his arms wrapped tightly around Maria's waist, his face buried against her shoulder in a continuous, unyielding hold. He refused to let her go. He was functioning under the terrifying, subconscious logic that if he broke physical contact with his anchor, the perimeter would collapse and the nightmare would start all over again.

Maria sat perfectly still, her own arms wrapped securely around Julian’s back. She was only fourteen, but she possessed an endless, patient empathy. She hadn't complained once about her arm falling asleep or needing a break.

But Gwendolyn’s tactical eyes missed nothing. She knew Maria was exhausted.

Gwendolyn set her notebook down on the coffee table and moved quietly to the sofa. She didn't try to peel Julian's hands away—that would only trigger a panic response. Instead, she knelt directly in front of him, bringing herself into his line of sight.

" Julep, " Gwendolyn whispered softly, placing her hands firmly over his. "You're safe. But Maria needs to stretch her arms, okay? I'm tagging in."

Julian let out a sharp, distressed whine, his grip tightening defensively on Maria’s sweater.

"I've got you," Gwendolyn promised, projecting absolute, immovable authority. She leaned forward, sliding her arms around his chest the exact moment Maria gently exhaled and leaned back.

It was a flawless, synchronized anchor swap. Julian transferred his desperate grip entirely to his older sister, burying his face into Gwendolyn’s neck. Gwendolyn absorbed his weight effortlessly, applying the firm, deep-pressure cuddle he desperately needed.

Maria rubbed her tired shoulders, offering Gwendolyn a small, grateful smile. Julian reached out with one trembling hand, refusing to completely sever the connection, and grasped the fabric of Maria's sleeve. Maria simply covered his hand with hers, silently promising she wasn't going anywhere.

At the dining table, Eira watched the exchange, a profound, aching lump forming in her throat. She looked down at the legal paperwork, her vision blurring. She was the mother. She was supposed to fix this, but she was so incredibly tired she could barely keep her eyes open.

A sharp, digitized BEEP echoed from the sofa. Julian had managed to pull his AAC device onto his lap with his free hand.

<aac>HUNGRY.</aac> the robotic voice announced.

"Okay, buddy," Gabriel said, stepping out of the hallway and leaning against the archway. "What do you want? Name it, and I'll go pick it up."

Julian’s fingers moved across the keys.

<aac>POUTINE.</aac>

Eira closed her eyes, letting out a soft, heartbroken sigh. Poutine was the ultimate comfort food—the taste of their extended family in Stanstead, Québec. It was exactly what his nervous system was craving: warm, heavy, and familiar.

"Julian, sweetie," Eira said, her voice cracking with exhaustion as she looked at her youngest son. "I'm so sorry. We can't get poutine here. We can't get the proper squeaky cheese curds in the States, and regular melted cheese isn't the same. You know that."

Julian’s shoulders slumped. For a boy whose world had just been violently upended, the denial of a safe, familiar comfort food was a heavy blow. He looked down at his lap, a quiet, disappointed vocalization escaping his throat.

But then, he slowly typed on his board.

<aac>I UNDERSTAND. IT IS OKAY.</aac>

Eira covered her face with her hands, the sheer, sweet compliance of her traumatized son finally breaking her composure.

Richard Harrison immediately stood up from the dining table. He didn't offer empty platitudes, and he didn't ask Eira what she wanted to do. He simply stepped over to her chair, placing a warm, supportive hand on her shoulder.

"Let me take the kitchen, Eira," Richard said, his voice a low, steady rumble of domestic authority. "You are running on fumes. Sit here and rest."

"Richard, you've done enough," Eira protested weakly, though she made no move to stand. "You hijacked a radio station for us. You shouldn't have to cook."

"I'm not a guest, Eira," Richard countered gently, offering a small, reassuring smile. "I'm making grilled cheese and heavy tomato soup. It's not poutine, but it's warm, it's heavy, and it will ground him."

He turned and walked into the kitchen, immediately pulling cast iron pans from the cabinets with the quiet efficiency of a man completely at home.

Eira watched him work for a moment, listening to the comforting sound of butter sizzling in a hot pan. She turned her head back toward the living room. Julian was still clinging tightly to Gwendolyn, his eyes heavy with exhaustion, but his hand was still firmly gripping Maria’s sleeve. If Maria moved even an inch, Julian's breathing would hitch in panic.

Eira realized, with absolute clarity, that the perimeter had permanently expanded. Gabriel and Gwendolyn were the shields, but Richard and Maria were the mortar holding the bricks together.

"Richard," Eira called out softly, her voice carrying across the open floor plan.

Richard paused, looking over his shoulder from the stove. "Yes, Eira?"

"Don't pack your things tonight," Eira requested. It wasn't just an offer; it was a deeply vulnerable plea from a mother who needed reinforcements. She looked at Maria, who was gently tracing circles on the back of Julian's hand. "Julian is too scared. He needs her right now. And I need you. Please, stay with us for a while."

Richard didn't hesitate. He looked at Eira, recognizing the profound trust she was placing in him and his daughter.

"We aren't going anywhere, Eira," Richard promised quietly, turning back to the stove. "We'll stay as long as you need us."

On the sofa, Gabriel met Gwendolyn's eyes. The frontline was secure, the garrison was fully staffed, and for the first time in three days, the house in Ewell finally felt like a home again.
