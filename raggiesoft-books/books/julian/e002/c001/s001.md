The house in Ewell had finally, fundamentally, exhaled.

It had been forty-eight hours since the wheelchair van pulled into the driveway, carrying the Rowlands family out of the freezing Ohio nightmare and back into the coastal Virginia flatlands. The heavy, suffocating dread that had frosted the windows and silenced the halls was entirely gone. In its place was a fierce, protective warmth.

In the living room, the radiator hummed its familiar, ambient rhythm. Julian was stationed perfectly in the center of the sofa, wrapped so thoroughly in his heavy weighted blanket that only his head and his hands were visible. His AAC device rested securely on his lap. The dark purple bruising along his collarbone was still visible, but the terrifying, rigid spasticity in his legs had completely melted away the second he crossed the threshold of his home.

He was safe. The perimeter was absolute.

From the spare room down the hall, the muffled, driving rhythm of a bass guitar and the dark, sweeping notes of an electric cello reverberated through the drywall.

Gabriel, Sarah, and Marcus were packed into the makeshift recording space. They weren't frantically screaming into a cheap microphone anymore; they were methodically, carefully fulfilling the promise Gabriel had made in Trauma Room 4. They were polishing the mix.

Sitting cross-legged on the floor just outside the spare room’s open door, safely away from the loudest amplifiers but perfectly in the line of sight, were Gwendolyn and Maria. Maria had her glockenspiel resting across her knees, striking the metal keys with a soft, bright precision, while Gwendolyn scribbled tempo adjustments into a spiral-bound notebook.

They were officially The Winter Palace. It was the name Gwendolyn had quietly written at the top of the lyric sheet days ago, a tribute to the safe, illuminated fortress they had built for Julian.

The heavy brass knocker on the front door suddenly clacked twice.

Eira emerged from the kitchen, wiping her hands on a dish towel. She didn't flinch at the sound of the door anymore. The ghost of Thomas Cross was buried in a county file hundreds of miles away.

She pulled the door open, a genuine, welcoming smile breaking across her face. "Mr. Harrison. Come in out of the wind."

The music therapy teacher stepped into the foyer, unwrapping a thick wool scarf from his neck. He looked entirely different than the fiercely focused program director who had hijacked the regional control board on Wednesday. Today, he was practically vibrating with a restless, electric energy. He carried a sleek, black leather portfolio under his arm.

"Eira," Mr. Harrison said, his voice dropping into a conspiratorial, excited register. "Is Gabriel in the back? I need to talk to the band."

Eira raised an eyebrow, gesturing down the hall. "They're fixing the bass line for Julian. Go on back."

Mr. Harrison walked down the hallway, leaning his head into the spare room. "Hold the track, guys. Kill the amps."

Gabriel hit the heavy metal switch on his amplifier, cutting the distorted guitar with a sharp pop. Marcus silenced the bass strings with his palm, and Sarah lowered her cello bow. Gwendolyn and Maria looked up from the hallway floor.

"What's going on, Mr. Harrison?" Gabriel asked, wiping a layer of sweat from his forehead. "Did the station executives finally fire you?"

"Quite the opposite, Gabriel," Mr. Harrison laughed, stepping fully into the room and opening his leather portfolio. "The corporate office is currently pretending they sanctioned the broadcast from the very beginning. The community response has been staggering."

Mr. Harrison pulled out a stack of pink message slips, holding them up like a winning lottery ticket.

"When we pushed that raw cassette tape out over the regional repeaters, we didn't just blanket the mountains," Mr. Harrison explained, his eyes shining with absolute pride. "We blanketed the entire mid-Atlantic coastal grid. And people didn't just hear the critical missing child bulletin. They heard the music. They heard a massive, cinematic rock anthem with a classical cello and a glockenspiel, completely shredding the commercial airwaves."

Gwendolyn stood up, her tactical mind instantly catching the shift in his tone. "Who called the station?"

Mr. Harrison looked directly at Gwendolyn, recognizing the brilliant, calculating composer who had actually built the song from the ground up.

"An Artists and Repertoire executive from an independent label up in Richmond," Mr. Harrison said, dropping the bombshell with absolute precision. "He was driving down the interstate when the override hit. He called the station manager yesterday morning. He doesn't just want to know who hijacked the signal. He wants to know who wrote the track."

Gabriel stared at his former teacher, his jaw dropping slightly. The heavy, calloused fingers of his left hand hovered entirely frozen over the fretboard of his guitar.

"A record label," Gabriel repeated, the words sounding completely alien in the cramped, foam-lined spare room. "They want to meet us?"

"He wants to meet The Winter Palace," Mr. Harrison corrected, tapping the pink slips. "He's driving down to the peninsula on Tuesday. He wants to sit down, hear the polished mix, and talk about a development deal. You didn't just build a rescue beacon, Gabriel. You built a hit."

From the living room, a sharp, digitized, synthetic voice suddenly barked through the hallway.

<aac>TOLD YOU IT WAS GOOD.</aac> Gabriel let out a loud, breathless laugh, the sheer, staggering reality of the moment finally crashing over him. He looked at Sarah, who was beaming behind her cello, and Marcus, who was spinning a drumstick in pure shock. Then, Gabriel looked down at Gwendolyn and Maria.

They had fought a monster in the dark, and they had won. And now, the rest of the world was listening.

"Tuesday," Gabriel said, a fierce, confident spark igniting in his chest. He reached over and flipped the power switch on his amplifier back on, the heavy electric hum filling the room once again. "Alright, Palace. We have three days to get this mix perfect for the boss."
