## 9:00 AM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Sunday Vultures

Sunday morning in the Ewell house was a profound, quiet sanctuary.

The heavy winter sun filtered softly through the frosted front windowpanes, casting long, peaceful shadows across the living room carpet. For the first time in four days, nobody was running on adrenaline.

Julian was still asleep. He and Maria had completely commandeered the queen-sized bed down the hall. After the terror of the dark the night before, the house was operating on a strictly enforced "do not disturb" policy until Julian decided he was ready to wake up.

At the dining table, Eira was savoring the absolute, mundane luxury of a silent Sunday morning. Spread out before her was the massive Sunday edition of The Peninsula Herald. She had a pair of scissors in her hand, methodically clipping her way through the brightly colored grocery and holiday coupon circulars. It was a grounding, completely ordinary routine that anchored her shattered nerves to the present. Richard moved around the adjoining kitchen in quiet, domestic synchronization, pouring fresh coffee into ceramic mugs.

The local media had honored the blackout. Richard’s stern directive to the station managers had worked beautifully. The camera crews from WTVC Channel 3 and WAVX Channel 13 had kept their distance, and the front page of the very newspaper Eira was currently cutting up had run a respectable, fact-based column about the closed FBI investigation without printing the family's address. The locals knew better than to cross the community that had rallied behind the radio broadcast.

But the national syndicates did not care about local gentlemen's agreements.

Gabriel was sitting at the opposite end of the dining table, his calloused fingers lightly tracing the edge of his coffee mug, when the low, heavy hum of a diesel engine rumbled down their quiet residential street.

It wasn't a standard car.

Gabriel’s head snapped up. His sharp, frontline eyes tracked the movement through the front window. A massive, sleek, white satellite van with out-of-state plates and a towering microwave mast slowly rolled to a stop directly against the curb of their front lawn.

The side door slid open. A polished, sharply dressed reporter stepped out into the freezing Virginia wind, holding a microphone bearing the logo of a major, national morning show. Right behind him, a cameraman hoisted a heavy broadcast rig onto his shoulder, the red recording light already blinking.

They weren't just looking for a quote; they were preparing to do a live remote broadcast from the front yard of a traumatized fourteen-year-old boy.

Gabriel stood up from the dining table. The heavy, protective energy that had been resting quietly in his chest instantly roared back to a rolling boil.

"Gabriel?" Eira asked, catching the sudden, violent shift in her son's posture. She paused, the scissors freezing halfway through a grocery coupon. She looked out the window and gasped, her hands gripping the edge of the table. "Oh, no."

Gwendolyn, who had been reading a book on the loveseat, slammed it shut and stood up. Her tactical eyes instantly assessed the threat level. "National news. They're going to set up on the grass to get the house in the background."

"No, they aren't," Gabriel rumbled, his voice dropping into a dangerous, glacial register.

He didn't grab his heavy winter coat. He just marched straight for the front entryway in his flannel shirt and jeans, unlocking the deadbolt with a sharp, metallic clack.

"Gabriel, wait," Richard called out, setting his coffee mug down and moving to follow the eighteen-year-old. He knew exactly how volatile a protective older brother could be against an invasive camera lens. "Let me handle the talking."

Gabriel pulled the front door open, letting a blast of freezing winter air into the foyer.

"You can talk to them if you want, Richard," Gabriel said, stepping out onto the frost-covered concrete porch. "But they are leaving."

Down at the curb, the national reporter was adjusting his earpiece, pointing toward the front window of the house. The cameraman panned his lens, catching Gabriel as he descended the porch steps.

Gabriel didn't jog, and he didn't shout. He walked down the center of the driveway with the heavy, unyielding stride of a man walking into a fight. His broad shoulders were squared, his jaw locked in absolute stone.

"Hey there!" the reporter called out, pasting on a bright, predatory smile of fabricated empathy. "Are you Gabriel? We're with the national morning broadcast. We just want to ask a few questions about your brother's miraculous return—"

"Turn the camera off," Gabriel commanded, stopping at the edge of his property line. His voice wasn't loud, but it carried a dark, vibrating threat that cut cleanly through the winter wind.

"We're just doing a piece on the community impact," the reporter pressed, completely ignoring the boundary, signaling the cameraman to push in tighter. "The whole country heard your song, Gabriel. People want to know how Julian is recovering from the abduction."

Gabriel closed the final three feet of distance. He didn't look at the reporter. He locked eyes directly with the cameraman.

Gabriel reached out with his large, calloused left hand and clamped his fingers entirely over the heavy glass lens of the broadcast camera, physically blinding the feed.

"Hey! Don't touch the rig!" the cameraman yelled, stumbling backward under the sudden, physical pressure.

"I said, turn it off," Gabriel repeated, his voice dropping another octave. "My brother is asleep in that house. You are not going to wake him up, and you are not going to turn his trauma into your Sunday morning entertainment."

The reporter’s fabricated smile completely vanished, replaced by an ugly, entitled sneer. "Listen, kid, we're on a public street. We have a First Amendment right to film the exterior of the house. You can't stop us from broadcasting."

"You're right. The street is public," Richard Harrison said, stepping up perfectly beside Gabriel.

The music therapy teacher didn't have Gabriel's intimidating, physical bulk, but he carried the absolute, unshakeable authority of an angry father. Richard pulled a small, black digital recorder from his pocket—a tool he used for mixing audio levels—and held it up.

"But the audio you're trying to capture isn't," Richard stated smoothly. "If you broadcast from this curb, you're going to pick up the ambient noise of the property. And as of yesterday afternoon, Vanguard Horizon Music Group officially holds the exclusive development and broadcasting rights to the name The Winter Palace and all associated audio of the Rowlands family."

Richard leveled a cold, calculating stare at the reporter.

"If you broadcast a live feed from this address without corporate authorization, I will personally call Elias Thorne in New York and have Vanguard's legal department bury your network in a multi-million dollar intellectual property lawsuit by Monday morning."

The reporter hesitated, his eyes darting from the unyielding, physical wall of Gabriel to the quiet, legal threat of Richard. National news networks loved a human interest story, but they were absolutely terrified of corporate litigation.

"This is ridiculous," the reporter scoffed, though he took a definitive step backward toward the van. "It's just a local story."

"Then leave it to the locals," Gabriel snarled, removing his hand from the camera lens and pointing a heavy finger toward the driver's side of the van. "Get off my street."

The cameraman, having assessed the absolute lack of fear in the eighteen-year-old’s eyes, didn't wait for the reporter. He hoisted the rig off his shoulder and slid the side door of the van open, climbing inside.

The reporter shot them one last, venomous glare before following suit. The heavy door slammed shut. The diesel engine roared, and the white satellite van pulled away from the curb, accelerating rapidly down the residential street until it completely disappeared around the corner.

Gabriel stood at the edge of the frosty grass, his chest heaving slightly as he watched the empty road.

Richard let out a slow, frosty breath, slipping the digital recorder back into his pocket. He looked at the eighteen-year-old, a profound sense of pride settling in his chest.

"The perimeter holds," Richard said quietly.

Gabriel finally let his rigid posture drop. A small, victorious smirk touched the corner of his mouth as he looked at Richard.

"Vanguard's legal department?" Gabriel asked, raising an eyebrow.

"Total bluff," Richard chuckled, clapping Gabriel warmly on the shoulder. "But corporate suits only speak the language of corporate threats. Come on. Let's go get some coffee and help your mother finish those coupons before you catch a cold."

Together, the frontline shield and the tactical anchor turned their backs on the street and walked back into the quiet, heavily defended sanctuary of the house in Ewell.

## 9:45 AM • Eira Rowlands — The Safe Harbor

The heavy winter sunlight pushed its way through the partially opened blinds of Julian’s bedroom, casting bright, geometric lines across the carpet.

In the center of the queen-sized bed, Julian slowly opened his eyes. His body felt like it was made of lead. The night had been a grueling, fractured series of terrifying micro-dreams where the freezing sway of the stolen sedan kept violently pulling him under. He hadn't slept for more than twenty minutes at a time, and the exhaustion settled deep in his bones.

But every single time he had startled awake, terrified and entirely unmoored, the perimeter had held.

Maria shifted against the pillows, letting out a quiet, sleepy sigh as she started to stir. The moment she moved, Julian’s arms tightened. He pulled her flush against his chest, burying his face into her shoulder in a desperate, unyielding hug. He wasn't ready to let go. The dark was gone, but the residual fear still clung to his nervous system, and Maria was the only anchor keeping him tethered to the room.

Maria didn't pull away. She blinked the sleep from her eyes, immediately recognizing the rigid tension in his shoulders. She wrapped her arms back around him, offering that firm, deep-pressure squeeze he relied on.

"I'm here, Julian," Maria murmured, her voice thick with sleep but absolute in its promise. "We're home."

They stayed like that for a long time, the quiet rhythm of the house echoing through the open doorway. Down the hall, Julian could hear the low, steady rumble of Gabriel's voice and the clinking of ceramic coffee mugs in the kitchen.

A sudden, sharp rumble in Julian's stomach finally broke the silence. He hadn't eaten a full, proper meal since Thursday night, and the grilled cheese from yesterday was long gone.

Maria pulled back slightly, offering a warm, knowing smile. "Hungry?"

Julian nodded eagerly.

Maria threw the heavy blankets back. She moved with the practiced, effortless routine of a best friend who had spent half her life alongside a manual wheelchair. She pulled his chair flush against the side of the mattress, locking the brakes with two sharp clicks.

"Alright, on three," Maria instructed, wrapping her arms securely under his arms and around his torso. Julian gripped her shoulders, helping to bear his own weight as best as his stiff muscles would allow. "One, two, three."

With a smooth, practiced pivot, Maria helped him transfer from the edge of the mattress down into the customized seating of his chair.

Julian let out a breath, reaching for his AAC device on the nightstand, but before he could type, another pressing biological reality hit him. He tapped his stomach, giving Maria a wide-eyed, urgent look.

"I've got it," Maria nodded, stepping to the door. "Gwen!"

A few seconds later, Gwendolyn appeared in the doorway, already dressed in a comfortable oversized sweater, her tactical mind instantly assessing the room.

"Bathroom?" Gwendolyn asked, reading her brother's posture immediately.

Julian nodded.

"Come on, Julep. Let's get you sorted," Gwendolyn said softly. She took the push-handles of his chair, rolling him out into the hallway and into the accessible bathroom.

While Maria took the opportunity to grab a quick change of clothes from her overnight bag, Gwendolyn handled the morning routine with the exact same quiet, unbreakable dignity she always provided. She possessed the core strength and the precise leverage needed to safely lift Julian, talking to him in a low, soothing cadence of Québécois French to keep his sensory inputs calm as she helped him use the toilet and wash his face.

By the time Gwendolyn wheeled a refreshed, dressed Julian out into the kitchen, the rest of the garrison was fully assembled.

Normally, Sunday mornings in James City County meant one thing: loading up the wheelchair van and driving down Richmond Road to one of Williamsburg’s legendary, crowded pancake houses. It was a local staple, a loud, bustling tradition of sticky syrup and towering stacks of buttermilk flapjacks.

But as Eira looked at Julian, seeing the dark, exhausted circles under his eyes and the way his hands still trembled slightly against his armrests, the decision was instantaneous.

"We are not leaving this house today," Eira announced, setting her coffee mug down on the counter. "We are not dealing with the Sunday brunch crowds, the noise, or the traffic."

Gabriel, who was standing by the stove with an apron tied awkwardly over his flannel shirt, flipped a massive, perfectly golden pancake in a cast-iron skillet.

"Way ahead of you, Mom," Gabriel grinned, pointing a spatula toward the dining table. "The Governor's Griddle has officially been relocated to the Ewell dining room. Richard made the batter, I'm working the griddle, and Gwen is on syrup duty."

Richard walked over, setting a massive plate loaded with steaming pancakes, crispy bacon, and scrambled eggs directly in front of Julian.

"No noisy restaurants today, Julian," Richard said warmly. "Just a quiet house, a hot breakfast, and the doors locked tight."

Julian looked at the towering stack of pancakes, then up at his brother, his mother, his sister, and the Harrisons. He reached out with his left hand, finding Maria’s hand where she sat beside him, and squeezed it tightly.

He pulled his AAC device onto his lap, his fingers flying across the keys.

<aac>THIS IS BETTER. THANK YOU.</aac>

The robotic voice echoed through the kitchen, lacking any human inflection, but carrying the absolute, undeniable relief of a boy who finally felt safe enough to just breathe. They didn't need a bustling restaurant to find a Sunday reset. They just needed the perimeter to hold.
