The heavy, accessible wheelchair van pulled out of the driveway in Ewell, its tires crunching over the frost-covered asphalt as Gabriel steered it toward US-60.

In the back, Julian sat securely locked into the floor restraints, his heavy winter coat zipped up to his chin. Maria sat in the jump seat right beside him, holding his hand. The van's heater was blasting, keeping the cabin incredibly warm against the bitter December chill.

As they approached the corner of US-60 and Forest Heights Drive, Gabriel slowed the van for the traffic light. Sitting at the intersection was a heavy, high-floor transit bus. It sported the familiar, tri-color livery of Peninsula Regional Transport—a crisp white roof, a thick, dark maroon stripe through the center, and a tan lower body.

Above the windshield, the mechanical flipdot destination sign cascaded with a rapid, rhythmic clack-clack-clack, the magnetic yellow-green dots physically flipping to change the text.

BLUE LINE

Clack-clack-clack.

COLONY OUTLET

Under normal circumstances, that exact sound was the center of Julian's morning routine. He and Maria were high school freshmen, and with exactly one week left until winter break, they were supposed to be standing on that frosty curb.

The school district offered a specialized bus for disabled students, but Julian absolutely refused to take it. Riding the special needs bus meant being separated from Maria, and Julian couldn't handle the sensory chaos of a school commute without his anchor.

So, every morning, they took the PRT Blue Line. They would flash their high school IDs to the driver to cover the fare—just as Gabriel and Gwendolyn did with their Williamsburg Community College IDs—leaving the family van parked at home for grocery runs or Sundays when the buses didn't operate. The driver would activate the mechanical whine of the heavy wheelchair lift, hoisting Julian’s manual chair aboard the high-floor bus. Then, they would take the quick, five-minute ride westbound to school, and catch the inbound bus heading toward the Transportation Center in the afternoon. It was an independent, perfectly calibrated thirty-minute loop.

But today, Julian just stared at the bus through the tinted window of the van. The rhythmic clack-clack-clack of the sign usually grounded him, but right now, it just left a quiet, lingering ache in his chest. His normal life felt impossibly far away.

Maria noticed his gaze. She squeezed his hand, leaning closer.

"We'll get back to it, Julian," Maria murmured softly. "I promise. Next semester, when you're ready, we'll be right back on the Blue Line."

Julian looked away from the window and offered her a small, exhausted smile. He typed a quick response on his AAC device, keeping the volume turned down so the robotic voice wouldn't startle the front of the van.

<aac>JUST US TODAY.</aac>

"Just us," Maria agreed, resting her head lightly against his shoulder.

Gabriel merged the van onto the highway, driving with a smooth, deliberate caution. Richard Harrison sat in the passenger seat, his eyes scanning the morning traffic with the quiet vigilance of an active sentry.

Twenty minutes later, the van pulled into the crowded parking lot of the Williamsburg Circuit Court.

The courthouse was an imposing, traditional brick building, completely devoid of warmth. To Julian, it looked exactly like the kind of place where a judge could make a terrifying, life-altering decision with the simple strike of a gavel. His breathing hitched slightly, his fingers tightening against his armrests.

Gabriel immediately threw the van into park but left the heavy engine running.

"Alright, we're holding the perimeter right here," Gabriel announced, turning in the driver's seat to look at his brother. He offered a fierce, reassuring grin. "The heat stays on, the doors stay locked, and nobody is getting out of this van."

Eira unbuckled her seatbelt in the middle row. She was dressed in a sharp, conservative wool coat, holding the thick manila folder of legal documents tightly to her chest. Gwendolyn sat beside her, radiating the absolute, calculating focus of a tactician stepping onto a battlefield.

"We won't be long, Julep," Gwendolyn said, reaching over to give Julian’s knee an affectionate squeeze. "<lang="fr-CA" trans="We're going to get this done quickly.">On va régler ça vite fait.</lang>"

"Richard, keep the radio low if he wants music," Eira instructed, her maternal instincts buzzing as she prepared to leave the safety of the vehicle. She looked at her youngest son, her eyes fiercely protective. "We are going to give the clerk the death certificate and the petitions. When we walk back out those doors, you will officially be Julian Rowlands."

Julian took a deep breath. The fear of the imposing brick building receded, blocked entirely by the physical barrier of the van and the absolute certainty of his family. He tapped his keyboard.

<aac>GO DO IT.</aac>

Eira smiled, a profound sense of pride swelling in her chest. She and Gwendolyn stepped out of the sliding side door, the freezing wind instantly catching their coats.

Gabriel slid the heavy door shut behind them, the locks engaging with a heavy, satisfying thunk.

Inside the warm, secure cabin, Richard reached out and turned the radio dial, finding a quiet classical station to keep the sensory environment perfectly regulated. Maria leaned her head against Julian's shoulder, and Gabriel kept his eyes locked on the courthouse doors.

The frontline was secure. Now, it was up to Eira and Gwendolyn to sever the final, legal chain binding them to Thomas Cross.
