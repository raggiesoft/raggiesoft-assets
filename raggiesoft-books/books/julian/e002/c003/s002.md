The Williamsburg Circuit Court was a towering fortress of red brick, heavy oak doors, and echoing marble floors.

Despite their initial plan to leave Julian safely locked in the warm perimeter of the wheelchair van, the reality of Virginia family law had forced a pivot. The clerk of the court had informed them that to waive a deceased parent's consent for a minor’s name change, the presiding judge would want to lay eyes on the minor in question.

So, the entire garrison moved inside.

While Gabriel, Gwendolyn, and Maria formed a tight, protective ring around Julian’s manual wheelchair in the echoing lobby, Eira marched directly toward a bank of coin-operated payphones against the far wall. Richard walked right beside her, acting as a physical buffer against the bustling morning crowd of lawyers and clerks.

Eira dropped a handful of quarters into the slot and dialed the local high school’s main office.

The public school system’s approach to autism and cerebral palsy was still notoriously rigid and institutional. Even with the recently published DSM-IV updating developmental classifications, the school district remained stubbornly resistant to unorthodox accommodations. But over the last seven years, Eira and Richard had fought a relentless, uphill battle against the county’s Special Education department. They had dragged psychologists and therapists into endless IEP (Individualized Education Program) meetings to formally document one undeniable fact: Maria was Julian’s anchor. Without her, he could not regulate his sensory environment; with her, he thrived.

"Yes, Principal Davies, it’s Eira Cross—soon to be Rowlands," Eira spoke into the heavy plastic receiver, her crisp British English carrying a sharp, rhythmic Welsh lilt that immediately commanded respect. "I am calling to formally confirm that neither Julian nor Maria Harrison will be attending for the final week of the term."

Richard leaned in close to the receiver. "Tell him the medical exemptions are signed," he added quietly.

"The trauma exemptions from his pediatrician are already en route to the guidance office," Eira continued flawlessly. "Given the federal nature of the incident over the weekend, I trust there will be no truancy pushback."

The principal’s voice crackled through the earpiece, full of stammering, eager-to-please American apologies. The school was completely backing off.

Eira hung up the phone with a definitive clack. She looked at Richard, letting out a sharp exhale. "Sorted. The school is giving them a wide berth."

"Good," Richard nodded, adjusting his coat. He looked across the lobby at his daughter, who was standing shoulder-to-shoulder with Julian, her hand resting firmly on the push-rim of his chair. "Let's go face the judge."

Fifteen minutes later, the heavy oak doors of Courtroom 3 swung open.

The Honorable Judge Lawson—a stern, gray-haired man with a thick, tidewater Virginia drawl—sat behind the elevated mahogany bench. He peered over his reading glasses at the formidable, highly organized family standing before him.

"I have four petitions on the docket this morning for the Cross family," Judge Lawson announced, his American accent flat and administrative as he shuffled the manila folders. "Let's start with the adults. Gabriel and Gwendolyn Cross."

Gabriel stepped forward, his broad shoulders squared. Gwendolyn stepped up perfectly in sync right beside him.

"We are filing as independent adults, Your Honour," Gabriel stated. His deep baritone resonated through the quiet courtroom, the musical, rolling cadence of his Welsh-British accent contrasting sharply with the judge's Southern drawl. "We wish to sever our legal ties to our father's surname. We're petitioning to take our mother's maiden name, Rowlands."

"It is our absolute right, Your Honour," Gwendolyn added, her own Welsh lilt crisp and tactical. "The paperwork and filing fees are fully in order."

Judge Lawson reviewed the forms, finding no legal impediments for two eighteen-year-olds. "Petitions for Gabriel and Gwendolyn granted," the judge stated, striking his gavel lightly.

He moved to the next file. "Eira Cross. I see you are petitioning to revert to your maiden name."

"I am, Your Honour," Eira replied, stepping forward to present the heavily embossed, court-certified divorce decree. "As stipulated in the finalization of the divorce, I am legally returning to the name Rowlands."

The judge barely glanced at the decree, as it was standard procedure. "Petition granted, Ms. Rowlands."

Then, the judge sighed, picking up the final, thickest folder. He looked down from the bench, his eyes landing on the fourteen-year-old boy sitting in the manual wheelchair, flanked closely by Maria.

"Now we come to the petition for the minor, Julian Cross," Judge Lawson said, his tone softening slightly, though he remained bound by the strict parameters of family law. "Ms. Rowlands, changing a minor's surname in the Commonwealth of Virginia typically requires the explicit, written consent of both biological parents. I see the divorce decree strips the father of visitation, but it does not formally terminate his parental rights regarding legal name changes."

"The father's consent is no longer required, Your Honour," Eira said, her voice turning to absolute steel.

She opened her folder and pulled out a single, starkly official document printed on heavy Ohio state paper. She handed it to the bailiff, who carried it up to the bench.

"That is the official death certificate for Thomas Cross, issued yesterday morning by the Athens County Coroner," Eira explained. "He can no longer make decisions for this family."

Judge Lawson looked at the death certificate, the bureaucratic reality of the situation clicking into place. The federal kidnapping bulletin had been all over the local Virginia news stations; the judge knew exactly who the man on that death certificate was, and what he had done to the boy sitting in the courtroom.

"I see," Judge Lawson said quietly. He set the paper down and looked directly at Julian. "Julian, son. Because of your age, the court still needs to know that this is what you want. Do you understand what this petition means?"

Julian’s chest tightened, the heavy, intimidating atmosphere of the courtroom pressing down on him. But Maria squeezed his hand, applying that perfect, grounding deep-pressure. Gabriel stepped up behind the wheelchair, resting his large hands on the push-grips, forming a physical wall at Julian's back.

Julian was not alone.

He pulled his AAC device onto his lap. His hands were shaking slightly, but his fingers found the keys with fierce, deliberate intent. The entire courtroom sat in absolute silence, waiting for the fourteen-year-old survivor to speak.

Julian hit the speaker button.

<aac>I WANT MOMMY'S BIRTH NAME.</aac>

The digitized, robotic voice echoed off the marble walls and the high wooden ceilings. It lacked a Welsh lilt or an American drawl, but it carried a weight of pure, unadulterated conviction that no human accent could ever match.

Judge Lawson stared at the boy. The sheer, protective power of the family standing around that wheelchair was undeniable. The judge didn't need any further clarification; he understood the intent perfectly.

"The court recognizes the minor's wishes," Judge Lawson declared, his voice thick with a profound, quiet respect. He picked up his wooden gavel. "With the biological father deceased, and the sole custodial parent petitioning on the minor's behalf, I find this name change to be fully in the best interest of the child."

BANG.

"Petition granted," the judge finalized. "Let the record reflect the legal name of the minor is now Julian Rowlands."

Gabriel let out a massive, triumphant breath, his grip tightening proudly on his brother's wheelchair. Gwendolyn closed her eyes, a fierce smile breaking across her face. Maria leaned down and threw her arms around Julian's neck, hugging him tightly.

Julian looked down at his AAC device, completely ignoring the judge and the clerks. He deleted his previous sentence and typed a single, brand-new word.

<aac>ROWLANDS.</aac>

They had walked into the courthouse carrying the heavy, traumatizing shadow of their father. But as they turned their backs on the judge and walked out through the heavy oak doors, they left the monster completely behind them.
