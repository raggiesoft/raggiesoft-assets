## 2:00 PM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Soundstage

The final bell of the fall semester had rung at noon, releasing the students of James City County for an agonizingly anticipated winter break. But for the newly minted Rowlands family and their garrison, the half-day of school didn't mean going home to relax. It meant loading into the wheelchair van and driving an hour up I-64 to Richmond.

Vanguard Horizon Music Group’s master recording studio was a massive, intimidating complex of soundproofed walls, heavy acoustic doors, and polished wood floors. The control room alone was the size of the entire Ewell living room, dominated by a sprawling, forty-eight-channel studio mixing console that looked like it required an engineering degree just to turn on.

For a group of teenagers used to crowding around a pawn-shop four-track cassette recorder, it was like stepping onto the bridge of a spaceship. Marcus stood near the thick, double-paned glass looking out onto the live tracking floor, his eyes wide as he took in the isolated drum booth. Sarah gripped the handle of her cello case a little tighter, intimidated but entirely captivated by the towering racks of outboard compressors and equalizers lining the walls.

But before a single instrument was unpacked, the perimeter was established.

Julian was wheeled into the control room and parked in a prime location right beside the massive leather producer's couch. Maria sat beside him, casually doing her geometry homework on a clipboard while resting her shoulder against his arm. The studio engineers, warned ahead of time by Elias Thorne, had flawlessly prepped the space. They had dimmed the harsh overhead halogens to a warm, ambient glow, and promised to keep the playback monitors at a strictly regulated, sensory-friendly volume.

Elias Thorne stood at the center of the control room, impeccably dressed in a tailored suit as always. He was flanked by two senior studio executives who looked considerably older and far more skeptical. One of them subtly checked his gold wristwatch, clearly operating under the assumption that Elias had dragged them down to Richmond to humor a group of suburban high schoolers.

"Alright, Winter Palace," Elias said, leaning against the edge of the mixing console, projecting absolute confidence. "Let's hear Draft Two. Show my colleagues exactly what I bought."

Gabriel stepped forward. The contrast between the slick, multi-million-dollar corporate environment and the eighteen-year-old boy in a worn denim jacket was stark. He reached into his pocket and pulled out the clear, scuffed plastic cassette tape.

He handed it to the head engineer, who took it with white-gloved care and slotted it into a pristine, high-fidelity Studer playback deck.

The massive studio monitors hummed to life. The pristine, delicate plinking of Julian’s music box filled the room, the notes crystal clear and perfectly defined.

And then, the drop hit.

The concussive boom of Sarah's cello and Gabriel's distorted guitar didn't just play; it detonated. Even at the strictly regulated, sensory-friendly volume, the acoustically perfect environment of the Richmond studio captured the sheer, theatrical weight of the song. The deep, driving bass frequencies bypassed the air entirely and vibrated directly through the polished wood floor.

Julian’s eyes went wide. He felt the heavy, rhythmic thrum of the bass drum travel straight up through the rubber tires of his manual wheelchair and into his bones. It wasn't overwhelming; it was grounding. He let out a breathless, happy vocalization, leaning his head against Maria's shoulder as Gabriel's deep baritone tore through the room.

It didn't sound like a desperate plea recorded in a cramped spare room anymore. It sounded like an incoming storm. It sounded like an army.

When the bridge hit, and Maria’s glockenspiel pierced cleanly through the heavy distortion of the rock-opera choir, the skeptical executive who had been checking his watch slowly lowered his arm. He stared blankly at the studio monitors, his professional detachment entirely shattered by the raw, bleeding power of the track.

When the song faded back into the fragile music box melody and the tape clicked to a hard stop, the control room descended into an absolute, stunned silence.

Elias Thorne didn't look at the band. He turned his head slowly to look at his two colleagues, offering a sharp, unapologetically triumphant smile.

"I told you," Elias said quietly, letting the silence hold the weight of the moment. "It's an anthem."

## 2:30 PM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The Language Barrier

"It's an incredible foundation," Elias said, turning his calculating gaze from the massive analog mixing console to Gabriel and Gwendolyn. "Now, we build Draft Three. We have the live string section booked for tomorrow, and the choir coming in on Monday. But before we lay down the scratch tracks, we need to finalize the vocal arrangement."

Gwendolyn stepped forward, a crisp, highly organized notebook in her hands. Her tactical mind was operating at peak efficiency.

"We have the vocal arrangement finalized, Elias," Gwendolyn stated, her Welsh lilt clipping with professional authority. "But we aren't just recording one track. We are recording four."

Elias raised an eyebrow, motioning for her to continue.

"The English version is the master," Gwendolyn explained, referencing her notes. "But our mother is from Wales, and we have extended family in Stanstead, Québec. We want to record a version entirely in Welsh, a version entirely in Québécois French, and a fourth version that seamlessly weaves all three languages together."

Gabriel crossed his arms, standing solidly beside his twin. "The song is about a beacon. It's about bringing someone home. We want to share all three of our languages with the world."

Elias looked at the eighteen-year-old twins. He didn't laugh, and he didn't immediately dismiss the ambition. He simply crossed his arms, leaning back against the heavy leather of the producer's chair.

"Where exactly in Wales is your mother from?" Elias asked.

"A village in the north," Gabriel answered, bracing himself for the inevitable, butchered American pronunciation. "Llanfairpwllgwyngyll—"

"—gogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch," Elias finished seamlessly.

He didn't stumble. He didn't pause. He rolled the complex, breathy ll consonants and the guttural ch with absolute, breathtaking, native-sounding perfection.

Gabriel’s jaw practically hit the soundproofed floor. Gwendolyn stared at the A&R executive in complete shock, her tactical brain momentarily short-circuiting. Over on the couch, even Julian looked up from his AAC device, a bright, deeply surprised smile breaking across his face.

Elias offered a wry, knowing smirk, seeing their utter disbelief.

"Four years ago, before Vanguard promoted me to the executive suite, I spent eighteen months tracking a post-punk band in a freezing, converted stone barn on the Isle of Anglesey," Elias explained, his voice losing a bit of its corporate polish and revealing the gritty, exhausted music producer underneath. "I drank at the local pub in Llanfair PG every single weekend. I know exactly what the Menai Strait looks like in the dead of winter. I respect your roots, Rowlands family. I really do."

Elias pushed off the chair, his expression shifting back into the ruthless, pragmatic executive who controlled the budgets.

"But the answer is no."

Gabriel bristled, the frontline protector instantly waking up, completely unfazed by the multi-million-dollar studio surrounding them. "Elias—"

"Listen to me, Gabriel," Elias interrupted, his voice firm but entirely respectful. He wasn't talking down to them; he was treating them like professionals. "You are not an established act yet. You are a debut band trying to break into a saturated 1997 market. I have the budget to market a massive, symphonic English rock-opera to mainstream American radio stations. I can get this on every dial from New York to Los Angeles."

Elias tapped his knuckles against the edge of the mixing board. "But I cannot walk into a corporate boardroom and demand they spend hundreds of thousands of dollars pressing, distributing, and marketing a Welsh and Québécois French rock song to American audiences. Mainstream rock radio simply will not play it. It's financial suicide for a debut EP. The studio is authorizing and funding the English track. That's it."

Gwendolyn looked at the floor, her jaw clenching. She knew Elias was right. The tactical, logical part of her brain understood the brutal reality of the music industry. They were asking a major label to take an impossible risk.

Gabriel let out a heavy, frustrated breath. He looked over at the couch.

Julian was typing quietly on his AAC device, completely unbothered by the corporate standoff. He hit the speaker button.

<aac>DO THE ENGLISH ONE FOR HIM.</aac>

Julian looked up at Gabriel, his eyes conveying a silent, unshakeable certainty. Gabriel understood the unspoken second half of that sentence perfectly. Do the English one for Vanguard. We will do the rest for us.

Gabriel looked at Gwendolyn. They shared a single, synchronized nod. They would record the foreign language tracks themselves, back in the safe, quiet perimeter of the Ewell house, exactly as they wanted them. No corporate executives. No market research. Just the family.

"Fine," Gabriel agreed, turning back to Elias. "English for the master track. But we aren't pulling any punches on the orchestration."

"I wouldn't let you if you tried," Elias promised, his predatory smile returning. "Get your guitars, Gabriel. Let's build Draft Three."
