## 10:15 AM — The Breach

Freshman English Literature was, by its very nature, a daily exercise in survival for Julian Rowlands.

It wasn't that the curriculum was too difficult; it was the fact that he was trapped in a room for forty-five minutes entirely dedicated to dissecting, speaking, and analyzing the English language. English was the language of the flashing police lights. It was the language of the Ohio trauma ward. And, most terrifyingly, it was the only language Thomas Cross had ever spoken. To Julian’s unmedicated, traumatized nervous system, the English language felt like walking barefoot across broken glass.

To cope, Julian had developed a quiet, highly effective academic accommodation. He sat in his manual wheelchair at his desk in the back row, flanked by Clara and Maria, his spiral-bound notebook open. As the teacher lectured in English, Julian meticulously translated every single note into Welsh. Writing in his mother's native tongue formed a psychological buffer, filtering the terrifying language of his abuser through the impenetrable, absolute safety of his family's sanctuary.

But today, the regular teacher was out.

Sitting behind the heavy wooden desk at the front of the room was a substitute. Mr. Evans was an older, stern-looking man with impeccable posture, a tweed jacket, and the quiet, calculating gaze of a strict disciplinarian. He had spent the first ten minutes of class establishing absolute order, demanding complete silence while the students worked on their reading comprehension packets.

For a while, the silence was perfect. But then, the perimeter was breached.

Sitting one aisle over was a loud, chronically disruptive freshman named Kevin. Kevin had been lurking in the hallway before the first bell and had overheard Gabriel giving Julian a quick, affectionate farewell.

Kevin leaned across the aisle, his eyes landing on the jagged, rolling consonants filling Julian's notebook.

"What kind of alien gibberish is that, Julep?" Kevin whispered, a cruel, mocking sneer on his face.

Julian’s pen snapped to a halt. His breath caught violently in his throat.

Julep. It was the password. It was the sacred, fiercely protected name forged in his nursery and used by his siblings to keep him safe in the dark. Hearing that specific name drop casually from the mouth of an outsider wasn't just an insult; it was a catastrophic security breach.

Julian detonated.

He didn't shrink back. A massive, uncontrollable surge of protective rage and pure panic flooded his system. He slammed both of his hands down onto his desk with a loud, echoing CRACK that made half the classroom jump. He pulled his AAC device forward, his fingers hammering the keys with violent force. He didn't use the English synthesizer.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="NEVER CALL ME THAT NAME!">PEIDIWCH BYTH Â GALW FI YR ENW HWNW!</lang></aac>

The digitized Welsh roared through the quiet classroom at maximum volume, the metallic, jagged syllables slicing through the air like a siren.

Kevin jumped back, momentarily startled, but his teenage ego quickly recovered. He scowled, leaning back in his chair. "Speak English, freak! This is America! What, are you gonna go cry to mommy?"

Clara and Maria were out of their seats before Kevin even finished the sentence. The legs of their chairs screeched agonizingly against the linoleum as they threw themselves between Kevin's desk and Julian's wheelchair. Their symmetrical shields were fully raised, their identical eyes blazing with absolute, protective fury.

"<lang="cy" trans="Shut your mouth and leave him alone!">Cau dy geg a gadewch lonydd iddo!</lang>" Clara and Maria screamed at the bully in perfect, terrifying unison, their own Welsh flawlessly mirroring the cadence of Julian's family.

"Enough."

The single word wasn't shouted, but it cracked through the room with the devastating authority of a whip.

Mr. Evans was suddenly standing at the end of the aisle. The older substitute towered over Kevin's desk, his expression carved from absolute stone.

"But he's—" Kevin started to complain, pointing a finger at Julian.

"Gather your belongings," Mr. Evans interrupted, his voice deathly quiet and perfectly level. He pulled a disciplinary referral pad from his jacket pocket and began writing with rapid, ruthless precision. "You do not demand language from anyone in this room, nor do you mock a fellow student. You will take this referral to the principal's office immediately. If you speak another word before you cross that threshold, I will personally ensure your suspension lasts until the summer."

Kevin looked at the substitute's cold, unyielding eyes, swallowed hard, grabbed his backpack, and practically sprinted out of the classroom.

The heavy door clicked shut. The classroom was dead silent.

Clara and Maria were still standing defensively in front of Julian, their chests heaving. Julian was gripping the armrests of his manual chair, his knuckles entirely white, his breathing shallow and erratic. He waited for the inevitable. He waited for the strict disciplinarian to turn his wrath on the boy who had just disrupted the reading packet with a loud, electronic foreign language.

Mr. Evans tore the copy of the referral from his pad. He looked down at the identical twins, offering a slow, respectful nod to the fiercely protective girls.

Then, the substitute took a step closer to Julian's wheelchair. The severe, intimidating mask of the disciplinarian instantly melted away. Mr. Evans looked down at the spiral-bound notebook on Julian's desk. He saw the Welsh translations. He looked at the trembling boy, and then he looked at the AAC device.

In a single, brilliant moment of observation, the older man understood exactly what he was looking at. He recognized the heavy, suffocating weight of trauma. He knew why a boy would need to hide from the English language inside an English classroom.

Mr. Evans crouched down slowly, bringing himself down to Julian's eye level so he wasn't looming.

"<lang="cy" trans="It's alright, boy,">Mae'n iawn, fachgen,</lang>" Mr. Evans said softly.

Julian gasped, his eyes going wide. The words were perfectly accented, rolling with the familiar, deeply comforting cadence of his own home.

"<lang="cy" trans="You did nothing wrong,">Fe wnaethoch chi ddim byd o'i le,</lang>" the substitute continued in flawless Welsh, his tone carrying a profound, anchoring warmth. "<lang="cy" trans="I see your notes. It is okay to take refuge in your language. I understand.">Rwy'n gweld eich nodiadau. Mae'n iawn i gymryd lloches yn eich iaith. Rwy'n deall.</lang>"

The rigid tension locked in Julian's shoulders completely shattered. The classroom wasn't a threat anymore. The perimeter had been secured by an unexpected ally.

Julian dropped his hands from his armrests. He let out a long, ragged exhale, slumping back into his chair. He reached for his AAC device, keeping it firmly locked on the Welsh synthesizer.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="THANK YOU VERY MUCH.">DIOLCH YN FAWR.</lang></aac>

"<lang="cy" trans="You're welcome,">Croeso,</lang>" Mr. Evans smiled gently. He stood back up, adjusting his tweed jacket, and looked at Clara and Maria. "Take your seats, girls. You did well."

As the substitute walked calmly back to the front of the room to resume the lesson, Julian let himself relax, Clara’s hand coming to rest firmly on his left shoulder while Maria squeezed his right arm. He looked at the chalkboard, and then down at his Welsh notes. For the first time all semester, he didn't feel like he was surviving English class; he actually felt safe.

He tapped a quick, quiet message on his device and showed the screen to Clara and Maria.

<aac>I WISH HE WAS OUR REGULAR TEACHER.</aac>

Clara and Maria leaned in, identical, relieved smiles spreading across their faces.

"Me too, Julep. Me too," they whispered back in perfect unison.

## 10:25 AM — The Mother Tongue

The heavy classroom door remained firmly shut, separating Kevin and his disciplinary referral from the rest of the freshman English class.

Inside Room 114, the remaining twenty-nine students sat in complete, stunned silence.

They had just witnessed the strictest substitute in the building obliterate the class bully, and then immediately drop to a crouch to softly comfort the quiet boy in the wheelchair using an unrecognizable, rhythmic language.

Mr. Evans walked slowly back to the chalkboard. He picked up a piece of white chalk and turned to face the room.

He could see the curiosity practically vibrating in the teenagers' eyes. They weren't hostile; they were completely captivated.

Mr. Evans looked over the rows of desks, his gaze landing gently on the back corner.

He offered a slight, respectful tilt of his head toward Julian, Maria, and Clara.

"Julian. Maria. Clara," Mr. Evans addressed them quietly, treating the fourteen-year-olds with the diplomatic respect of equals. "Ignorance breeds fear, and fear breeds cruelty. With your permission... may we demystify the language for them?"

Julian sat comfortably against the backrest of his manual chair, the lingering adrenaline of his near-meltdown entirely flushed from his system.

He looked at Maria and Clara, who flanked him on either side. The identical twins offered him small, synchronized, encouraging nods.

Julian pulled his AAC device forward, keeping the synthesizer locked to his safe harbor.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="YES. TEACH THEM.">OES. DYSGWCH NHW.</lang></aac>

The digitized Welsh echoed through the room. A few students in the front row leaned forward, completely fascinated by the metallic, rolling consonants.

Mr. Evans smiled warmly. He turned to the chalkboard and wrote a single word in sharp, precise cursive: Cymraeg.

"What you just heard," Mr. Evans announced, projecting his voice clearly across the room, "is Cymraeg. In English, it is known as Welsh. It is a Celtic language, spoken natively in the country of Wales, which lies to the west of England. It is one of the oldest living languages in Europe."

He tapped the chalk against the board. "To an untrained ear, it sounds alien. The alphabet is different. We use letters like 'W' and 'Y' as vowels. We roll our R's, and we use a sound—the double L—that does not exist in the English language."

The substitute wrote two basic phrases on the board: Bore da and Diolch.

"Bore da means 'Good morning,'" Mr. Evans instructed, pronouncing it clearly. "And Diolch means 'Thank you.' Go ahead. Try it."

A disjointed, thoroughly Americanized chorus of Bore da and Diolch echoed back to him. The class was entirely hooked.

The rigid, boring English reading packet was forgotten, replaced by the thrilling discovery of a secret, ancient code right in their own classroom.

Julian watched his classmates attempt the pronunciation. A small, genuine smile touched the corners of his mouth.

The language of his sanctuary wasn't being mocked anymore; it was being respected.

"Now," Mr. Evans continued, a distinct spark of national pride lighting up his stern features. "The Welsh are very proud of our heritage. In fact, we hold the title for the longest, most complicated town name in all of Europe. It is a badge of honor for any true Welshman to be able to pronounce it in a single breath."

He turned to the chalkboard, preparing to write the fifty-eight-letter behemoth across the slate. "The town is called—"

"Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch," two voices chimed in perfect, flawless unison from the back row.

Mr. Evans froze, the chalk hovering an inch from the blackboard.

The entire class turned around in their seats.

Maria and Clara sat perfectly straight at their desks, flanking Julian's wheelchair. They hadn't stumbled. They hadn't paused for breath. They had rolled the complex, breathy ll consonants and the guttural ch with absolute, breathtaking, synchronized perfection.

They sounded exactly like Eira Rowlands in stereo.

Julian tapped his armrest rapidly, letting out a bright, highly vocalized laugh of pure delight.

Mr. Evans slowly lowered his piece of chalk. He stared at the fourteen-year-old American identical twins in absolute, unadulterated shock.

"I'm sorry... could you translate that for the class, girls?"

Maria and Clara turned to look at each other. Identical, slightly evil grins broke across their faces. They had been taught the translation by Eira years ago, and they didn't just know it by heart—they knew exactly how to weaponize their twin dynamic for maximum dramatic effect.

They turned back to the substitute teacher, ping-ponging the translation back and forth, one word at a time, without a single microsecond of hesitation.

"It," Maria started. "Translates," Clara continued. "To," Maria said. "Saint," Clara fired back. "Mary's," "Church," "in," "the," "hollow," "of," "the," "white," "hazel," "near," "to," "the," "rapid," "whirlpool," "of," "Llantysilio," "of," "the," "red," "cave."

Clara delivered the final word with a sharp, satisfied nod. The stereophonic performance was completely flawless and undeniably unnerving.

The classroom erupted into spontaneous, impressed applause. A boy near the windows let out a low whistle of disbelief.

The twins waited for the noise to die down, offering a casual, very American shrug that contrasted completely with their flawless Celtic ping-pong match. They had never stepped foot outside of Virginia—save for the terrifying drive to Ohio to bring Julian home—but they had spent seven years entirely immersed in the Rowlands family perimeter.

"But if you actually live there, nobody says the whole thing," Maria clarified, her standard American accent returning to match her sister's. "Julian's mom says it's mostly for the tourists and the train station sign. Locals just call it Llanfairpwll, or Llanfair PG if you're in a hurry."

Mr. Evans leaned against his heavy wooden desk, completely abandoning his strict, disciplinarian persona.

He let out a rich, genuine laugh, shaking his head in sheer amazement.

"Spot on, girls. Absolutely spot on," Mr. Evans praised, his eyes shining.

He looked specifically at the three teenagers in the back row.

"And as it happens... I know exactly what that rapid whirlpool looks like. My name is Jordan Evans, and I was born and raised two streets over from that very church."

Julian’s breath hitched. He looked at the substitute teacher, his eyes going incredibly wide.

Of all the substitute teachers in the entire James City County public school district. Of all the classrooms, on all the days. The universe had dropped a man from his exact home village right onto the frontline to protect him.

Julian pulled his hands off his armrests and quickly typed on his AAC device. He didn't use the English output, and he didn't need to. He was speaking directly to a man who understood his perimeter perfectly.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="I WAS BORN THERE TOO. WE MOVED TO AMERICA WHEN I WAS SIX. YOU ARE THE BEST TEACHER EVER.">CEFAIS FY NGENI YNO HEFYD. SYMUDON NI I AMERICA PAN OEDDWN I'N CHWECH. CHI YW YR ATHRO GORAU ERIOED.</lang></aac>

Jordan Evans read the translation from the cadence of the robotic voice.

He froze, his eyes widening slightly as the immense weight of that statement hit him.

This wasn't just a boy clinging to his mother's heritage. This was a native son of Anglesey. This was a boy who had spent the first six years of his life breathing the cold salt air of the Menai Strait before being uprooted and brought to America.

It made the boy's desperate, fierce attachment to the Welsh language instantly, infinitely more profound. It was his first home.

A profound, deeply moved smile softened the older man's strict features.

He placed his right hand over his heart and offered a slight, formal bow to the boy in the wheelchair.

"<lang="cy" trans="It is an honor to meet you, Julian,">Mae'n anrhydedd i cwrdd â chi, Julian,</lang>" Mr. Evans replied warmly.

He held Julian's gaze, his voice dropping to a register of absolute, validating safety.

"<lang="cy" trans="And remember... no matter how far you travel, you are always home in our language.">A chofiwch... waeth pa mor bell ydych chi'n teithio, rydych chi bob amser adref yn ein hiaith ni.</lang>"

Julian let out a long, shuddering sigh of absolute relief, his left hand finding Maria's and his right hand finding Clara's, holding onto his anchors tight.

The bell rang overhead, signaling the end of the period.

But as the freshman class packed up their bags, no one was talking about the English reading packet, and no one was talking about Kevin getting sent to the office.

They were all talking, and trying to pronounce, the incredible, ancient language that belonged to the quiet boy in the back row.

## 2:45 PM — The Transit Loop

When the final bell of the day rang, James City County High School erupted into a chaotic, echoing bottleneck of slamming lockers and shouting teenagers.

Julian kept his head down, his heavy noise-isolating headphones firmly clamped over his ears. He navigated his manual wheelchair smoothly through the crowded corridor, perfectly synchronized with Maria, who walked flush against his left wheel. She acted as a physical bumper, expertly steering oblivious freshmen and rushing upperclassmen away from Julian's personal space.

They pushed through the heavy double doors and out into the crisp, bright afternoon air.

During Julian's chaotic freshman enrollment meeting back in January, the county’s transportation department had aggressively pushed for Julian to take the specialized special-needs bus. They had presented the smaller, yellow bus with the hydraulic lift as the safest, most logical option.

Eira Rowlands had taken one look at the district's strict liability policies—which stated that only legally disabled students and assigned aides could ride, strictly forbidding friends or siblings—and had flatly rejected it.

“If Maria is not on the manifest, Julian is not on the vehicle,” Eira had stated, her Welsh accent carrying absolute, zero-tolerance authority. “My son will take the city transit.”

And so, every afternoon at 2:45, Julian and Maria bypassed the loud, diesel-choked lineup of yellow school buses in the loading zone.

Instead, they walked to the edge of the campus, stopping at the crosswalk of Campus Drive. It was a quiet, tree-lined suburban street with a strict twenty-five-mile-per-hour speed limit, separating the high school from the sprawling brick buildings of Williamsburg Community College.

Maria looked both ways, her hand resting protectively on the back of Julian's chair, before they crossed the quiet asphalt.

The moment they pulled open the heavy glass doors of the WCC library, the sensory environment drastically shifted. The chaotic, immature screaming of the high school vanished, replaced by the hushed, mature ambient noise of college students quietly typing on laptops and flipping through massive textbooks. It smelled like old paper, floor wax, and the dark roast coffee from the campus café.

Julian pulled his headphones down to rest around his neck, letting out a long, quiet sigh of relief.

They navigated through the stacks of reference books, heading toward a large oak table in the quietest corner of the second floor.

Gabriel and Gwendolyn were already there.

Gabriel was slouched in a wooden chair, a pair of reading glasses perched on his nose as he highlighted a massive Sociology textbook. Gwendolyn sat across from him, her posture perfect, rapidly cross-referencing two different notebooks for her macroeconomics class.

When Gabriel saw the younger teens approaching, his face instantly softened. He closed his textbook, tossing his highlighter onto the table.

"<lang="cy" trans="How was it today, Julep?">Sut oedd hi heddiw, Julep?</lang>" Gabriel asked softly, his deep baritone keeping to the quiet, library-appropriate volume.

Julian pulled up to the end of the table. He didn't type on his AAC device; he just offered a small, exhausted, but genuine smile, flashing a quick thumbs-up.

"The sub in English Lit was from Anglesey," Maria whispered, pulling out a chair and dropping her heavy backpack onto the floor. "He totally put Kevin in his place, and then taught the whole class how to say Bore da."

Gwendolyn stopped writing, looking up with a sharp, incredibly impressed smirk. "A Welshman holding the line in English Lit? Gwych. (Brilliant.) We'll have to send him a thank-you note."

They stayed in the quiet sanctuary of the library for another forty-five minutes, allowing Julian's nervous system to completely decompress after the sensory load of the school day. He leaned his head against Maria's shoulder, watching his older siblings study, entirely at peace within the fully reassembled perimeter.

At 3:45 PM, Gwendolyn checked her watch and tapped the table. "Alright, garrison. Time to move."

The four teenagers packed up their bags and walked out of the library, heading toward the main PRT bus shelter at the edge of the college campus.

At exactly 3:52 PM, the heavy, high-floor transit bus pulled up to the curb. It wore the familiar, comforting tri-color livery of Peninsula Regional Transport—a crisp white roof, a thick dark maroon stripe, and a tan lower body.

Above the windshield, the mechanical flipdot sign cascaded with its rapid, rhythmic, incredibly soothing clack-clack-clack.

BLUE LINE

Clack-clack-clack.

TRANSPORTATION CENTER

The heavy pneumatic doors hissed open. The bus driver, a friendly older woman who knew their routine by heart, offered a warm smile.

Gabriel and Gwendolyn stepped up first, flashing their laminated WCC college IDs to the driver to cover the fare. Maria stepped up next, showing her Ewell High School ID.

"Lift's coming down, Julian!" the driver called out, hitting a heavy switch on the dashboard.

The mechanical whine of the wheelchair lift engaged, the heavy metal platform lowering smoothly to the curb. Julian backed his manual chair onto the lift, locking his brakes. With a hydraulic hum, he was hoisted aboard the high-floor bus.

Gabriel paid the fare for him, tapping Julian's high school ID against the farebox, while Maria guided the wheelchair into the securement bay, expertly snapping the heavy floor restraints into place.

Gabriel sat in the row directly behind Julian, while Gwendolyn sat across the aisle, scanning the rest of the passengers with her usual tactical vigilance. Maria sat in the flip-down jump seat directly beside Julian's wheel, their knees touching.

The air brakes hissed, and the heavy bus pulled away from the curb.

It was only a five-minute ride back to their neighborhood. It was a short, perfectly calibrated loop. But as Julian looked out the tinted window, feeling the heavy, grounding rumble of the diesel engine beneath his chair and the absolute certainty of his family surrounding him, he knew it was the best part of his day.
