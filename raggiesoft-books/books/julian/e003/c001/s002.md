The heavy classroom door remained firmly shut, separating Kevin and his disciplinary referral from the rest of the freshman English class.

Inside Room 114, the remaining twenty-nine students sat in complete, stunned silence.

They had just witnessed the strictest substitute in the building obliterate the class bully, and then immediately drop to a crouch to softly comfort the quiet boy in the wheelchair using an unrecognizable, rhythmic language.

Mr. Evans walked slowly back to the chalkboard. He picked up a piece of white chalk and turned to face the room.

He could see the curiosity practically vibrating in the teenagers' eyes. They weren't hostile; they were completely captivated.

Mr. Evans looked over the rows of desks, his gaze landing gently on the back corner.

He offered a slight, respectful tilt of his head toward Julian, Maria, and Clara.

"Julian. Maria. Clara," Mr. Evans addressed them quietly, treating the fourteen-year-olds with the diplomatic respect of equals. "Ignorance breeds fear, and fear breeds cruelty. With your permission... may we demystify the language for them?"

Julian sat comfortably against the backrest of his manual chair, the lingering adrenaline of his near-meltdown entirely flushed from his system.

He looked at Maria and Clara, who flanked him on either side. The identical twins offered him small, synchronized, encouraging nods.

Julian pulled his AAC device forward, keeping the synthesizer locked to his safe harbor.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="YES. TEACH THEM.">OES. DYSGWCH NHW.</lang></aac>

The digitized Welsh echoed through the room. A few students in the front row leaned forward, completely fascinated by the metallic, rolling consonants.

Mr. Evans smiled warmly. He turned to the chalkboard and wrote a single word in sharp, precise cursive: Cymraeg.

"What you just heard," Mr. Evans announced, projecting his voice clearly across the room, "is Cymraeg. In English, it is known as Welsh. It is a Celtic language, spoken natively in the country of Wales, which lies to the west of England. It is one of the oldest living languages in Europe."

He tapped the chalk against the board. "To an untrained ear, it sounds alien. The alphabet is different. We use letters like 'W' and 'Y' as vowels. We roll our R's, and we use a sound—the double L—that does not exist in the English language."

The substitute wrote two basic phrases on the board: Bore da and Diolch.

"Bore da means 'Good morning,'" Mr. Evans instructed, pronouncing it clearly. "And Diolch means 'Thank you.' Go ahead. Try it."

A disjointed, thoroughly Americanized chorus of Bore da and Diolch echoed back to him. The class was entirely hooked.

The rigid, boring English reading packet was forgotten, replaced by the thrilling discovery of a secret, ancient code right in their own classroom.

Julian watched his classmates attempt the pronunciation. A small, genuine smile touched the corners of his mouth.

The language of his sanctuary wasn't being mocked anymore; it was being respected.

"Now," Mr. Evans continued, a distinct spark of national pride lighting up his stern features. "The Welsh are very proud of our heritage. In fact, we hold the title for the longest, most complicated town name in all of Europe. It is a badge of honor for any true Welshman to be able to pronounce it in a single breath."

He turned to the chalkboard, preparing to write the fifty-eight-letter behemoth across the slate. "The town is called—"

"Llanfairpwllgwyngyllgogerychwyrndrobwllllantysiliogogogoch," two voices chimed in perfect, flawless unison from the back row.

Mr. Evans froze, the chalk hovering an inch from the blackboard.

The entire class turned around in their seats.

Maria and Clara sat perfectly straight at their desks, flanking Julian's wheelchair. They hadn't stumbled. They hadn't paused for breath. They had rolled the complex, breathy ll consonants and the guttural ch with absolute, breathtaking, synchronized perfection.

They sounded exactly like Eira Rowlands in stereo.

Julian tapped his armrest rapidly, letting out a bright, highly vocalized laugh of pure delight.

Mr. Evans slowly lowered his piece of chalk. He stared at the fourteen-year-old American identical twins in absolute, unadulterated shock.

"I'm sorry... could you translate that for the class, girls?"

Maria and Clara turned to look at each other. Identical, slightly evil grins broke across their faces. They had been taught the translation by Eira years ago, and they didn't just know it by heart—they knew exactly how to weaponize their twin dynamic for maximum dramatic effect.

They turned back to the substitute teacher, ping-ponging the translation back and forth, one word at a time, without a single microsecond of hesitation.

"It," Maria started. "Translates," Clara continued. "To," Maria said. "Saint," Clara fired back. "Mary's," "Church," "in," "the," "hollow," "of," "the," "white," "hazel," "near," "to," "the," "rapid," "whirlpool," "of," "Llantysilio," "of," "the," "red," "cave."

Clara delivered the final word with a sharp, satisfied nod. The stereophonic performance was completely flawless and undeniably unnerving.

The classroom erupted into spontaneous, impressed applause. A boy near the windows let out a low whistle of disbelief.

The twins waited for the noise to die down, offering a casual, very American shrug that contrasted completely with their flawless Celtic ping-pong match. They had never stepped foot outside of Virginia—save for the terrifying drive to Ohio to bring Julian home—but they had spent seven years entirely immersed in the Rowlands family perimeter.

"But if you actually live there, nobody says the whole thing," Maria clarified, her standard American accent returning to match her sister's. "Julian's mom says it's mostly for the tourists and the train station sign. Locals just call it Llanfairpwll, or Llanfair PG if you're in a hurry."

Mr. Evans leaned against his heavy wooden desk, completely abandoning his strict, disciplinarian persona.

He let out a rich, genuine laugh, shaking his head in sheer amazement.

"Spot on, girls. Absolutely spot on," Mr. Evans praised, his eyes shining.

He looked specifically at the three teenagers in the back row.

"And as it happens... I know exactly what that rapid whirlpool looks like. My name is Jordan Evans, and I was born and raised two streets over from that very church."

Julian’s breath hitched. He looked at the substitute teacher, his eyes going incredibly wide.

Of all the substitute teachers in the entire James City County public school district. Of all the classrooms, on all the days. The universe had dropped a man from his exact home village right onto the frontline to protect him.

Julian pulled his hands off his armrests and quickly typed on his AAC device. He didn't use the English output, and he didn't need to. He was speaking directly to a man who understood his perimeter perfectly.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="I WAS BORN THERE TOO. WE MOVED TO AMERICA WHEN I WAS SIX. YOU ARE THE BEST TEACHER EVER.">CEFAIS FY NGENI YNO HEFYD. SYMUDON NI I AMERICA PAN OEDDWN I'N CHWECH. CHI YW YR ATHRO GORAU ERIOED.</lang></aac>

Jordan Evans read the translation from the cadence of the robotic voice.

He froze, his eyes widening slightly as the immense weight of that statement hit him.

This wasn't just a boy clinging to his mother's heritage. This was a native son of Anglesey. This was a boy who had spent the first six years of his life breathing the cold salt air of the Menai Strait before being uprooted and brought to America.

It made the boy's desperate, fierce attachment to the Welsh language instantly, infinitely more profound. It was his first home.

A profound, deeply moved smile softened the older man's strict features.

He placed his right hand over his heart and offered a slight, formal bow to the boy in the wheelchair.

"<lang="cy" trans="It is an honor to meet you, Julian,">Mae'n anrhydedd i cwrdd â chi, Julian,</lang>" Mr. Evans replied warmly.

He held Julian's gaze, his voice dropping to a register of absolute, validating safety.

"<lang="cy" trans="And remember... no matter how far you travel, you are always home in our language.">A chofiwch... waeth pa mor bell ydych chi'n teithio, rydych chi bob amser adref yn ein hiaith ni.</lang>"

Julian let out a long, shuddering sigh of absolute relief, his left hand finding Maria's and his right hand finding Clara's, holding onto his anchors tight.

The bell rang overhead, signaling the end of the period.

But as the freshman class packed up their bags, no one was talking about the English reading packet, and no one was talking about Kevin getting sent to the office.

They were all talking, and trying to pronounce, the incredible, ancient language that belonged to the quiet boy in the back row.
