## 10:00 AM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The I-64 Corridor

August in the Virginia tidewater is not just a season; it is a physical, suffocating weight. The morning air outside the Ewell house was thick with a sweltering, 90-degree humidity that made the horizon shimmer over the asphalt.

But inside the heavy, customized wheelchair van tearing westward up Interstate 64, the climate was absolutely, rigidly controlled.

The air conditioning roared from the dashboard vents, keeping the cabin incredibly cool. At fifteen years old, Julian had grown taller, his frame lengthening into a young man’s, but his reliance on the sensory perimeter of his family was just as absolute as it had been when he was a child. He sat securely locked into the floor restraints in the center of the van, wearing his dark navy Aran wool sweater—the exact one his mother had bought him for the quiet Réveillon of '96—despite the brutal Virginia heat outside. It wasn't about temperature; it was about the heavy, grounding weight of the tight stitches holding his nervous system together.

On his left, occupying the flip-down jump seat, sat fifteen-year-old Clara. On his right sat her identical twin, Maria. Their knees were pressed flush against the thick rubber tires of his manual chair. Maria had her hand resting casually over Julian’s on his armrest, her thumb gently tracing the familiar knuckles. It was a silent, continuous tactile tether that kept the anxiety of the impending international flight at bay.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Are you ready for the plane, Julep?">Tu es prêt pour l'avion, Julep?</lang>" Maria asked softly in flawless Québécois French, her voice barely rising above the hum of the van's heavy tires against the highway.

Julian didn't reach for his AAC device. He just looked at her, his eyes bright with a mix of intense, nervous energy and profound excitement, and offered a sharp, definitive nod. He reached up with his free hand and tapped the heavy, professional-grade isolation headphones resting around his neck. He had his armor. He was ready.

In the back row of the van, the nineteen-year-old twins were already operating in full tactical deployment mode. Gabriel had his broad shoulders slouched against the window, but his eyes were constantly scanning the highway traffic. Beside him, Gwendolyn had a thick, three-ring binder open on her lap. It was the master itinerary, heavily highlighted and tabbed, containing the exact layouts for Richmond International, the nightmare transfer at JFK, and the arrival terminals at Manchester.

"Flight 412 out of RIC is on time," Gwendolyn announced, her Welsh lilt crisp and commanding. "Richard, if we maintain this speed, we hit the Sandston exit in twelve minutes. Gabriel, you and I are on baggage duty the second the doors open. We leave the heavy lifting to the skycaps for the checked bags, but the wheelchair gate-check tag is our primary objective."

"I've got it covered, Gwen," Gabriel rumbled, his deep baritone projecting an unshakeable, frontline confidence. "Nobody touches his chair until we clear it."

Up front, the patriarch of the blended family gripped the steering wheel. Richard Harrison navigated the heavy vehicle with the quiet, effortless reliability that had made him the mortar of their family. He glanced at the rearview mirror, checking on the teenagers in the back, before looking over at his wife in the passenger seat.

Eira was staring out the passenger window, watching the dense, green Virginia pine trees blur past. She was dressed sharply for travel, her British passport and her U.S. Permanent Resident card securely zipped into her leather crossbody bag. But her fingers were tapping a restless, anxious rhythm against her knee.

It had been nine years since she had stepped foot on the Isle of Anglesey. She had left a crumbling marriage and a volatile life to try and force an American dream that had ultimately turned into a nightmare.

But she wasn't returning as a defeated exile. She was returning with Vanguard Horizon royalty money in the bank, a beautiful, fully accessible stone cottage waiting near the red cave, a fiercely devoted husband by her side, and all five of her children completely, legally safe.

"<lang="cy" trans="We are almost there, Eira,">Rydyn ni bron yno, Eira,</lang>" Richard murmured softly, switching seamlessly into her native tongue to soothe her racing mind.

Eira stopped tapping her knee. She reached across the center console, resting her hand warmly over Richard's on the steering wheel. She let out a long, shuddering exhale, a brilliant smile finally breaking through her travel anxiety.

"<lang="cy" trans="We are going home,">Rydyn ni'n mynd adref,</lang>" Eira whispered back.

Up ahead, the dense tree line finally broke. The towering, brutalist concrete structure of the Richmond International Airport air traffic control tower pierced the sweltering summer sky over Sandston.

Gabriel sat forward in the back row, his posture instantly straightening. Gwendolyn snapped her tactical binder shut with a definitive clack. In the center of the van, Julian pulled his heavy isolation headphones up over his ears, drowning out the world and plunging himself into absolute, predictable silence.

The perimeter was locked. The garrison was officially deploying.

## 10:30 AM • Eira Rowlands — The Pre-Clearance

The automatic sliding doors of Richmond International Airport parted, and the Rowlands-Harrison garrison stepped out of the suffocating, 90-degree Virginia heat and into the echoing, air-conditioned expanse of the departure terminal.

For a typical family, the airport was just a stressful preamble to a vacation. But for Julian, the terminal was a sensory minefield. The high ceilings bounced the chaotic, overlapping sounds of thousands of people in a dozen different directions. The sharp clack-clack of hard plastic suitcase wheels rolling over the terrazzo floors, the blaring, distorted overhead announcements, and the chaotic hum of stressed travelers formed a suffocating wall of noise.

Julian reached up and pulled his heavy, Vanguard Horizon-funded isolation headphones firmly over his ears.

The chaotic roar of the terminal instantly vanished, reduced to a dull, manageable murmur. He let out a long, grounding exhale, his shoulders dropping an inch.

"I've got the cart," Richard announced, grabbing a heavy metal luggage trolley from the rack near the doors.

Gabriel effortlessly hoisted their three massive, scuffed canvas suitcases onto the trolley, stacking them with the precision of a frontline quartermaster.

"Formation," Gwendolyn ordered softly.

They didn't need to practice it. Clara stepped flush against Julian's left wheel, her hand resting over his. Maria took his right, her shoulder brushing against his arm. Gabriel pushed the luggage cart directly in front of them, acting as a physical snowplow to force oblivious travelers to part ways, while Eira and Gwendolyn took the lead, heading straight for the international ticketing desk of Meridian Airlines.

When they reached the front of the queue, the Meridian ticket agent offered a polite, professional smile. "Checking in for Flight 412 to New York, connecting to Manchester?"

Eira unzipped her leather crossbody bag. She reached inside and pulled out a thick, uniform stack of seven dark-blue United States passports, sliding them across the laminate counter.

Julian, watching closely from his wheelchair, frowned. A sudden, sharp spike of anxiety hit his chest. He pulled one side of his headphones back just enough to hear, his fingers quickly finding the keys of his AAC device.

<aac>WHY THE AMERICAN ONES?</aac> the robotic voice asked, the volume kept low but the urgency undeniable. <aac>WE ARE GOING HOME. WE ARE WELSH.</aac>

To Julian's incredibly literal, traumatized mind, using the American passports felt like a betrayal. It felt like holding onto the identity of Thomas Cross, instead of claiming the heritage that had literally saved his life.

Eira paused, turning away from the desk to look at her son. She understood the panic instantly. She didn't dismiss it.

"<lang="cy" trans="It's alright, Julep,">Mae'n iawn, Julep,</lang>" Eira whispered, stepping close to his chair and speaking in their native tongue so the ticket agent wouldn't understand. "<lang="cy" trans="This is only for the airline.">Dim ond i'r cwmni hedfan yw hyn.</lang>"

"She's right, Julian," Gwendolyn added, leaning in. "The airline agent just needs to match the names on the tickets to a valid photo ID to let us on the plane. But when we land in Manchester, we are walking right up to the UK Border desk, and we are handing them our burgundy British passports. We enter our country as citizens. I promise."

Julian stared at his sister, processing the tactical logic. The American passport wasn't an identity; it was just a boarding pass. He let out a quiet sigh, nodding once, and slipped the earcup of his headphones back into place.

Eira turned back to the agent, sliding a pristine, red-tabbed manila folder across the counter alongside the passports.

"Six checked bags," Eira stated briskly. "And the medical accommodation clearance for the manual wheelchair. I spoke with your accessibility director, Mr. Vance, on Tuesday. The profile is already noted in our booking."

The agent opened the folder, her eyes scanning the pre-approved airline forms and the letter from Julian's pediatrician. She typed rapidly into her terminal, pulling up the family's reservation.

"Ah, yes. I see it right here, Mrs. Rowlands," the agent confirmed, her tone shifting into one of profound professional respect. She didn't offer a generic airport transport chair; she simply reached under her desk and pulled out a bright, neon-orange gate-check tag attached to an elastic string.

She scribbled the flight number on it and handed it directly to Gabriel, along with the printed boarding passes.

"Gate-check is cleared. Just leave the chair at the end of the jet bridge, and the ramp crew at JFK has already been notified to bring it back up for the layover."

"Thank you," Gabriel said, offering a polite, appreciative nod.

He walked back to Julian and carefully looped the bright orange tag around the heavy metal crossbar under the seat of the manual wheelchair, pulling the elastic tight.

Julian looked down at the neon tag. To anyone else in the airport, it was just a piece of cheap cardboard. But to Julian, it was a profound symbol of his family's absolute, Frictionless competence. The world was chaotic, but his perimeter was bulletproof.

"Alright, Palace," Gabriel said, clapping his hands together once as Richard took control of the boarding passes. "The luggage is gone, and the chair is secured. Next stop: the metal detectors."

Julian squeezed Maria and Clara's hands. The perimeter moved forward.

## 10:45 AM • Gabriel Rowlands — The Checkpoint

Airport security was a loud, clattering bottleneck of private security contractors, rattling plastic bins, and the monotonous, electronic beep of the walk-through magnetometers.

The chaotic chokepoint was a sensory minefield. Businessmen in oversized suits impatiently checked their pagers, and families wrestled with bulky carry-on bags. For Julian, even with his heavy isolation headphones firmly in place, the visual noise of the security lane was incredibly overwhelming.

The garrison moved to the front of the line, immediately executing their established protocol.

Gabriel and Richard stepped forward to handle the physical logistics. They grabbed a stack of gray plastic bins, rapidly emptying their pockets, removing their watches, and loading the family's carry-on duffels onto the rubber rollers of the X-ray belt.

Eira stepped up to the metal detector, presenting the boarding passes to the private security guard manning the archway.

"The boy in the wheelchair," the security guard said, gesturing lazily with a handheld metal-detecting wand. "He's going to set off the archway in that thing. Can he stand and walk through? If not, I need to wand him down and inspect the chair cushions."

"He cannot bear any weight on his legs, and he is severely autistic," Eira stated, her voice projecting a calm, absolute, and zero-tolerance authority. She didn't ask for permission; she dictated the terms of engagement. "He cannot be touched by a stranger, or he will go into severe muscular spasticity. My daughter will carry him through the archway, and my son will bring the empty chair around for your inspection."

The guard blinked, momentarily taken aback by the frictionless, military precision of the mother's instructions. He nodded, stepping aside to open the hinged bypass gate next to the metal detector. "Alright, ma'am. Bring the chair through here."

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Come on, Julep. It's time,">Allez, Julep. C'est l'heure,</lang>" Gwendolyn murmured, stepping to the front of his wheelchair.

Julian hated leaving the safety of his customized seat in public, but he trusted his sister implicitly. He unclasped his seatbelt and leaned forward slightly.

Gwendolyn possessed a flawless, practiced mechanical leverage. She wrapped her arms securely around his torso and under his knees, lifting the fifteen-year-old boy out of the chair with a smooth, effortless exertion of core strength. She cradled him securely against her chest, her steady heartbeat providing an immediate, grounding rhythm against his ribs.

The moment Julian was airborne, his anchors moved in.

Maria stepped flush against his right side, and Clara against his left. They didn't just walk next to him; Maria reached up and wrapped her hand securely around his right hand, while Clara locked her fingers through his left.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="We are here,">On est là,</lang>" Maria whispered, keeping her eyes locked on his.

Together, the three girls walked through the metal detector archway in perfect, synchronized tandem. Julian was entirely suspended, unable to touch the floor, but because he was physically tethered to Gwendolyn, Clara, and Maria, his unmedicated nervous system remained completely calm. He didn't flinch. The perimeter held him together.

Whoosh. They passed through the archway in absolute silence. No alarms. No red lights.

Immediately behind them, Gabriel pushed the empty manual wheelchair through the bypass gate. The security guard ran the handheld wand quickly over the seat and the backrest, glancing under the frame.

"Looks good. You're clear," the guard confirmed, stepping away.

Gabriel didn't wait a single second. He pushed the chair directly behind the metal detector, locking the brakes into place. Gwendolyn smoothly pivoted and lowered Julian back into his customized seat.

The moment Julian's weight settled back into the familiar, contoured cushions, he let out a long, heavy exhale. He reached down and clicked his seatbelt securely into place. Maria immediately adjusted the dark navy Aran wool sweater over his chest, while Clara ensured his heavy headphones were still perfectly positioned.

"Well done, everyone," Richard praised quietly, gathering their cleared duffel bags from the end of the X-ray belt and passing them out.

Gabriel grabbed the push-handles of Julian's chair. As they walked away from the clattering security checkpoint and merged into the wider, carpeted concourse of the domestic departure gates, the suffocating tension of the morning finally began to evaporate.

The Richmond departure lounge felt infinitely calmer. Sunlight poured through the massive, floor-to-ceiling windows, illuminating the tarmac where a sleek, silver-and-blue twin-engine jetliner belonging to the regional coastal carrier was parked at Gate B4.

Julian pulled his headphones down around his neck. The ambient noise here was a quiet, manageable hum. He looked out the window at the jet, a sudden, thrilling spark of adrenaline hitting his chest.

He wasn't trapped in the back of a freezing car. He wasn't hiding in his bedroom.

He pulled his AAC device onto his lap, his fingers flying across the keys with a bright, eager energy. He selected the Welsh synthesizer, the language of the home he hadn't seen in nine years.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="ARE WE FLYING NOW?">RYDYN NI'N HEDFAN NAWR?</lang></aac>

Gabriel chuckled, a massive, genuine smile breaking across his face as he parked the chair near the boarding door.

"<lang="cy" trans="Yes, brother,">Ydyn, brawd,</lang>" Gabriel confirmed, clapping a heavy hand affectionately onto Julian's shoulder. " Rydyn ni'n hedfan nawr. "

Eira stood beside Richard, looking out at the aircraft, her eyes shining with unshed tears of profound relief. The hardest logistical hurdles of the morning were behind them. They had successfully navigated the ticket counters, the luggage scales, and the security gauntlet without a single breach in their armor.

Next stop: New York. And then, the Menai Strait.

## 11:30 AM • Julian Rowlands — The Ascent

The transition from the customized wheelchair into the narrow, fabric-upholstered airline seat was handled with the same frictionless, military precision the family applied to everything else.

Gwendolyn carried Julian down the jet bridge, transferring him seamlessly into the middle seat of Row 12. Gabriel immediately took the empty manual chair back up the ramp, ensuring the baggage handlers saw the neon-orange tag and loaded it securely into the forward cargo hold.

By the time the heavy cabin door sealed shut and the twin-engine jet pushed back from Gate B4, the perimeter was perfectly established.

Julian sat in the middle seat, his heavy isolation headphones resting around his neck. Maria sat in the window seat to his right, while Clara took the aisle on his left. Directly behind them, Gabriel and Gwendolyn formed the rear guard, and across the aisle, Richard and Eira kept a watchful eye on the formation.

As the jet taxied toward the runway, the low, mechanical whine of the engines began to vibrate through the floorboards. Julian reached out, firmly grasping Maria’s hand with his right hand, and Clara’s with his left.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Here we go,">Ici on y va,</lang>" Maria whispered in Québécois French, offering a bright, excited smile as the plane turned onto the active runway.

The jet engines roared, a massive, deafening surge of power that pushed Julian firmly back into his seat. As the aircraft accelerated down the tarmac and the nose lifted sharply into the sweltering Virginia sky, the intense G-force pressed down on his chest.

For Julian, the heavy gravitational pull didn't trigger his anxiety. It felt exactly like a massive, full-body deep-pressure cuddle. His unmedicated muscles relaxed entirely into the incline of the climb.

He looked out the small oval window past Maria, watching the sprawling, green tidewater of James City County rapidly shrink into a patchwork of tiny neighborhoods and winding rivers.

As the ground fell away, a sudden, vivid memory surfaced in his mind, piercing through the noise of the cabin.

He had not been on an airplane since he was six years old.

He remembered the flight out of the United Kingdom, nine years ago. He remembered sitting in a much larger airplane, his small legs dangling over the edge of the seat. But mostly, he remembered the man sitting next to him.

Tom Cross hadn't been a monster back then. He had been a smiling, sharply dressed father, holding his six-year-old son's hand as they flew across the Atlantic. Tom had leaned over, his voice devoid of the terrifying, paranoid rage that would eventually consume him, and promised Julian that America was going to be a grand, beautiful adventure.

It was a profound, deeply complicated memory. For years, Tom's escalating abuse had completely overwritten the man he used to be. The abduction had violently severed whatever remaining illusion of a father-son bond existed.

But sitting in Row 12, rocketing up to thirty thousand feet, Julian didn't feel paralyzed by the memory. He felt an overwhelming, undeniable sense of closure.

The American adventure his father had promised had shattered. The promises were empty, and the man who made them was dead.

But out of that wreckage, Julian hadn't just survived. He had gained Richard, who sat across the aisle reading a paperback novel, completely unfazed by the turbulence. He had gained Clara and Maria, who were currently anchoring his hands, perfectly in sync with his breathing. He had watched Gabriel and Gwendolyn forge themselves into unbreakable shields of steel.

Julian looked away from the window, turning his head to look at Clara on his left.

He didn't need to pull his AAC device onto his lap to communicate. He just squeezed her hand twice, offering a brilliant, radiant smile that completely reached his eyes.

"<lang="cy" trans="We are going home, Julep,">Rydyn ni'n mynd adref, Julep,</lang>" Clara whispered in flawless Welsh, instantly reading the overwhelming joy on his face.

Julian let out a happy, rhythmic vocalization that was completely swallowed by the ambient roar of the jet engines. He pulled his heavy headphones up over his ears, leaning his head against Maria’s shoulder, and closed his eyes.

He wasn't flying into the terrifying unknown with a man who refused to learn his mother's language. He was flying back to the very roots of his sanctuary, completely surrounded by a garrison that spoke his language perfectly.

The flight to New York’s JFK airport was a quick, ninety-minute hop up the eastern seaboard. It was just enough time for Julian's nervous system to completely reset before they had to face the ultimate, chaotic gauntlet of the international transfer terminal.
