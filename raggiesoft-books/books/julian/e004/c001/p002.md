## 1:00 PM • Gwendolyn Rowlands — The Hub

The wheels of the twin-engine jet hit the tarmac at John F. Kennedy International Airport with a heavy, shuddering thud, the thrust reversers roaring as the aircraft rapidly decelerated.

Julian kept his eyes closed, his hands firmly anchored in Maria and Clara’s grips. The sudden deceleration pushed him forward against his seatbelt, but the familiar, symmetrical pressure on his hands kept his unmedicated nervous system grounded.

When the seatbelt sign finally chimed off, the cabin erupted into the usual frantic, claustrophobic scramble of passengers reaching for the overhead bins.

The garrison didn't move an inch.

Gabriel stood up into the aisle, his broad shoulders effectively blocking anyone from crowding Row 12. "Hold the line," he rumbled quietly. "We wait for the chair."

Ten minutes later, the cabin had emptied. Gwendolyn effortlessly lifted Julian, carrying him down the jet bridge where Gabriel was already waiting with the customized manual wheelchair. The bright neon-orange gate-check tag was still securely tied to the crossbar.

Gwendolyn lowered him into the seat, and Julian immediately felt the tension bleed out of his spine. The airplane seat had been tolerable, but his chair was an extension of his own body. It was his mobile fortress.

"Alright, Palace," Richard said, checking his watch as he took the lead. "We are in Terminal 2. Our flight to Manchester leaves out of the International Arrivals Building, which handles the foreign carrier departures. We have to take the curbside shuttle."

Eira nodded, her expression hardening into absolute, tactical focus. "Headphones on, Julep. It's going to be loud."

Julian pulled the heavy isolation cups securely over his ears.

They marched up the jet bridge and into the terminal. JFK was not a quiet, regional airport. It was a massive, echoing cavern of stressed, shouting travelers, blaring announcements, and rushing electric carts. But the real assault began when they bypassed the baggage claims—since their luggage was checked through to Manchester—and pushed through the automatic doors onto the curbside pickup lane.

The August heat in New York was punishing, radiating off the concrete in shimmering, exhaust-choked waves. The smell of burnt jet fuel, hot asphalt, and diesel smoke was instantly suffocating.

A heavy, articulated shuttle bus hissed to a stop at the curb, its doors folding open with a loud, mechanical clatter.

"Ramp!" Gabriel shouted over the din of the traffic, catching the attention of the bus driver.

The driver stepped out of his seat, deploying a heavy metal plate onto the curb. Gabriel didn't wait for assistance. He expertly navigated Julian’s chair up the steep incline and into the designated wheelchair bay on the bus. He engaged the chair's brakes and immediately locked the heavy, yellow floor tie-downs onto the frame.

Gwendolyn, Richard, and Eira filed in, while Clara and Maria immediately took the flip-down seats directly beside Julian's wheels.

The doors hissed shut, and the bus lurched forward.

For Julian, the ten-minute ride around the perimeter of JFK was a sensory nightmare, even with the headphones blocking the noise. The bus rattled violently over the uneven tarmac, the suspension groaning at every turn. The heavy smell of diesel fumes seeped through the rubber seals of the doors, making him nauseous.

He squeezed his eyes shut, his hands balling into fists on his armrests. The phantom sway of a moving vehicle was clawing at the edges of his mind, threatening to drag him back to the dark, freezing roads of Ohio.

A hand gently covered his left fist. He opened his eyes.

Clara was leaning forward, her face mere inches from his. She didn't speak—she knew he couldn't hear her through the thick foam of the headphones—but she offered a slow, deliberate, and incredibly exaggerated smile.

On his right, Maria tapped his knee twice, holding up her hand and counting down on her fingers. Three. Two. One. The bus lurched to a halt, the air brakes hissing loudly.

"We're here," Gabriel announced, moving swiftly to unclip the floor restraints. "Let's move."

They rolled down the ramp and pushed through a set of towering glass doors into the International Departures Hall.

If the curbside was a sensory assault, the international terminal was a sprawling, echoing metropolis. It was a kaleidoscope of humanity. Tens of thousands of people swarmed the check-in desks, speaking dozens of different languages. The vaulted ceilings trapped the noise, creating a continuous, low-frequency roar.

But Eira Rowlands did not falter. She was entirely in her element. She moved with a crisp, undeniable authority, her eyes locked on the massive, mechanical flip-board high above the concourse that clattered rapidly as it updated gate numbers.

"Gate 4," Eira directed, pointing down a long, crowded corridor. "Richard, Gabriel, clear the path."

The two men moved to the front of the formation. They didn't push or shove, but their combined size and determined stride caused the crowds of international travelers to naturally part like the Red Sea.

Gwendolyn took the push-handles of Julian's chair, maintaining a brisk, smooth pace, while the twins kept their shoulders flush against the wheels, physically blocking any stray luggage carts from getting too close.

They bypassed the duty-free shops and the crowded terminal bars, marching relentlessly until the dense crowds finally began to thin out near the end of the concourse.

Gate 4 was a spacious, carpeted waiting area with floor-to-ceiling windows looking out over the massive wingspan of a wide-body, transatlantic jetliner.

Gwendolyn wheeled Julian into a quiet corner against the glass, pivoting the chair so his back was to the wall. He had a clear, unobstructed view of the entire gate area, and no one could approach him from behind.

"Perimeter secured," Gwendolyn said, letting out a sharp exhale as she dropped her heavy carry-on bag onto the carpet.

Julian pulled his headphones down to rest around his neck. The ambient noise here was manageable. He looked up at his family. They were all sweating, their clothes rumpled from the oppressive heat of the shuttle bus, but their eyes were bright with a fierce, collective triumph.

They had survived the hub.

Julian pulled his AAC device onto his lap. His hands were shaking slightly from the adrenaline of the transit, but he navigated effortlessly to the Welsh synthesizer.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="JFK IS TOO LOUD.">MAE JFK YN DWEUD Y RHYW BETH.</lang></aac>

Gabriel let out a booming, exhausted laugh, collapsing into one of the vinyl airport chairs. "<lang="cy" trans="You are exactly right, brother.">Rydych chi'n llygad eich lle, brawd,</lang>" "It's a madhouse."

Eira stepped up to the window, placing her hand gently on Julian's shoulder. She looked out at the massive international jet sitting on the tarmac, the ground crews actively loading the cargo holds for the long flight over the Atlantic.

"It's loud, Julep," Eira agreed softly, her British accent returning to its natural, unhurried cadence now that the frantic rush was over. "But that plane right there? That is the very last step. When we get off that plane, we will be standing in Manchester."

Julian looked out the window at the jet. He reached out, his fingers finding Maria's hand.

He didn't need to type anything else. He was ready to go home.

## 6:15 PM • Eira Rowlands — The Boarding

The static crackle of the public address system echoed through the International Departures Hall, followed by the gate agent's voice announcing the pre-boarding process for passengers requiring extra time or special assistance.

Gabriel immediately hoisted his heavy canvas duffel onto his shoulder, offering Richard a sharp, ready nod. The garrison was moving out.

But in the center of their formation, Julian’s manual wheelchair jerked to a sudden, rigid halt.

Julian’s hands began patting his chest, then his lap, his eyes going wide with a sudden, frantic spike of panic. He looked toward Eira’s leather crossbody bag, his breathing turning shallow and rapid. He pulled his AAC device squarely onto his lap, his fingers hitting the keys with urgent, heavy strikes.

<aac>PASSPORT.</aac> the robotic voice barked, the volume cutting through the ambient hum of the terminal. <aac>I DO NOT HAVE MY PASSPORT.</aac>

He remembered the ticketing desk in Richmond. He remembered his mother sliding the stack of dark-blue booklets across the counter to the agent. To his literal, anxious mind, the gate door was another insurmountable border. If he didn't have his passport in his hand right now, they were going to leave him behind.

Before the panic could fully trigger a muscular spasm, Gwendolyn was there.

She dropped her carry-on bag onto the carpet and immediately knelt directly in front of his wheelchair, bringing herself perfectly into his line of sight. She didn't try to reason with him from a distance or tell him to calm down. She stepped directly into his space, wrapping both of her arms securely around his chest and pulling him into a firm, unyielding deep-pressure hug.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Quiet, Julep,">Tranquille, Julep,</lang>" Gwendolyn murmured, her voice an incredibly soft, grounding anchor amidst the chaos of the airport. She held him tightly, her steady heartbeat pressing against his ribs.

Julian gasped, his rigid shoulders fighting the hold for only a fraction of a second before completely melting into his older sister's embrace. He buried his face against her shoulder, his trembling hands gripping the fabric of her sweater.

"<lang="cy" trans="You do not need the passport for the plane, love,">Nid oes angen y pasbort arnoch chi ar gyfer yr awyren, cariad,</lang>" Gwendolyn explained in a low, soothing Welsh lilt, rocking him gently. "We just need the paper boarding pass to get on the aircraft. That's it. Mom has your British passport zipped away, completely safe. You will hold it tomorrow morning when we land, and you will hand it to the guard to go home. I promise you are not being left behind."

Julian let out a long, shuddering exhale against her shoulder. The terrifying logic puzzle in his mind had been solved. He squeezed her tightly one last time before leaning back, offering a small, exhausted nod.

Gwendolyn smiled, smoothing a stray piece of hair from his forehead. " Allez. Let's get on this plane."

The transition down the jet bridge was executed flawlessly. Gwendolyn seamlessly transferred Julian from his customized chair into the middle seat of Row 12, while Gabriel parked the manual wheelchair at the threshold of the aircraft door, making absolutely certain the baggage handlers saw the neon-orange gate-check tag before he stepped inside.

Within minutes, the perimeter was locked. Julian sat securely in his seat, his heavy isolation headphones resting comfortably around his neck, perfectly flanked by Maria on his right and Clara on his left. Across the aisle, Eira settled into her window seat, leaning her head against Richard’s shoulder with a profound, exhausted sigh of relief.

Directly behind Julian, Gabriel and Gwendolyn slid into Row 13.

The heavy, reinforced cabin door was pulled shut with a definitive, mechanical thud. The ambient noise of the airport vanished, replaced by the deep, resonant hum of the wide-body jet's auxiliary power unit and the rush of the overhead air conditioning.

Gabriel stowed his duffel under the seat in front of him and sat back. He let out a massive, heavy breath, the intense, volatile frontline energy that had kept his muscles coiled like springs all day finally, completely bleeding out of his jaw and shoulders.

Beside him, Gwendolyn closed her tactical binder, slipping it into the seatback pocket.

The nineteen-year-old twins looked at each other. They didn't need to speak. They had spent their entire childhoods as hyper-vigilant human shields, constantly scanning their environment for the heavy footsteps of their father, and later, standing as the impenetrable wall that kept the world from overwhelming their little brother.

But right now, the doors were sealed. The threat was entirely gone.

Gabriel shifted the armrest between them up and out of the way. He slid closer to the middle seat, wrapping his heavy, broad arm securely around his twin sister's shoulders.

Gwendolyn didn't hesitate. The brilliant, calculating tactician immediately let her guard down, leaning her entire body weight against Gabriel's side. She rested her head comfortably against his chest, tucking her feet up onto the edge of her seat, and closed her eyes.

It was a quiet, domestic cuddle—the exact same way they slept every night in their shared bedroom in Virginia to keep the ghosts at bay. But here, sitting on a transatlantic jetliner taxiing toward the runway, the embrace felt infinitely lighter.

Gabriel rested his chin against the top of her head, his eyes tracking the tarmac out the window. They were flying over the ocean, leaving the remnants of Thomas Cross's America entirely behind them. They were going back to the cobblestones, the cold salt air, and the language of their roots.

" Rydyn ni'n mynd adref, " Gabriel whispered into his sister's hair.

Gwendolyn smiled against his shirt, her breathing slowing to match his steady, rhythmic pulse. They were finally going home.

## 9:30 PM • Julian Rowlands — The Red-Eye

The interior of the wide-body jet underwent a slow, deliberate transformation as it reached cruising altitude high over the dark expanse of the Atlantic Ocean.

The flight attendants moved quietly down the aisles, collecting the remnants of the dinner service, before the harsh overhead cabin lights finally clicked off. They were replaced by the soft, dim glow of the floor runners and the scattered, pale beams of individual reading lights.

The chaotic, frantic energy of the departure had completely dissipated. The only sound left was the continuous, heavy, white-noise roar of the massive jet engines outside the fuselage.

Across the aisle from the teenagers, Richard Harrison rested his head against the high back of his seat. He had an open paperback novel resting on his lap, but he hadn't turned a page in over twenty minutes. He was staring out the small oval window into the pitch-black void of the night sky.

At forty-two years old, Richard had never left the United States. His entire life had been defined by the borders of Virginia—teaching music therapy in the local public schools, raising his twin daughters after his first wife passed, and eventually navigating the corporate broadcasting ladder.

But as he looked at the dark glass, the sheer magnitude of what they were doing settled heavily into his chest. He wasn't just taking a vacation. He was crossing an ocean to help his wife reclaim a homeland that had been stolen from her, and to bring a traumatized fifteen-year-old boy back to the only place he had ever truly felt safe.

Eira shifted in the window seat beside him, letting out a soft, exhausted sigh as she rested her head against his shoulder.

"You're thinking incredibly loudly, Richard," Eira murmured, her voice thick with the creeping pull of sleep.

Richard smiled, wrapping his arm securely around her. "Just looking at the dark. I've never been over the Atlantic before."

"It looks exactly the same as the dark over Virginia," Eira whispered, her eyes already closed. "But tomorrow morning, the sun is going to come up over the Irish Sea. And I'm going to show you the mountains."

"I can't wait," Richard said quietly, kissing the top of her head. He looked over his shoulder. In Row 13, the formidable frontline shields had finally powered down. Gabriel was fast asleep, his head tilted back against the headrest, while Gwendolyn was curled up against his side, breathing in a slow, steady rhythm.

But in Row 12, the battle for rest was still being fought.

Julian was exhausted. The sheer sensory toll of the sweltering, chaotic transfer at the New York terminal was still echoing in his mind. Physically, he was safe; the preventative dose of muscle relaxants his pediatrician had prescribed for the flight was doing its job perfectly, keeping his legs from locking into the agonizing, rigid spasticity that usually accompanied cramped spaces.

But chemical relaxation doesn't instantly erase psychological adrenaline. His unmedicated mind was still racing, the phantom smells of diesel and the echoing shouts of the terminal playing on a loop beneath his heavy isolation headphones. His hands were clenched into tight fists on his lap, his breathing shallow and rapid.

He needed his anchors.

Maria and Clara didn't need to ask what was wrong. They could feel the vibrating tension radiating from his shoulders. The cramped economy seats made a proper, full-body hold difficult, but the identical twins had spent years adapting to Julian's exact needs.

Clara unbuckled her seatbelt just enough to shift her weight. She leaned over the fixed armrest, pressing her side entirely flush against Julian's left arm. She reached across his lap, taking his tightly clenched left fist and sandwiching it firmly between both of her hands, applying a steady, crushing pressure.

On his right, Maria mirrored the movement perfectly. She leaned her head heavily onto Julian's shoulder, wrapping her arm securely around his back to press against his spine. She took his right fist, interlocking her fingers with his and squeezing with a fierce, unyielding gravity.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Let yourself go, Julep,">Laisse-toi aller, Julep,</lang>" Maria whispered in Québécois French, her lips right near the edge of his foam earcup so he could just barely hear the soothing, rhythmic cadence of her voice. "<lang="fr-CA" trans="We are here. No one can reach you.">On est là. Personne ne peut t'atteindre.</lang>"

"<lang="cy" trans="Sleep peacefully,">Cysga yn dawel,</lang>" Clara added from his left, the ancient, flowing Welsh syllables wrapping around him like a second blanket.

Julian gasped, his chest heaving against the seatbelt as the symmetrical, deep-pressure cuddle finally short-circuited the residual panic loop. The physical sensation of his two best friends pressing against him from both sides replaced the chaotic memories of the airport.

He focused entirely on the heavy, synchronized pressure on his hands. Slowly, the remaining tension in his shoulders melted away. His fists unclenched entirely, his fingers relaxing into the twins' grips.

He let his head loll to the right, resting it heavily against Maria's hair.

The perimeter was impenetrable. The airplane was hurtling through the freezing upper atmosphere at six hundred miles an hour, but in Row 12, suspended somewhere between the trauma of the United States and the promise of the Menai Strait, Julian Rowlands finally, completely let go.

His breathing slowed, matching the deep, ambient rumble of the jet engines, and he fell asleep.
