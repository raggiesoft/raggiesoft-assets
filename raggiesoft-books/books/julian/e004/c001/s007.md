The interior of the wide-body jet underwent a slow, deliberate transformation as it reached cruising altitude high over the dark expanse of the Atlantic Ocean.

The flight attendants moved quietly down the aisles, collecting the remnants of the dinner service, before the harsh overhead cabin lights finally clicked off. They were replaced by the soft, dim glow of the floor runners and the scattered, pale beams of individual reading lights.

The chaotic, frantic energy of the departure had completely dissipated. The only sound left was the continuous, heavy, white-noise roar of the massive jet engines outside the fuselage.

Across the aisle from the teenagers, Richard Harrison rested his head against the high back of his seat. He had an open paperback novel resting on his lap, but he hadn't turned a page in over twenty minutes. He was staring out the small oval window into the pitch-black void of the night sky.

At forty-two years old, Richard had never left the United States. His entire life had been defined by the borders of Virginia—teaching music therapy in the local public schools, raising his twin daughters after his first wife passed, and eventually navigating the corporate broadcasting ladder.

But as he looked at the dark glass, the sheer magnitude of what they were doing settled heavily into his chest. He wasn't just taking a vacation. He was crossing an ocean to help his wife reclaim a homeland that had been stolen from her, and to bring a traumatized fifteen-year-old boy back to the only place he had ever truly felt safe.

Eira shifted in the window seat beside him, letting out a soft, exhausted sigh as she rested her head against his shoulder.

"You're thinking incredibly loudly, Richard," Eira murmured, her voice thick with the creeping pull of sleep.

Richard smiled, wrapping his arm securely around her. "Just looking at the dark. I've never been over the Atlantic before."

"It looks exactly the same as the dark over Virginia," Eira whispered, her eyes already closed. "But tomorrow morning, the sun is going to come up over the Irish Sea. And I'm going to show you the mountains."

"I can't wait," Richard said quietly, kissing the top of her head. He looked over his shoulder. In Row 13, the formidable frontline shields had finally powered down. Gabriel was fast asleep, his head tilted back against the headrest, while Gwendolyn was curled up against his side, breathing in a slow, steady rhythm.

But in Row 12, the battle for rest was still being fought.

Julian was exhausted. The sheer sensory toll of the sweltering, chaotic transfer at the New York terminal was still echoing in his mind. Physically, he was safe; the preventative dose of muscle relaxants his pediatrician had prescribed for the flight was doing its job perfectly, keeping his legs from locking into the agonizing, rigid spasticity that usually accompanied cramped spaces.

But chemical relaxation doesn't instantly erase psychological adrenaline. His unmedicated mind was still racing, the phantom smells of diesel and the echoing shouts of the terminal playing on a loop beneath his heavy isolation headphones. His hands were clenched into tight fists on his lap, his breathing shallow and rapid.

He needed his anchors.

Maria and Clara didn't need to ask what was wrong. They could feel the vibrating tension radiating from his shoulders. The cramped economy seats made a proper, full-body hold difficult, but the identical twins had spent years adapting to Julian's exact needs.

Clara unbuckled her seatbelt just enough to shift her weight. She leaned over the fixed armrest, pressing her side entirely flush against Julian's left arm. She reached across his lap, taking his tightly clenched left fist and sandwiching it firmly between both of her hands, applying a steady, crushing pressure.

On his right, Maria mirrored the movement perfectly. She leaned her head heavily onto Julian's shoulder, wrapping her arm securely around his back to press against his spine. She took his right fist, interlocking her fingers with his and squeezing with a fierce, unyielding gravity.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="Let yourself go, Julep,">Laisse-toi aller, Julep,</lang>" Maria whispered in Québécois French, her lips right near the edge of his foam earcup so he could just barely hear the soothing, rhythmic cadence of her voice. "<lang="fr-CA" trans="We are here. No one can reach you.">On est là. Personne ne peut t'atteindre.</lang>"

"<lang="cy" trans="Sleep peacefully,">Cysga yn dawel,</lang>" Clara added from his left, the ancient, flowing Welsh syllables wrapping around him like a second blanket.

Julian gasped, his chest heaving against the seatbelt as the symmetrical, deep-pressure cuddle finally short-circuited the residual panic loop. The physical sensation of his two best friends pressing against him from both sides replaced the chaotic memories of the airport.

He focused entirely on the heavy, synchronized pressure on his hands. Slowly, the remaining tension in his shoulders melted away. His fists unclenched entirely, his fingers relaxing into the twins' grips.

He let his head loll to the right, resting it heavily against Maria's hair.

The perimeter was impenetrable. The airplane was hurtling through the freezing upper atmosphere at six hundred miles an hour, but in Row 12, suspended somewhere between the trauma of the United States and the promise of the Menai Strait, Julian Rowlands finally, completely let go.

His breathing slowed, matching the deep, ambient rumble of the jet engines, and he fell asleep.
