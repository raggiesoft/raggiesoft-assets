## 7:00 AM • Julian Rowlands — The Terminator Line

Richard Harrison had not slept a single minute.

He sat perfectly still in the aisle seat of Row 12, his open paperback novel entirely forgotten on his lap. For the past four hours, he had kept his eyes locked on the small, oval window across the aisle.

When they had departed New York, the sky outside was a suffocating, pitch-black void. But because the heavy, wide-body jet was hurtling eastward across the Atlantic at six hundred miles per hour, they were physically chasing the morning.

The transition, when it finally began, did not happen slowly.

First, a razor-thin, vibrating line of bruised purple appeared on the edge of the curvature of the Earth. Within minutes, the purple ignited into a fiercely glowing, radioactive streak of atomic orange. The colors didn't lazily stretch across the horizon like they did over the flatlands of Virginia; they boiled and expanded with unnatural, accelerated speed, completely flooding the cabin with a sharp, unfiltered, brilliant light.

Eira stirred in the window seat beside him. She let out a soft groan, shielding her eyes with the back of her hand against the sudden, piercing glare of the high-altitude dawn.

"Good morning," Richard whispered gently, reaching over to lower the plastic window shade halfway to cut the glare.

Eira blinked, sitting up and looking out at the sprawling, endless expanse of white, cotton-like clouds stretching out below them, painted entirely in gold and amber.

"<lang="cy" trans="What is the time?">Sut mae'r amser?</lang>" she murmured, her voice thick with sleep as she rubbed her eyes.

"Just past seven in the morning, local time," Richard replied, a quiet sense of awe bleeding into his voice. "We're over the Irish Sea. The captain just announced the initial descent into Manchester."

Eira's posture instantly straightened. The exhaustion of the transatlantic crossing vanished from her face, replaced by a sharp, electric surge of adrenaline. She leaned all the way forward against her seatbelt, pressing her forehead almost flush against the thick acrylic glass of the window, peering down through the breaks in the cloud cover.

Across the aisle, the sudden influx of sunlight broke the perimeter's deep slumber.

Julian’s eyes fluttered open behind the thick rims of his glasses. For a terrifying, disorienting fraction of a second, the phantom memory of Ohio flickered in his mind. He remembered waking up in the freezing, rusted backseat of his father’s sedan, the pale, gray dawn illuminating the terrifying reality that he had been stolen.

But the panic couldn't take physical hold. The preventative muscle relaxants his pediatrician had prescribed for the flight were doing exactly what they were supposed to do, keeping his legs comfortably pliant and completely preventing the agonizing, rigid spasticity of the past.

And more importantly, his tactile anchors were right there to ground his racing mind.

His right hand was securely interwoven with Maria's. She was still fast asleep, her head resting heavily against his shoulder, her breathing slow and rhythmic. His left hand was entirely enveloped by Clara's warm, firm grip. She was already awake, her blue eyes watching him closely in the golden light of the cabin.

Clara smiled, giving his left hand a firm, deliberate squeeze. "<lang="cy" trans="Good morning, Julep,">Bore da, Julep,</lang>" she whispered in Welsh.

Julian let out a long, heavy exhale, sinking perfectly into the fabric of the airline seat. He wasn't in the car. He wasn't trapped. He squeezed her hand back, offering a soft, sleep-drunk smile.

He reached up with his free fingers and carefully pulled his heavy isolation headphones down around his neck. The ambient hum of the cabin was manageable, replaced by the quiet rustle of passengers beginning to wake up and stow their blankets.

"Julian," Eira called softly across the aisle.

Julian turned his head. His mother wasn't looking at him; her eyes were still fiercely locked onto the window. Her hands were trembling slightly where they rested against the plastic trim.

"Look," Eira whispered, her voice cracking with a profound, overwhelming emotion.

Julian leaned forward against his seatbelt, peering past his mother's shoulder and out the window.

The heavy, golden cloud cover was finally beginning to break apart as the jet descended toward the coastline. Below them, cutting through the swirling morning mist, the dark, jagged, ancient peaks of the Snowdonia mountain range pierced the sky. To the north, the deep, rushing, tidal waters of the Menai Strait glittered like shattered glass in the brilliant morning sun, separating the rugged Welsh mainland from the sprawling, green sanctuary of the Isle of Anglesey.

Julian stared at the coastline. He had not seen it since he was six years old.

He didn't reach for his AAC device. He didn't need to type the words. The sheer, overwhelming magnitude of the view hit him like a physical weight in his chest. A sudden, bright tear slipped out from behind his glasses, rolling silently down his cheek.

He was home.

In Row 13, Gabriel leaned forward, resting his heavy chin on the back of Julian's seat. He looked out the window at the rugged mountains, a massive, victorious grin breaking across his face.

"Alright, garrison," Gabriel rumbled, his deep baritone carrying the unyielding confidence of a general who had successfully delivered his troops across the ocean. "Time to pack up the perimeter. Next stop: the ground."

## 7:45 AM • Eira Rowlands — The Border

The transition off the aircraft at Manchester Airport was the smoothest maneuver of the entire journey.

Because they were seated near the front of the economy cabin in Row 12, the wait was minimal. Gwendolyn lifted Julian from his seat with practiced ease, carrying him out the heavy cabin door and onto the cool, carpeted incline of the jet bridge. Gabriel was right behind her, retrieving the customized manual wheelchair from the baggage handlers who had brought it up from the cargo hold exactly as promised.

The moment Julian was lowered back into his own chair, he felt a profound, atmospheric shift.

The air bleeding into the jet bridge from the terminal didn't smell like the suffocating, exhaust-choked humidity of New York or the heavy pine of Virginia. It was cooler, damper, and carried the distinct, metallic tang of the British morning.

"Perimeter," Gabriel ordered softly, taking the push-handles of the chair.

Maria and Clara instantly took their positions on the left and right wheels. Richard led the way, holding three small, rectangular white paper Landing Cards he had filled out on the plane, while Eira walked directly beside Julian, her leather crossbody bag unzipped and ready.

The walk to the UK Immigration Hall was a long, echoing maze of glass corridors. Julian kept his headphones around his neck. The ambient noise of the Manchester terminal was different than America—the overlapping voices were quieter, the accents rolling in familiar, comforting cadences that he had only heard inside the walls of the Ewell house.

When they reached the massive Immigration Hall, they were met with two distinct queues: one marked UK/EU Passports, and a significantly longer, slower serpentine line marked All Other Passports.

Typically, mixed-nationality families were forced to split up or endure the agonizing wait of the foreign queue. But Eira Rowlands did not surrender her perimeter.

She marched the entire garrison straight up to the front of the empty UK/EU queue, stepping up to the glass partition of the Border Control desk.

The UK Border Force officer, a stern-looking man in a crisp white shirt and dark epaulets, looked up from his monitor. He saw a family of seven, a heavy-duty wheelchair, and a mix of complex travel documents.

"Morning, Officer," Eira said, her British accent instantly cutting through any bureaucratic hesitation. She didn't hand over a massive, confusing stack of documents. She controlled the narrative perfectly.

She slid four pristine, burgundy European Union/United Kingdom passports under the glass partition.

"Four British nationals returning home," Eira stated, pointing to herself, Gabriel, Gwendolyn, and then resting her hand gently on Julian's shoulder.

The officer took the four burgundy books. He opened Eira's first, checking her photo against her face. He scanned it, stamped it, and set it aside. He did the same for Gwendolyn and Gabriel.

Then, he opened the fourth passport. He looked down at the photo of the fifteen-year-old boy, and then looked over the desk at Julian.

Julian sat perfectly still in his chair. His mind was crystal clear, the preventative medication keeping him physically relaxed while the twins' hands resting on his wheels kept him psychologically grounded. He met the officer's eyes through the thick rims of his glasses. He wasn't hiding. He wasn't Thomas Cross's stolen American son.

"Welcome back, Mr. Rowlands," the officer said with a polite nod, sliding the burgundy passport back under the glass.

Julian’s breath hitched in his chest. It was the first time in nine years a government official had addressed him by his true name, in his own country. He reached down to his AAC device, his fingers trembling just slightly, and typed a single word using the English synthesizer.

<aac>THANKS.</aac>

The officer offered a small smile, then looked back at Eira. "And the rest of your party, ma'am?"

Eira slid the three dark-blue United States passports under the glass, along with the three completed paper Landing Cards.

"My husband, Richard, and my two stepdaughters, Maria and Clara," Eira explained smoothly. "They are American citizens traveling with us on holiday."

The officer opened Richard's passport. "Purpose and duration of your stay, sir?"

"Family holiday," Richard answered, his calm, steady voice projecting absolute reliability. "My wife just purchased a cottage in Llanfairpwll on Anglesey. We are staying for the month to get the house settled, and we fly back to New York on the second of September, right before the girls start their school year."

The officer reviewed the Landing Cards, ensuring the return date matched the story. A British citizen bringing her American husband and dependents home for a summer holiday at a verifiable address was the easiest clearance of the day.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

The officer brought a heavy, black ink stamp down on a blank page in each of the three American passports, granting them standard six-month visitor entry. He slid the blue books back under the glass.

"Right then," the officer said, gathering the landing cards. "Enjoy the new cottage in Wales, folks. You're all cleared through."

"Thank you," Eira breathed, a massive, profound wave of relief finally washing over her face. She gathered the passports, carefully zipping them back into her leather bag.

Gabriel pushed Julian’s chair past the glass booth and out through the final set of automatic doors into the chaotic, echoing expanse of the international baggage claim.

They had crossed the final legal threshold. There were no more checkpoints, no more ticket agents, and no more metal detectors.

Eira turned around to face her family. She looked at Richard, then at the twins, then at her three Welsh children.

"<lang="cy" trans="Welcome home,">Croeso adref,</lang>" Eira whispered, her eyes shining with unshed tears.

Gabriel let out a massive, booming whoop that echoed over the baggage carousels, pulling Gwendolyn into a sudden, bone-crushing hug. Richard laughed, wrapping his arms around his wife.

In the center of the celebration, Julian looked down at his lap. He pulled his AAC device close, deleting the English text and switching the synthesizer back to its permanent, rightful setting.

<aac><lang="cy" trans="IT IS TIME FOR THE TRAIN.">MAE'N AMSER YR CAR TREN.</lang></aac>

## 8:15 AM • Julian Rowlands — The Commuter Hop

The international baggage claim at Manchester Airport was a cavernous, echoing hall of grinding conveyor belts and exhausted travelers, but the Rowlands-Harrison garrison moved through it with frictionless, military precision.

Gabriel and Richard stepped up to the edge of the metal carousel, claiming two heavy luggage trolleys. As the chute spat out their six massive, scuffed canvas suitcases, the two men hoisted them onto the trolleys with the effortless synchronization of a frontline quartermaster and a steadfast anchor.

A few feet away, safely out of the chaotic crush of the crowd, Julian sat in his customized manual wheelchair, perfectly flanked by Maria on his right and Clara on his left. The heavy isolation headphones still rested comfortably around his neck. The ambient noise of the British terminal was different than the suffocating roar of New York. The overlapping voices here carried the rolling, familiar cadences of his mother's side of the Atlantic, instantly lowering the defensive spikes in his unmedicated nervous system.

"Right then, garrison," Gabriel rumbled, his deep baritone carrying the crisp, rhythmic Welsh lilt of his heritage. He gripped the handle of the lead luggage trolley. "Bags are secured. Let's get to the platforms."

"The railway station is connected directly to the terminal via the Skylink walkway," Gwendolyn directed, her own British English clipping with tactical authority as she referenced the heavily tabbed itinerary in her binder. "We take the local commuter line straight into Manchester Piccadilly. We have a forty-five-minute layover there before we board the coastal line to Wales."

Eira nodded, her posture radiating a fierce, triumphant energy. "Lead the way, Gwen."

The transition from the international baggage claim to the railway station was the first time the garrison didn’t have to actively fight the environment for space.

Gabriel and Richard pushed the two towering luggage trolleys through the elevated, glass-walled Skylink walkway. As they left the heavily air-conditioned terminal behind, the climate shifted. The air filtering in from the sliding doors didn't carry the suffocating, exhaust-choked 90-degree heat of August in Virginia. It was crisp, damp, and carried the distinct, metallic chill of a northern English morning.

"Right, grab your coats if you need them," Gabriel directed, his deep baritone carrying the crisp, rhythmic Welsh lilt of his heritage. "It's a bit brisk on the platforms."

"Oh, I'll take this over the Richmond humidity any day of the week," Richard chuckled. His warm, Hampton Roads drawl provided a grounding, familiar contrast to the echoing British announcements overhead.

Gwendolyn walked point, her tactical binder tucked under her arm. "We take the local commuter line straight into Crewe. It's only a thirty-minute hop, but it gets us out of the airport hub. We have a forty-minute layover at Crewe to switch to the North Western Trains coastal service."

Julian sat securely in his customized manual wheelchair in the center of the formation, flanked flush by Maria and Clara. His heavy isolation headphones rested comfortably around his neck. The ambient noise here wasn't a threat; the overlapping voices echoing down the walkway carried the rolling, familiar cadences of his mother's side of the Atlantic, instantly lowering the defensive spikes in his unmedicated nervous system.

When they descended the lifts to the station platform, the logistics Eira had meticulously arranged weeks in advance instantly materialized. Because Julian's manual wheelchair required specialized boarding assistance, the Passenger Assist protocol was already active.

A uniformed conductor stood waiting by the open doors of the heavy, electric commuter train, a bright yellow folding ramp resting against the carriage threshold.

"Rowlands party?" the conductor asked, checking his clipboard.

"That's us. Spot on, thank you," Eira replied, her British accent instantly establishing a natural, frictionless rapport that had been entirely absent in the United States.

Gabriel pushed Julian’s chair smoothly up the incline and into the dedicated accessible bay. The environment inside the carriage was a profound, immediate upgrade from the cramped fuselage of the jetliner. There was breathing room.

Gabriel engaged the brakes on the wheelchair, ensuring it was securely locked into place against the bulkhead. Instead of being crammed side-by-side in narrow airline seats, Maria and Clara slid into the folding seats directly across from Julian. They didn't need to contort their shoulders to reach him; they simply reached across the small gap. Maria enveloped his right hand, and Clara anchored his left.

With a sharp blast of a whistle, the carriage doors hissed shut.

The electric train pulled away from the Manchester Airport station, accelerating smoothly out into the gray sprawl of the suburbs.

Julian let his head fall back against his headrest, a profound wave of relief washing over his face. The train did not swerve unpredictably like a stolen car on a terrifying Appalachian highway, and it didn't rattle violently like the airport shuttle bus at JFK. It was locked onto a fixed, undeniable path. The heavy, rhythmic clack-clack of the steel wheels vibrating through the floorboards acted as a massive, mechanical metronome, sending a deep-pressure vibration straight up through the frame of his chair and into his bones.

He hadn't been on a train since he was six years old, back before Tom Cross had uprooted them across the ocean. But as the carriage swayed gently with the curve of the tracks, a deep, buried somatic memory woke up inside him. The rails felt like childhood. They felt like the days before the screaming started.

"<lang="fr-CA" trans="It's perfect, right?">C'est parfait, non?</lang>" Maria smiled in Québécois French, offering a bright, exhausted smile as she felt the tension completely bleed out of his fingers. "<lang="fr-CA" trans="Much better than the plane.">Beaucoup mieux que l'avion.</lang>"

Julian pulled his AAC device squarely onto his lap. His hands were completely steady. He navigated past the Welsh and French dictionaries, deliberately selecting the British English synthesizer. He wanted to engage with the world outside the Ewell house on his own terms, using the vocabulary of his homeland.

<aac>BRILLIANT RIDE.</aac> The digitized voice spoke, the crisp, synthesized British accent perfectly matching the environment outside the window. <aac>JUST LIKE I REMEMBER. MUCH BETTER THAN THE LORRY AT THE AIRPORT.</aac>

Gabriel let out a booming laugh from the adjacent seat, recognizing Julian's playful jab at the rattling shuttle bus they had endured in New York.

"Just a quick warm-up ride to get your sea legs back, Julep," Gwendolyn said, offering a warm, relaxed smile.

For the Americans in the group, the linguistic dynamic had entirely flipped. Richard, Maria, and Clara's Hampton Roads accents suddenly marked them as the foreigners, while Julian, his mother, and his older siblings were flawlessly integrated into the background noise of the carriage. Julian let out a quiet, happy hum, his fingers tapping rhythmically against his armrest. His voice, his family, and his environment were finally completely synchronized.

"Crewe is a massive Victorian junction," Eira warned gently, leaning across the aisle. "It will be busy, Julian. But we're just moving from one platform to another to catch the coastal train."

Julian nodded. He didn't reach for his headphones. He just squeezed Maria and Clara's hands, watching the English countryside blur past the thick glass of the window. The perimeter was impenetrable, the phantom sways of the past were silenced by the rhythm of the rails, and the thirty-minute hop was exactly the grounding anchor he needed before facing the coast.
