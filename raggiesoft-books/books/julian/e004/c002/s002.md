The transition off the aircraft at Manchester Airport was the smoothest maneuver of the entire journey.

Because they were seated near the front of the economy cabin in Row 12, the wait was minimal. Gwendolyn lifted Julian from his seat with practiced ease, carrying him out the heavy cabin door and onto the cool, carpeted incline of the jet bridge. Gabriel was right behind her, retrieving the customized manual wheelchair from the baggage handlers who had brought it up from the cargo hold exactly as promised.

The moment Julian was lowered back into his own chair, he felt a profound, atmospheric shift.

The air bleeding into the jet bridge from the terminal didn't smell like the suffocating, exhaust-choked humidity of New York or the heavy pine of Virginia. It was cooler, damper, and carried the distinct, metallic tang of the British morning.

"Perimeter," Gabriel ordered softly, taking the push-handles of the chair.

Maria and Clara instantly took their positions on the left and right wheels. Richard led the way, holding three small, rectangular white paper Landing Cards he had filled out on the plane, while Eira walked directly beside Julian, her leather crossbody bag unzipped and ready.

The walk to the UK Immigration Hall was a long, echoing maze of glass corridors. Julian kept his headphones around his neck. The ambient noise of the Manchester terminal was different than America—the overlapping voices were quieter, the accents rolling in familiar, comforting cadences that he had only heard inside the walls of the Ewell house.

When they reached the massive Immigration Hall, they were met with two distinct queues: one marked UK/EU Passports, and a significantly longer, slower serpentine line marked All Other Passports.

Typically, mixed-nationality families were forced to split up or endure the agonizing wait of the foreign queue. But Eira Rowlands did not surrender her perimeter.

She marched the entire garrison straight up to the front of the empty UK/EU queue, stepping up to the glass partition of the Border Control desk.

The UK Border Force officer, a stern-looking man in a crisp white shirt and dark epaulets, looked up from his monitor. He saw a family of seven, a heavy-duty wheelchair, and a mix of complex travel documents.

"Morning, Officer," Eira said, her British accent instantly cutting through any bureaucratic hesitation. She didn't hand over a massive, confusing stack of documents. She controlled the narrative perfectly.

She slid four pristine, burgundy European Union/United Kingdom passports under the glass partition.

"Four British nationals returning home," Eira stated, pointing to herself, Gabriel, Gwendolyn, and then resting her hand gently on Julian's shoulder.

The officer took the four burgundy books. He opened Eira's first, checking her photo against her face. He scanned it, stamped it, and set it aside. He did the same for Gwendolyn and Gabriel.

Then, he opened the fourth passport. He looked down at the photo of the fifteen-year-old boy, and then looked over the desk at Julian.

Julian sat perfectly still in his chair. His mind was crystal clear, the preventative medication keeping him physically relaxed while the twins' hands resting on his wheels kept him psychologically grounded. He met the officer's eyes through the thick rims of his glasses. He wasn't hiding. He wasn't Thomas Cross's stolen American son.

"Welcome back, Mr. Rowlands," the officer said with a polite nod, sliding the burgundy passport back under the glass.

Julian’s breath hitched in his chest. It was the first time in nine years a government official had addressed him by his true name, in his own country. He reached down to his AAC device, his fingers trembling just slightly, and typed a single word using the English synthesizer.

<aac>THANKS.</aac>

The officer offered a small smile, then looked back at Eira. "And the rest of your party, ma'am?"

Eira slid the three dark-blue United States passports under the glass, along with the three completed paper Landing Cards.

"My husband, Richard, and my two stepdaughters, Maria and Clara," Eira explained smoothly. "They are American citizens traveling with us on holiday."

The officer opened Richard's passport. "Purpose and duration of your stay, sir?"

"Family holiday," Richard answered, his calm, steady voice projecting absolute reliability. "My wife just purchased a cottage in Llanfairpwll on Anglesey. We are staying for the month to get the house settled, and we fly back to New York on the second of September, right before the girls start their school year."

The officer reviewed the Landing Cards, ensuring the return date matched the story. A British citizen bringing her American husband and dependents home for a summer holiday at a verifiable address was the easiest clearance of the day.

Thump. Thump. Thump.

The officer brought a heavy, black ink stamp down on a blank page in each of the three American passports, granting them standard six-month visitor entry. He slid the blue books back under the glass.

"Right then," the officer said, gathering the landing cards. "Enjoy the new cottage in Wales, folks. You're all cleared through."

"Thank you," Eira breathed, a massive, profound wave of relief finally washing over her face. She gathered the passports, carefully zipping them back into her leather bag.

Gabriel pushed Julian’s chair past the glass booth and out through the final set of automatic doors into the chaotic, echoing expanse of the international baggage claim.

They had crossed the final legal threshold. There were no more checkpoints, no more ticket agents, and no more metal detectors.

Eira turned around to face her family. She looked at Richard, then at the twins, then at her three Welsh children.

"<lang="cy" trans="Welcome home,">Croeso adref,</lang>" Eira whispered, her eyes shining with unshed tears.

Gabriel let out a massive, booming whoop that echoed over the baggage carousels, pulling Gwendolyn into a sudden, bone-crushing hug. Richard laughed, wrapping his arms around his wife.

In the center of the celebration, Julian looked down at his lap. He pulled his AAC device close, deleting the English text and switching the synthesizer back to its permanent, rightful setting.
