## 11:30 AM — Waiting for the First Flakes

"You think we're going to have snow?" Katie asked, her breath fogging a small circle on the cold glass pane as the two of us stood shoulder to shoulder at my upstairs bedroom window.

"Hope so. SNOW DAY!" I said, practically vibrating with excitement as I pressed my palms against the wooden sill and looked out at our residential street in Virginia Beach.

Outside on the first Saturday of January 1996, it was bitterly cold, and heavy, slate-gray clouds were rolling in off the Atlantic until the sky looked like brushed steel. A step to Katie's right, our ten-year-old maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher pushed her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses up the bridge of her nose, brushed her long medium-blonde hair back over the shoulder of her plum sweatshirt, and leaned toward the glass beside us. Because Brittany's parents—Mom's sister, Aunt Karen Fletcher, and Uncle Steve Fletcher—worked odd shift hours up in town while trying to keep up with an aging family farm way down in the rural southern end of Virginia Beach on Blackwater Road, Brittany had practically grown up at our house in Kempsville being watched after school by Dad or by our retired paternal grandmother, Grandma. Grandma was still stuck up in Newport, Rhode Island this week visiting Aunt Liz and Chelsea after New Year's, and Aunt Karen had dropped Brittany off with Dad at ten-thirty that morning so she could squeeze in a quick grocery run in Kempsville before heading back down toward Blackwater ahead of the storm.

"My mom said the bread aisle was already wiped out completely clean," Brittany said, squinting out past the eaves at the empty asphalt street. "Do you guys really think we'll get enough to cancel school?"

Behind us on my bedroom stand, the local Norfolk GBS news station had cut into regular Saturday morning programming with wall-to-wall coverage beneath a bold winter graphic calling the approaching nor'easter the Blizzard of '96. Standing in front of a swirling radar map that stretched from the Gulf Coast all the way past the Virginia Capes, the chief meteorologist pointed to bands of deep blue marching across Virginia—projecting two to three feet of powdery snow out in the Shenandoah Valley and Blue Ridge Mountains while warning all of Tidewater and Virginia Beach to brace for heavy afternoon snow shifting tonight to driving sleet, freezing rain, thirty-five-mile-per-hour northeast gales off the ocean, and major coastal and wind-tide flooding from the oceanfront boardwalk down to the low-lying back roads around Back Bay and Pungo Ferry Road. Even starting on a Saturday, a real coastal blizzard meant Monday and Tuesday would be wiped clean off the Virginia Beach school calendar.

"Snow," Katie replied hopefully, leaning her shoulder against mine as the news anchor read off rising tide tables for Lynnhaven Inlet and Sewells Point. All three of us were thrilled at the thought of the whole city shutting down.

At twelve-thirty, while Katie, Brittany, and I were sitting around the kitchen table eating our lunch sandwiches, the first white flakes began to drift past the dining room window. At first it wasn't much—just fine, powdery crystals swirling across the dry grass—but within twenty minutes the northeast wind picked up off the coast and the flakes grew thick and steady, driving sideways across Kempsville until the wooden fence rails, the neighborhood cul-de-sacs, and the roof of Dad's Holt sedan vanished under a deepening blanket of white just as Aunt Karen hurried straight down south to beat the rising water on the rural roads toward Blackwater, promising Dad on the porch that she or Uncle Steve would come back up for Brittany as soon as the squall eased.

"Wonder if it will stick," Katie giggled, pointing her finger toward the patio as the wind sculpted crisp white drifts against the back steps.

By late afternoon, several inches of wind-packed snow had buried the entire backyard under a clean, silent blanket of white.

Inside the house, however, the quiet didn't last. Around five o'clock, the familiar, sharp sound of Mom's and Dad's voices started rising from the upstairs hallway—first a slammed cabinet door, then the bitter, overlapping shouting match that always followed whenever Dad found another empty bottle hidden behind the pantry cans.

## 5:45 PM — The Backyard Bear Hug

Down in the living room, Brittany froze on the tan couch with a coloring book in her lap, her eyes wide behind her wire-rimmed glasses at the sudden angry shouting overhead. Making sure Brittany was tucked safely away from the bottom of the stairs, Katie and I didn't wait for the yelling to reach the landing—we pulled on our heavy winter jackets and boots and slipped out the back door into the twilight snow so I could catch my breath.

Out in the backyard, inside the perimeter of our white wooden picket fence, the falling snow acted like a giant acoustic blanket over the neighborhood. The streetlamps glowed pale orange through the swirling flakes, and every footstep made a soft, muted crunch under our shoes.

Standing near the center of the yard with the snow settling onto my short blonde hair, the cold wind hit my face, and all at once the weight of everything we had been carrying over the past year caught up with me. My chest tightened, and before I could stop myself, I started sniffling in the cold air.

Katie turned around immediately. She didn't hesitate for a second—she stepped straight through the snow and pulled me into a tight, fierce bear hug, wrapping both of her blue-jacketed arms around my shoulders.

![Katie Prairie in her blue winter jacket and Nathaniel Prairie in his black winter jacket holding each other in a tight bear hug in their snow-covered Virginia Beach backyard in front of the white picket fence during the Blizzard of '96]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b002/jan_1996_blizzard_backyard_snow_hug_katie_nathaniel.jpg)

"What's wrong?" Katie asked softly against my ear, holding onto me with all her strength.

"I'm just so happy you're willing to do all of this for me," I whispered into her shoulder, my voice shaking slightly as I held onto the back of her winter coat, unsure of how to put the rest of the knot in my chest into words.

Katie didn't need me to explain it. Because she was my twin, she already knew the exact two things crushing my ribs.

"You're still upset with last year, and that girl everyone thought you had a crush on?" Katie asked gently.

"Yes, I am," I whimpered, burying my face against her collar, overwhelmingly grateful that my twin sister was hugging me instead of telling me to toughen up. All through the previous spring at school, a pack of kids in our grade had latched onto a rumor that I had a crush on a girl in our homeroom, turning every hallway change and cafeteria period into a relentless, mocking gauntlet that wouldn't stop no matter what I said. "And it does not help that Mom and Dad are arguing with each other again. I just want to enjoy the snow without the screaming."

"I'm right here, and I will never leave you," Katie replied fiercely, pulling me into an even tighter bear hug as the heavy January snow came down all around us in the twilight. "I'm your sister, and I would just never, ever leave you."

She held onto me for a long moment in the quiet yard, letting the steady pressure of her arms slow my breathing down, before she spoke again.

"Your classmates are mean," Katie continued, her voice firm against the cold wind. "Especially when they refuse to back down. When we go back to school, I will gladly play my part of pretend girlfriend so they leave you alone."

"Thank you, but I think everyone knows that you're my sister," I whispered, managing a small, shaky laugh even as my teeth started chattering from the twenty-degree air.

"Come on, let's head back inside before we freeze," Katie said, taking my hand and leading me back across the snow-covered grass toward the back door.

Inside the warm kitchen, the shouting upstairs had finally stopped—Mom had gone to bed for the night behind her closed door, and Dad was standing by the wall phone speaking quietly into the receiver while snow piled against the dining room glass. Standing beside the dark green laminate counter while we stomped the powder off our boots, Katie wrapped her arms around my neck for one more long, warming hug until the chill left my fingers—right as the soft whisper of falling snow outside abruptly turned into the sharp, staccato rattle of sleet pelting the windowpanes off the Atlantic.
