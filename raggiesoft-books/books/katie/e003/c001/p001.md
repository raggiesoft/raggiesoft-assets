## Thursday, May 24, 2001, 2:00 PM — Officer Whitehead's Line & Holding Our Breath Through Smoker's Corner

"Deep breath on three before we step through the fence," our sixteen-year-old maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher warned, pushing her thin silver wire-rimmed oval glasses up the bridge of her nose with one finger as the heavy steel side doors of Northwood High School swung shut behind our backpacks in the warm May sunshine.

"Already holding mine," Katie laughed, tightening her left hand around my right hand as the two o'clock dismissal bell faded across the red-brick courtyard and forty diesel school buses roared to life in the afternoon loop behind us.

"Wait until we actually cross the grass to Lions Way, or you're going to pass out in the middle of the crosswalk," I told my twin sister as Brittany reached over to take my left hand in hers—putting me in my favorite spot right in the middle so I could hold both of their hands as we walked, just like sitting between them at lunch or on the couch let me hug both of my two favorite women at once—while we followed the sidewalk past the handicap spaces at the edge of the student parking lot and stepped straight out across the wide green grass lawn toward the perimeter road.

In Virginia Beach City Public Schools, high-schoolers couldn't just scribble a note and hop onto somebody else's yellow bus—every route out of Kempsville was packed three to a vinyl bench, and drivers didn't take afternoon bus passes. What regular afternoon buses also didn't take, however, was a roll-call clipboard. As long as your parents filed a standing written alternate-dismissal walker authorization with the attendance secretary in the Main Office—and listed your aunt right below your dad on the emergency contact card—you could walk right off campus at two o'clock whenever you didn't want to ride your regular bus home.

Ever since the summer before ninth grade, when Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve Fletcher had finally sold their exhausting old family farm way down in the rural southern end of Virginia Beach on Blackwater Road and moved into a neat brick ranch on Earlton Court, that five-minute walk off the edge of campus had been our favorite escape hatch of the week. Back when we were little kids, Brittany had practically lived at our house in Kempsville on weekday afternoons—watched by Dad or by our retired paternal grandmother, Grandma Ruth, who lived off her pension and Social Security in our fourth upstairs bedroom until Grandma Ruth moved back home to Newport, Rhode Island in 1998—whenever Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve worked odd shift hours up in town. Yet once school started each September, rural attendance lines had always pulled us apart, sending Katie and me five minutes down the road to Northwood Elementary and Morrison Middle while poor Brittany endured flooded wind tides across Pungo Ferry Road on her bus to Blackwater Elementary and hour-long rides to Pungo Ridge Middle. Moving to Earlton Court ahead of freshman year had put all three of us under the blue gabled roofs of Northwood High together every single day—and put Brittany's front porch less than a quarter mile from the campus fence.

There was only one catch standing between Lions Way and Earlton Court, and all three of us could smell it before we even finished cutting across the school lawn.

Whoever had laid out the back edge of Northwood High's grounds had painted a crisp white ladder-style crosswalk across two-lane Lions Way without bothering to pour a campus sidewalk to meet it—or a single curb cut on either side of the street. On our side of Lions Way, the wide green grass simply ended at an unbroken standard concrete curb; on the far side, right in front of a tall gray cylindrical concrete light pole and two red-and-white striped road-end barricades, the crosswalk hit a second unbroken standard curb before a short, narrow strip of concrete ran straight through a gap in the galvanized chain-link fence and met the asphalt dead end of Macgregory Street completely level.

My left ankle twinged with the dull end-of-day stiffness that always crept into Katie's calves and mine by fourth block, so with Katie holding my right hand and Brittany holding my left hand, the three of us steadied each other as we stepped down off the grass curb onto the white crosswalk stripes, hurried across two-lane Lions Way, and stepped up over the opposite uncut curb onto the narrow concrete strip leading through the fence.

Behind us on the green grass of the school side of Lions Way stood School Resource Officer Whitehead with his arms folded across his dark navy uniform shirt. Fifteen feet ahead of us to the right, lounging where the flush concrete strip met the public asphalt dead end of Macgregory Street, stood a cluster of eighteen-year-old seniors everyone at Northwood called the Smoker's Corner crowd.

Every freshman learned the legal geometry of that concrete pole during the first week of September. Under Virginia law, eighteen-year-olds could legally possess tobacco once both shoes cleared school property, and the asphalt turnaround of Macgregory Street belonged to the City of Virginia Beach rather than the school board. As long as nobody under eighteen took a cigarette, nobody flicked anything across the property line, and nobody stepped back inside the chain-link opening during school hours, there was nothing Officer Whitehead could legally do except stand just inside the Lions Way fence like a hawk waiting for someone to slip up.

"Afternoon, Officer Whitehead," I murmured politely before sealing my lips shut.

Officer Whitehead gave the three of us a brief, approving nod—he knew Katie, Brittany, and me on sight as three quiet sophomores who never lingered on the asphalt for half a second—while Katie and Brittany both squeezed my hands tighter on either side and all three of us marched briskly through the narrow opening in the fence.

Even out in the open May breeze, a stale drift of burnt paper and ash hung near the edge of the turnaround from earlier in the afternoon. All three of us hated cigarette smoke with every fiber of our bodies; Katie and I had never forgotten watching our Aunt Liz light up outside the Newport News train station while our older cousin Chelsea coughed into her mittens, and nobody in the Fletcher house had ever touched tobacco either. Keeping our chins tucked and our lungs sealed tight, we cleared the Macgregory Street turnaround in twelve quick strides, followed the quiet residential sidewalk down to the corner, and turned right onto tree-shaded Earlton Court.

All three of us let our breath out in one loud, simultaneous rush and burst out laughing under the pin oaks.

"Ugh, I swear that corner smells like an old bowling alley carpet," Brittany groaned, fanning the collar of her plum-rose top with one hand as her caramel-blonde hair swung over her backpack straps. "If the city ever put a real gate lock on Macgregory Street, half the senior class wouldn't know where to stand at two-oh-five."

"At least your street is around the bend where the breeze stays clean," Katie grinned, rolling her shoulders happily now that the noise of two thousand teenagers and forty school buses was behind us. "Is Aunt Karen working until four-thirty today?"

"Four-thirty, and Dad's on the three-to-eleven maintenance shift, so we get the whole living room to ourselves for two hours," Brittany smiled, pulling her brass house key on its beaded keychain out of her jeans pocket as we walked up the flat concrete driveway of her parents' single-story brick ranch. "And Mom put a fresh twelve-pack of ginger ale on the bottom fridge shelf last night specifically because she figured you two would walk over with me today."

## Thursday, May 24, 2001, 2:20 PM — Cold Ginger Ale on Earlton Court & Our Floor Plan for Three

Inside the Fletchers' house, the central air conditioning hummed quietly against the eighty-degree Tidewater afternoon, and the kitchen smelled faintly of lemon dish soap and the pot roast Aunt Karen had left simmering on low in the ceramic slow cooker on the counter. Right beside the fruit bowl sat a folded yellow sticky note in Aunt Karen's neat cursive handwriting telling Brittany to make sure Katie and Nate stayed for supper if Uncle David wanted an extra hour to study for his community-college computer exam after work.

"See? My mom likes you two way better than she likes half our own relatives," Brittany teased warmly as we kicked off our sneakers neatly on the hallway mat, set our heavy sophomore backpacks beside the coffee table, and carried three ice-cold green cans of ginger ale plus an open family-size box of frosted snack cakes and ridged potato chips into the sunlit living room. Years of lonely afternoons down on the Blackwater farmstead and shallow remarks from mean girls in the Northwood locker bays had left Brittany self-medicating stress with comfort food whenever the day wore her down, though around Katie and me nobody ever judged what was on a plate or how a shirt fit—the living room was simply safe ground.

Without anybody needing to call dibs on seats, the three of us settled onto Aunt Karen's wide plaid sofa in the exact formation we had perfected over five winters and two years of high school. Katie curled up on my left side with her legs tucked under the soft cotton throw blanket and her head resting against my left shoulder, while Brittany set her cold soda can on a coaster, slipped off her wire-rimmed glasses for a minute to rub the bridge of her nose, and tucked herself snugly against my right side—wrapping her left arm warmly around my shoulders while reaching across my chest to tap Katie's knuckles.

For a long, peaceful minute, nobody turned on the television. Just having four quiet walls, no raised voices upstairs, and the steady, familiar warmth of leaning against each other let every knot from four ninety-minute blocks of sophomore geometry, chemistry, and hallway crowds melt right out of my spine.

"Do you guys realize we only have three weeks left until sophomore year is completely done?" Brittany murmured against my right shoulder before sliding her glasses back on and picking up her ginger ale. "We're sixteen now. Two more years at Northwood, and we actually graduate."

"Don't remind Mom," I said dryly, taking a sip from my can while my left hand stayed laced comfortably with Katie's on top of the throw blanket. "At our house, anytime anybody mentions the future, it turns into an argument before dinner's even on the table. Half the reason Katie and I signed up to knock out an academic credit in summer school this July is just so we get Third-Block lunch and library study hall locked together next fall."

Brittany shifted slightly on the cushion so she could look straight at both of us, her expression turning earnest behind her silver frames. Reaching down beside the armrest where her canvas backpack leaned against the carpet, she unzipped the front pouch and pulled out a glossy, magazine-sized booklet folded open to a page circled twice in purple gel pen.

"Look what I grabbed out of the free rack by the automatic doors at Market Square when I went grocery shopping with Mom on Tuesday," Brittany said, spreading the *Hampton Roads Apartment & Townhome Guide* open across my lap so Katie and I could both see the black-and-white floor-plan diagrams.

Katie lifted her head off my shoulder, her blue eyes lighting up immediately as she traced the little architectural drawing with her fingertip. "Wait—is that a three-bedroom ground-floor flat?"

"Two bedrooms and a den, or a full three-bedroom right off Kempsville or over near Virginia Dominion University," Brittany nodded eagerly, pointing to the monthly rent printed below the square-footage box. "I did the math during fourth-block study period. Once the three of us graduate in June of 2003 and turn eighteen, you two are going to be writing software and taking computer classes at CCHR just like Uncle David, and I'm going to get a full-time office or bookkeeping job while I take night classes. If the three of us split the rent and electric bill three ways, it costs less per person than a dorm room."

I stared down at the little ink drawing of the living room—a wide rectangular space with a window overlooking a courtyard and zero stairs at the front entrance—and felt a genuine grin spread across my face.

"Look at the living-room wall right there," I pointed out, tapping the center of the floor plan between Katie's hand and Brittany's sleeve. "That wall is fourteen feet wide. We could put a massive sectional couch right there, set up two computer desks along the opposite wall so Katie and I can code side by side without cords tripping anybody, and keep the fridge stocked with nothing but ginger ale, fruit punch, and Hearthstone leftovers."

"Zero screaming upstairs," Katie added softly, looking across my chest at Brittany with a bright, unguarded smile. "Zero cigarette smoke within a hundred yards of the windows. And nobody telling the three of us we have to split up or act like strangers."

"Exactly," Brittany said firmly, closing the apartment guide carefully so the dog-eared page stayed marked and resting her head right back down against my shoulder while Katie nestled back into my other side. "Back when we were stuck down on Blackwater Road and Grandma Ruth used to watch the three of us on your living-room rug while the tide came over Pungo Ferry Road—and that Blizzard of '96 weekend when you guys let me climb under your blanket on the couch—your house with Uncle David and Grandma Ruth was the only place I never felt left out. When we grow up, we're getting our own place and living together as three. Deal?"

"Deal," Katie promised without a second's hesitation, reaching across my lap to squeeze Brittany's hand.

"Count on it," I agreed, resting my chin lightly over the top of Katie's platinum-blonde hair while keeping my right arm warm and steady around Brittany's shoulders—the three of us tucked safely together on the quiet Earlton Court couch with the whole summer stretching ahead of us.
