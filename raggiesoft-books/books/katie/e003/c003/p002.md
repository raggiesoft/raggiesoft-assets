## Friday, September 14, 2001, 1:15 PM — When Our Legs Finally Gave Out at School

"Just forty-five more minutes until the two o'clock dismissal bell, Katie, and Dad'll be waiting for us right out front in the pickup loop," I whispered at a quarter past one on Friday afternoon as the two of us walked slowly down the quiet first-floor B-Wing hallway of **Northwood High School** on our way back from the guidance office.

All week long, all I knew was that Friday afternoon at three o'clock was our next scheduled checkup with **Dr. Evelyn Chen**. Dad had arranged his Friday schedule at the **Community College of Hampton Roads** so he could pick Katie and me up right when school let out at two o'clock and drive us straight over to Dr. Chen's office for our three o'clock appointment to see how our arms and legs were doing and go over whatever test results had come back from the lab.

All we had to do was make it thirty more feet down the waxed linoleum corridor to our sixth-block classroom door.

We were holding hands between us for balance, walking close to the lockers so we could brush our free fingertips against the metal doors if our ankles wobbled, when the sensation below my knees suddenly vanished.

It wasn't a slow cramp like on the stairwell the week before, and it wasn't just our calves getting tired. Halfway past Room 112, it felt as though someone had reached inside our spines and pulled the electrical plug on both of our lower bodies at the exact same second.

My right knee buckled inward without an ounce of resistance. Beside me, Katie let out a sharp gasp as both of her ankles folded underneath her at the exact same instant, and before either one of us could catch the locker handles, the two of us collapsed straight down onto the hard linoleum floor.

"Nathaniel! Katie!" **Mr. Henderson**, our eleventh-grade U.S. History teacher, called out in alarm from his open classroom doorway ten feet ahead of us, dropping his dry-erase marker onto his podium and hurrying out into the hallway. "Are you two all right?!"

My heart was hammering against my ribs from the shock of going down so fast, and my instinct—the stubborn sixteen-year-old instinct that had kept both of us forcing our legs down hallways all week—was to get right back up before the rest of the class saw us on the floor.

"We're okay, Mr. Henderson—just let us stand back up," I gasped, planting my left palm flat on the cold floor while Katie braced her hands against the tiles beside me.

Straining with every muscle left in our shoulders, wrists, and thighs, Katie and I tried to push ourselves back up onto our feet.

We didn't even make it halfway to our knees.

The moment we put weight on our legs and trembling forearms, both of our legs and arms crumpled completely out from under us a second time—sending both of us pitching forward and sideways with nothing left to break our fall.

*Crack. Thud.*

The right side of my forehead slammed hard against the unforgiving linoleum-over-concrete floor, sending a bright, stinging flash across my vision, and six inches away I heard the sickening thud of the left side of Katie's head striking the tiles right beside the locker base.

Before the ringing in my ears even settled, my very first instinct—and Katie's—was the exact same one we had had since the day we were born. Lying flat on the cold linoleum floor with a hot, throbbing knot rising on my forehead, I reached my right hand blindly across the six inches of tile between us, and Katie's left hand met mine in midair, our fingers locking together in a vise grip so neither of us was alone on that floor.

"Stay down! Do not try to get up again!" Mr. Henderson shouted, his voice dropping an octave in urgent alarm as he saw both of our heads strike the floor and our hands clasp together between us.

He sprinted three steps to the classroom wall intercom just inside his doorway and pressed the red emergency call button. "Main Office, get Nurse Callahan to the first-floor B-Wing hallway outside Room 112 immediately! I need a **Clinic Emergency** escalation right now—Nathaniel and Katie Prairie both collapsed in the hallway, their legs gave out a second time when they tried to get up, and **both of them hit their heads on the floor**!"

## Friday, September 14, 2001, 1:18 PM — Manual C-Spine Stabilization & The Emergency Room Escalation

Within forty-five seconds, **Nurse Callahan**, the school's registered nurse, arrived at a dead run with her orange trauma bag, followed closely by **Assistant Principal Brooks** and the clinic aide.

One look at the two of us lying flat on the hard tile floor holding hands with red, swelling contusions rising on our foreheads—and Mr. Henderson's report that our arms had given out on the second collapse so nothing had cushioned our heads—was all Nurse Callahan needed to escalate the protocol straight to the Emergency Room.

"Nobody lift them and nobody let them sit up!" Nurse Callahan ordered immediately, dropping to her knees behind my head while her trained clinic aide knelt behind Katie's head. Gently but firmly, Nurse Callahan placed both of her palms along the sides of my head to hold my neck in manual inline cervical stabilization, while her aide stabilized Katie's head the exact same way so neither of us could turn or nod our necks.

"Mr. Brooks," Nurse Callahan commanded, "we have an unbroken dual head strike against concrete-backed tile plus acute bilateral lower-extremity paralysis. Under school division head-and-spine protocol, they cannot be lifted into clinic wheelchairs and they cannot go home in a passenger car until an Emergency Room CT scan clears their heads and cervical spines. Call **911** right now for Virginia Beach EMS transport to **Bayside General Hospital Emergency Room**, put B-Wing on a temporary **Hold in Place** so the hallway stays clear when the bell rings, and call their father to meet the ambulance at the ER!"

"Calling 911 and Mr. Prairie right now," Assistant Principal Brooks said, unclipping his radio and telephone handset as Mr. Henderson closed the classroom doors along the corridor.

Down on the floor, even with Nurse Callahan's hands cradling the sides of my head so I couldn't turn my neck, I kept my right hand laced tightly through Katie's left hand on the tiles between us.

"Nathaniel, Katie—keep looking straight up at the ceiling tiles so your necks stay completely still, and keep holding each other's hands," Nurse Callahan said calmly as she checked my pupils and then Katie's with her penlight. "Can both of you hear me and tell me your full names?"

"Nathaniel Prairie," I answered clearly, squeezing Katie's fingers twice so she knew I was okay.

"Katie Prairie," Katie answered right after me, squeezing my hand back just as tightly.

"Did either of you black out when your heads hit the floor? Do you know where you are, what day it is, and what happened right before you fell?" Nurse Callahan asked.

"No, ma'am, neither of us blacked out," I said, my voice steady despite the throbbing ache over my right eyebrow. "It's Friday, September fourteenth, and we're in the B-Wing hallway at Northwood High School. Our dad was supposed to pick us up at the two o'clock dismissal bell to take us to our three o'clock appointment with Dr. Evelyn Chen to see how our arms and legs were doing—and while we were walking down the hall, our legs just gave out all at once."

"And when we tried to push ourselves back up," Katie added clearly from beside me, "our legs and arms crumpled again and we hit our heads."

"Can either of you wiggle your toes or push your feet forward against Mr. Henderson's palms?" Nurse Callahan asked.

I concentrated with everything I had, ordering my ankles and calves to move. Nothing happened. Neither of my feet moved a fraction of an inch, and neither did Katie's.

"All right, don't strain," Nurse Callahan told us gently as the distant wail of sirens approached down the boulevard outside. "Your dad is already on his way to the Bayside General Emergency Room to meet you, and I had the front office call **Dr. Evelyn Chen's** clinic too—she has hospital privileges at Bayside General and she's bringing your test results straight over to the Emergency Room to meet your dad there."

Four minutes later, four **Virginia Beach EMS** paramedics wheeled two ambulance stretchers through the B-Wing double doors. Working with practiced precision so our spines never bent or twisted, the paramedics fitted soft foam cervical collars around our necks, unclipped two-piece aluminum **scoop stretchers** that slid underneath our backs and hips from both sides on the floor, and lifted Katie and me smoothly onto the two padded gurneys.

"Please don't separate us," Katie pleaded from inside her foam collar as the paramedics clicked the stretcher straps across our chests.

"We wouldn't dream of it, kiddo—both stretchers are riding right next to each other in Squad 14," the lead paramedic promised warmly, rolling our two gurneys side by side down the clear B-Wing hallway so Katie and I could reach across the gap between the side rails and hold hands all the way into the back of the ambulance.

***

## Friday, September 14, 2001, 3:10 PM — Cleared in the ER, Borrowed Time Is Up, & Time for Powerchairs

By three-ten that afternoon—after Dad had met the ambulance doors at the **Bayside General Hospital Emergency Room**, and after the ER trauma team had wheeled both of us through the CT scanner—the hospital staff pulled back the privacy curtain between Trauma Bays 3 and 4 and pushed our two hospital gurneys right up beside each other so our foam collars could come off and our hands could lock back together.

![Inside Dr. Evelyn Chen's consultation office in Virginia Beach in September 2001, sixteen-year-old fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in wheelchairs holding hands beside their father David Prairie while Dr. Chen reviews their neuromuscular test results and prescribes their power wheelchairs]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b003/sep_2001_dr_chen_prognosis_arms_legs_consultation.jpg)

Walking into the combined ER bay alongside the emergency physician was **Dr. Evelyn Chen**, wearing her white coat and carrying the thick manila lab folder from her clinic across the medical plaza.

"First things first—the head and cervical spine CT scans for both Nathaniel and Katie are completely clean," Dr. Chen announced with a deep breath of relief, resting a warm hand on Dad's arm before checking the ice packs pressed against the matching goose eggs on my right forehead and Katie's left temple. "Nurse Callahan followed the exact right protocol calling EMS after a dual head strike on a concrete school floor, and thank goodness neither of you suffered a skull fracture, concussion, or intracranial bleed. You are both Glasgow Coma Scale fifteen, sharp, and intact above the neck."

Then Dr. Chen stepped to the foot of our two ER beds and tested the reflexes and motor response in our knees, calves, and ankles.

Just like on the hallway floor outside Room 112 at Northwood High School, when she tapped our patellar tendons and asked us to lift our shins against her fingers, our lower legs didn't budge.

Dr. Chen pulled a rolling stool up beside our two beds, opened the thick manila lab folder containing the genetic sequencing and nerve-conduction studies that had arrived from Norfolk that morning, and looked at Dad, Katie, and me with deep, solemn compassion.

"David, Nathaniel, Katie," Dr. Chen said quietly, "two and a half weeks ago, when you came to see me the Monday morning after your cruise, I told you that Nathaniel and Katie's legs were walking on borrowed time—and that they would eventually need power wheelchairs. We had scheduled your three o'clock checkup today to review these test results and talk about transitioning to powerchairs."

She looked straight into my eyes, and then into Katie's.

"What happened in the B-Wing hallway at Northwood High School at one-fifteen this afternoon wasn't a temporary spell, and it wasn't just end-of-the-week fatigue," Dr. Chen said gently. "Your borrowed time is up. Neither of your legs can support your body weight anymore, and from this afternoon forward, neither of you can ever try to stand or walk again—because trying to push yourselves up will only cause another collapse like the one that sent you to the Emergency Room today. It is officially time for powerchairs."

Even though Katie and I had known in our hearts from the second we reached for each other's hands on that linoleum floor that our walking days were over, hearing Dr. Chen say the words out loud made everything real. Beside me, Katie's fingers tightened around mine between our two hospital beds, and I squeezed her hand right back with everything I had.

"Tell us what the test results showed, Evelyn," Dad said steadily, standing right between our beds with one hand resting on my shoulder and the other on Katie's. "Why did their legs give out completely today, and what does it mean for their arms?"

"When you two were toddlers," Dr. Chen explained clearly, "your uneven balance was diagnosed as mild spastic diplegic cerebral palsy. Because mild CP is static and non-progressive—and because your mother always brushed off any new symptoms—nobody realized that your early balance issues were masking a second, genetic condition that both of you inherited as fraternal twins: a juvenile-onset progressive axonal motor neuropathy."

Dr. Chen pointed to the nerve-conduction graphs inside the folder.

"In this condition, the longest motor nerves in the body—the ones running from the lower spine down to the feet, ankles, calves, and quadriceps—lose their electrical signal amplitude first, and the sixteen-year-old adolescent growth spurt pushes those nerves past their threshold," Dr. Chen said. "Today at school, those lower-extremity motor nerves finally dropped below the threshold required to keep your knees locked upright. And as you both felt when you tried to push yourselves up off the hallway floor and crumpled the second time, the neuropathy is also causing progressive weakness in your shoulders, biceps, and forearms—which is why standard manual wheelchairs that you have to push with your hands are out of the question."

"What about our fingers, Dr. Chen?" Katie asked, her voice small but brave as she held onto my hand. "Are we still going to be able to type on our computers and write *Quantum Basic* code?"

Dr. Chen's face warmed with an immediate, reassuring smile.

"Yes, Katie—and that is the best news in your entire lab packet," Dr. Chen answered. "As long as we stop you from straining your shoulders and wrists trying to lift your body weight or push manual wheelchair rims, your fine finger dexterity at a keyboard will remain intact for years to come. You will both be able to type, write computer code, use a mouse, and hold hands—and with a fingertip joystick on your power wheelchair armrests, you'll be able to glide through Northwood High School all day without tiring your arms at all."

Dr. Chen pulled three prescription and accommodation forms from her folder and signed them right on the bedside tray table.

"First," Dr. Chen said, "I have signed an urgent hospital prescription for two **SwiftChair** compact, portable take-apart power wheelchairs—small-footprint motorized chairs with ten-inch drive wheels, right-hand armrest joysticks, and removable battery boxes and seats that you can break down to fit inside the trunk of your sedan, David, along with a smooth composite transfer sliding board so Nathaniel and Katie can slide horizontally from their wheelchair seats onto the back seat of the car or onto their beds without ever putting weight on their legs. Second, Hospital Physical Therapy is bringing up two loaner wheelchairs and a transfer board right now so Dad can take you home safely tonight until the custom powerchairs are delivered in two weeks. And third, here is your updated **Section 504 Plan** for Northwood High School: elevator keys, duplicate classroom textbooks so you never lift a heavy bag, and full-time wheelchair access starting Monday morning."

Dad picked up the signed forms, then leaned down between our two hospital beds and wrapped one arm around Katie and one arm around me, kissing the top of Katie's head and then mine just above our bruised foreheads.

"I was never so scared in my life as when Mr. Brooks called me and said you two were headed to the Emergency Room," Dad whispered, his voice thick with emotion as he held us both close. "Wait until you see how fast I learn to break those two powerchairs down for the trunk of the car. Your legs may have run out of borrowed time today, but as long as the three of us have each other, nothing is going to stop you."
