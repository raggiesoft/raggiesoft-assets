## Saturday, October 6, 2001, 1:10 PM — "Then We'll Change the Truck" & Our New House off Military Highway

"Watch how smoothly the boom clears the tailgate sill without touching the bumper," Dad said with a proud grin on the first Saturday of October, pressing the corded pendant switch in his hand as the newly installed twelve-volt powered bed hoist inside the back of our 1991 Crestline Garrison 350 four-door Crew Cab 4x4 lifted my unoccupied compact SwiftChair off the concrete driveway, swung it cleanly inside the weather-sealed fiberglass camper shell capping the eight-foot cargo bed, and set its wheels straight down onto the reinforced steel four-point securement tracks bolted through the bed floor.

Sitting side by side on the wide cloth-and-vinyl rear passenger bench seat with the full-size rear Crew Cab doors swung open to the October breeze and our inner hands clasped tightly together, sixteen-year-old Katie and I watched Dad lock all four cargo restraints onto my empty wheelchair frame right beside Katie's already-secured chair—turning the capped pickup bed into a bone-dry rolling wheelchair garage where rain, road salt, and winter slush could never touch our motors, twenty-four-volt battery boxes, upholstery, or armrest joystick electronics.

To Katie and me, this two-tone navy-and-silver heavy-duty one-ton pickup wasn't just Dad's truck; it was part of our family. Dad had bought that Garrison 350 Highland Crew Cab brand-new in 1991 when Katie and I were six years old—the final year Crestline built the classic square-shouldered heavy-duty four-door body before rounding the fenders off, promoted on every Sunday afternoon broadcast with gravelly Roy Slater singing Crestline's *Steady as Stone* anthem. Every childhood memory we loved lived inside that cab: the solid rotary hand-crank window handles on all four doors, the manual chrome plunger lock pins on the sills, the old-fashioned chrome-latched triangular front vent windows that pivoted open to scoop cool evening air across our faces, the horizontal metal HVAC sliders that clicked into factory air conditioning in July, the twin black-knobbed floor shifters for the cast-iron four-speed manual gearbox—with its ultra-low creeper first gear—and manual four-wheel-drive transfer case, and the two matching pull-out stereo carrier caddies Dad had wired up from Circuit Barn so he could slide his entire full-bodied AM/FM stereo cassette unit out of the permanent dashboard dock by its metal bail handle and click his separately wired AM/FM stereo CD carrier unit into the exact same fourteen-pin rear dock block without ever throwing away decades of cherished cassette tapes.

Dad had already qualified in his own right for permanent Virginia disabled license plates on the truck back when he bought it—requiring zero adapted driving controls to work the clutch and four-speed stick—while keeping his own personal removable blue disability placard in his wallet for traveling in other vehicles, right alongside the two separate individual permanent disability placards Dr. Carter had certified independently for Katie and for me so each of the three of us held our own personal credential and never needed to hang a redundant tag from the rearview mirror when the truck's plates were on the bumper.

Four days earlier, when the seating clinic delivered our two compact SwiftChair power wheelchairs after our legs gave out for good in the B-Wing hallway at Northwood High—and with Brenda having taken the Holt sedan in the divorce—Katie and I had looked out the window at the tall cab sill of our beloved one-ton 4x4 and quietly braced ourselves to hear that we couldn't ride in Dad's pickup anymore. A lowered-floor wheelchair conversion van cost upwards of thirty-five thousand dollars that we didn't have, and neither Katie nor I wanted to trade sitting hip-to-hip together on our childhood rear bench seat for being strapped apart as rolling cargo.

Dad had taken one look at our faces, rested a steady hand on each of our shoulders, and said five words neither of us would ever forget:

*"Then we'll change the truck."*

Working with a local mobility outfitter in Norfolk, Dad kept the rule crystal clear: people rode together in the cab, and unoccupied power chairs rode secured as cargo under the weatherproof camper shell in the bed. At the rear passenger entry of the four-door Crew Cab, the shop installed an articulating powered person-transfer lift assist alongside heavy-duty sill grab bars that raised Katie and me smoothly from wheelchair height right onto the wide rear bench seat so we could buckle in side by side holding hands—regulating each other's sensory nerves the exact way we had since we were six—while the powered bed hoist and engineered frame-bolted bed restraints handled both heavy powerchairs behind us without Dad ever straining his back over the tall bed rails.

Even with our beloved pickup adapted around us, Dad had been completely honest with Katie and me around the kitchen table two weeks earlier when he opened his budget ledger.

Without a second income—and with the cruise credit card bill and legal fees hanging over our heads—we couldn't afford the mortgage on our old two-story house anymore. More importantly, even if we could have paid for it, our old house had a cramped downstairs stall bathroom too narrow for a wheelchair or a transfer bench and fourteen steep wooden stairs leading up to all four bedrooms and the main hall bath—stairs that neither Katie nor I could ever climb again now that the borrowed time in our legs had run out.

So Dad had put the two-story house up for sale—using the modest equity to wipe out Brenda's cruise debt completely, fund the small walk-away settlement check that Mr. Vance was conditioning on Brenda signing the Code of Virginia Section 20-121.4 divorce stipulation reverting to her maiden name, Walsh, so that just as Katie and I had asked, she could never show up at a medical office or school flashing the Prairie name while still making sure she wouldn't end up on the street, and finance the mobility hoist, rear-cab transfer lift, and fiberglass camper shell on our paid-off 1991 pickup—and spent every evening for two weeks poring over rental listings, Virginia Beach school-zone boundary maps, and printed bus schedules from the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority. Even Brenda's own younger sister, our Aunt Karen Fletcher, along with Uncle Steve, had spent six long years since that summer of 1995 trying to get Brenda to face her drinking and had been so appalled by what she did to us on the cruise ship that they stood squarely in Dad's corner—with Uncle Steve hauling half our furniture in his farm truck alongside Dad's Crew Cab and Aunt Karen quietly pressing an envelope into Dad's hand to help cover our rental security deposit and utility hookups whenever Dad's student budget got stretched thin. Out of quiet decency toward Aunt Karen, Dad kept one unspoken rule under our roof from day one: he never gloated about the divorce papers and never badmouthed Brenda in front of Karen, remembering that Brenda was still Karen's older sister and that Karen was grieving who her sister used to be.

What Dad found was a stroke of pure logistical genius: a small, single-story brick ranch house for rent on Arvin Road at the corner of Indian River Road just off Military Highway, right where Virginia Beach met Norfolk and Chesapeake.

First, the rent was nearly four hundred dollars a month less than our old mortgage, bringing our monthly bills comfortably inside Dad's budget while he finished school at CCHR. Second, every single room in the little house—the living room, the eat-in kitchen, the hallway bathroom with its roll-up sink and shower transfer bench, and our bedrooms—sat on one flat ground floor, with a gentle aluminum ramp Dad had bolted over the two brick front porch steps. Third, because the house sat just inside the western edge of Northwood High School's zoned attendance boundary, Katie and I didn't have to change high schools in the middle of our Junior year, and a lift-equipped Virginia Beach school bus could pick us up right at the end of our driveway every weekday morning.

And fourth—the detail Dad was proudest of after he unlatched the four-point bed restraints inside our camper-shell wheelchair garage, swung both SwiftChairs smoothly down onto the concrete driveway with the powered hoist, and guided Katie and me down on the rear-door transfer lift into our waiting seats—was what ran right along Indian River Road and the massive eight-lane corridor of US-13 (Military Highway) at the end of our block.

"Latch the camper shell and let's roll out to the corner on Military Highway before the one-twenty-five bus gets here," Dad smiled, clicking the fiberglass rear hatch shut above our pickup tailgate and holding up a yellow-highlighted paper pamphlet folded in his hand. "Whenever we aren't taking the truck together, Route 15 out on Military Highway gives the two of you independent wheels across the entire city."

Clicking our lap belts shut and flipping our armrests back down, Katie and I pressed the green power buttons on our armrest joysticks and hummed side by side down Arvin Road to Indian River Road behind Dad. In less than three minutes, we reached the corner of Military Highway and Indian River Road, where four lanes of northbound traffic and four lanes of southbound traffic roared past a raised center median.

## Saturday, October 6, 2001, 1:25 PM — Catching CVTA Route 15 on Eight-Lane Military Highway

"Look at the timetable right here under Military Hwy. & Indian River Rd.," Dad said over the steady hum of eight-lane highway traffic as the three of us stopped beside the metal bus stop post in the crisp October sunshine, unfolding the printed CVTA Route 15 — *Naval Base Norfolk / Robert Hall Blvd.* schedule so both Katie and I could read it.

Holding Katie's left hand with my right hand between our two compact power wheelchairs, I leaned in with my sister to follow the yellow highlighter line Dad had drawn across the grid of numbers.

"Wait a second," Katie said, her eyes widening as she traced the columns from left to right. "It runs every thirty minutes all day Monday through Saturday?"

"Like clockwork," Dad smiled, tapping each column with his index finger. "Going northbound up Military Highway, Route 15 stops right across the highway at Military Highway and Indian River Road at :01 and :31 past every hour from six-thirty-one in the morning until six-thirty-one at night, and then once an hour until ten-thirty-one. In exactly fourteen minutes—at :15 and :45 past the hour—it drops you right at the doors of Glenrock Mall, where you can transfer to Routes 20, 23, 25, or 27, or stay right on Route 15 past Glenrock Shopping Center, Little Creek Road, and Hampton Boulevard all the way to Naval Base Norfolk."

"And what about going southbound on our side of the road?" I asked, looking at the lower table where Dad had highlighted two alternating rows in yellow and green.

"That's the best part," Dad said, pointing to the bold route note printed across the center of the timetable: *On arrival at Old Emerald Creek Rd. and Military Hwy., every other trip will alternate between Robert Hall and Emerald Creek Mall Monday through Saturday.* "Southbound buses leave Glenrock Mall at :15 and :45 past the hour and get right here to Military Highway and Indian River Road ten minutes later, at :25 and :55 past every hour. Every bus that stops here at :25 past the hour—like the 1:25 PM bus coming down the right-hand lane right now—turns onto the Emerald Creek Mall branch and puts us at the mall entrance twenty minutes later at 1:45 PM. And every bus that stops here at :55 past the hour goes straight down to Robert Hall Boulevard and Tidewater Square by :15 past the next hour."

Right on cue, signaling across the four southbound lanes of US-13 (Military Highway) toward our curb was one of the classic forty-foot high-floor workhorses of the fleet: a 900-series Beacon V transit bus (Fleet #914) that operated alongside the agency's high-floor MetroCoach buses. Because Tidewater Regional Transit (TRT) on the Southside and Peninsula Transit (PenTran) across the harbor had only merged two years earlier in October 1999 to form the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority (CVTA), Bus #914 was still wearing its original 1990s TRT paint scheme—a crisp white body banded with horizontal navy-blue and green stripes down its flanks—with a rectangular CVTA logo vinyl decal sticker applied right next to the front boarding doors below the *15 EMERALD CREEK MALL* destination sign.

As Bus #914 hissed to a stop parallel to the concrete curb, the bi-fold front doors swung open. Because the 900-series Beacon V was a true high-floor bus whose main passenger deck sat three full feet above the street at the top of a three-step stairwell, there was no sloped ramp. Instead, standing high up on the elevated driver's platform, the uniformed operator pulled the parking brake knob, flipped up the two forward priority benches behind the driver's area, and switched on the Elevate-U electro-hydraulic stairwell wheelchair lift.

"Afternoon, folks! Welcome aboard The 15," the operator called down warmly from the elevated doorway as the three deep front steps flattened into a level horizontal steel elevator platform, telescoped outward on twin vertical hydraulic lift arms with yellow-and-black tubular side handrails, and lowered straight down to rest flat on the concrete sidewalk before dropping the yellow outer safety barrier flap flush against the curb. "Come on onto the elevator platform one at a time—I've got both wheelchair bays folded up and ready for you!"

Katie and I glanced at each other with identical grins. Even when Dad was at work or in class with the pickup truck, nobody had to wait at home. Giving Katie's hand a quick squeeze before letting go for thirty seconds, I nudged my right-hand joystick forward and rolled my compact power chair onto the flat horizontal steel platform between the two side handrails first; the outer yellow safety barrier flipped up behind my rear wheels to lock me in, the twin vertical hydraulic arms raised my chair straight up three feet in the air like an elevator to the high interior floor of Bus #914, and I backed neatly into the right-hand securement bay. Seconds later, the elevator platform lowered straight back down to the sidewalk for Katie and lifted her power chair right up to the high deck across the aisle from me before folding seamlessly back into three staircase steps so Dad could climb up into the bus and drop our fares into the farebox.

Once the operator had clicked the four-point floor straps onto both of our power-chair frames, the front doors hissed shut and Bus #914 merged back into the four southbound lanes of Military Highway.

Across the center aisle of the high-floor bus, Katie reached her left hand out over her armrest, and I reached my right hand out to meet hers, our fingers lacing together in the warm afternoon sunlight streaming through the tall transit windows.

"We don't need a thirty-five-thousand-dollar conversion van to go wherever we want, Nate," Katie said softly over the deep diesel rumble of the Beacon V engine as we cruised south toward Emerald Creek Mall. "Between the school bus on weekdays, riding together in the back of our modified pickup with Dad, and Route 15 out on Military Highway at the end of our block, we've got the whole city back."

"And a house with zero stairs," I agreed, squeezing my sister's hand as Dad smiled at the two of us from the seat beside her. "We're going to be just fine."
