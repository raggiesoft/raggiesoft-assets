## Thursday, November 8, 2001, 2:00 PM — Eight Spare Minutes Before Bus 417 & Where the Sidewalk Runs Out

"Nate, Katie—Bus 417 is stuck third in line behind two slow tie-downs, so I've got at least eight minutes before I can lower our lift!" Miss Gloria, the cheerful bus aide on our afternoon Special Needs school bus, called over the rumble of idling diesel engines outside Stairway A as she tapped her aluminum clipboard against her mittened palm in the crisp November air. "Don't roll further than the edge of the handicap lot!"

"Just rolling down the sidewalk to the corner of the lot for some air, Miss Gloria—we'll be right back before our bus pulls up!" Katie called back warmly, nudging her right-hand joystick forward as I matched her speed inch for inch beside her.

Five months had changed every single rule of two o'clock dismissal at Northwood High School.

Back in May at the end of tenth grade, walking off campus with our maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher had required nothing more than Dad's standing walker note on file in the Main Office and cutting across the lawn to the fence. Once our legs had given out for good in the B-Wing hallway in September and Dr. Carter had prescribed our compact black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs, however, we no longer rode a regular fifty-four-passenger yellow bus—and we couldn't simply roll off school grounds unannounced. Under our Section 504 transportation plan, Katie and I were assigned to Bus 417, a short yellow lift-equipped bus that loaded right outside Stairway A. Every weekday afternoon at two o'clock, the driver and Miss Gloria checked each wheelchair student onto the hydraulic platform by name on a printed manifest before dropping Katie and me off at the aluminum front-porch ramp of our single-story brick ranch on Arvin Road under the unattended home self-release waiver Dad had signed. Leaving campus any other way would have required an adult signing us off the manifest in the Main Office before fourth block ended so Special Needs Dispatch wouldn't radio a missing-student alert across Kempsville.

Even if we had lived in some alternate universe where Dad signed ten walker notes and Brittany hadn't started pulling away from us at school, though, one glance ahead showed why our SwiftChairs could never make that five-minute trip between Northwood High and Earlton Court in either direction.

Following the closest concrete sidewalk leading away from the school entrance, Katie and I glided down the curb ramp into the blue-striped handicap parking section at the outer corner of the student parking lot—and eased our right index fingers back to neutral right where the asphalt stopped.

Directly in front of our five-inch front caster wheels stretched a wide, bumpy expanse of unpaved green grass with zero sidewalk leading out to two-lane Lions Way. Out on Lions Way itself, a set of white ladder-style crosswalk stripes ran across the asphalt—yet neither end of that painted crosswalk had a curb cut at all. Leaving Northwood High, a wheelchair couldn't cross the soft turf, couldn't drop off the unbroken standard concrete curb onto Lions Way, and couldn't climb the matching unbroken standard curb on the far side by the gray concrete light pole. Even coming from Brittany's house on the other side of the galvanized chain-link fence—where the short concrete strip actually met the asphalt dead end of Macgregory Street completely level so you could roll straight through the narrow fence gap—your front casters would dead-end three feet later at the uncut Lions Way curb with zero ramp on either side and nothing but grass across the road.

For an ambulatory teenager in sneakers, stepping off grass down a normal street curb and stepping back up the other side was so effortless you didn't even notice your knees bending. For two fifty-pound motorized power wheelchairs, this blue-striped corner of the student handicap parking lot was as close to Smoker's Corner as Katie and I could physically get.

Katie reached her left hand across the narrow gap between our armrests and laced her fingers tightly through my right hand, both of us looking across the stretch of green grass and two-lane Lions Way toward the narrow opening in the chain-link fence.

"There she goes through the gate now," Katie said quietly beside me.

All autumn long, watching Brittany drift away from our corner cafeteria table and Mrs. Periwinkle's library toward Tyler's loud senior crowd in the Commons had left an ache in both of our chests that neither of us liked to talk about out loud. Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve hadn't drifted an inch—three weeks earlier, right after Brenda signed the divorce stipulation restoring her maiden name, Brenda had driven straight to Earlton Court demanding that her sister cut Dad off, only to scream that Karen was a traitor and storm off the Fletchers' porch for good when Aunt Karen refused to take her side, reminded her that Dad had still paid off her cruise debt so she had a roof over her head, and offered to drive her to an Alcoholics Anonymous meeting instead. Two Sundays ago, when Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve came over to Arvin Road with homemade lasagna to admire how smoothly our SwiftChairs turned in the hallway, Dad hadn't gloated once about the maiden-name order or said a single ugly word about Karen's sister at the table, and Aunt Karen still called Dad twice a week to check on us—yet at school, sixteen-year-old Brittany acted as if stopping at our lunch table for more than five seconds might get her kicked out of her new circle.

"Look who's waiting on the Macgregory side of the fence," I murmured, my jaw tightening slightly. "Because we aren't walking across the grass and out that gate beside her at two o'clock anymore, she's walking straight into them all by herself every afternoon."

## Thursday, November 8, 2001, 2:06 PM — An Open Palm on Macgregory Street & The Cousin We Still Knew

Across Lions Way on the public asphalt dead end of Macgregory Street—where the narrow concrete strip met the neighborhood pavement flush in front of brick ranch houses framed by golden-orange November trees, while Officer Whitehead was still forty yards up the bus loop near the main driveway—Brittany had stopped at Smoker's Corner instead of walking straight down toward Earlton Court.

Adjusting the strap of her backpack over her plum ribbed sweater, Brittany stood listening with an anxious, eager-to-please half-smile behind her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as two older seniors—a tall eighteen-year-old boy in a dark unbranded jacket who hung around Tyler's locker every morning and a senior girl—laughed near the chain-link fence.

Beside me at the edge of the handicap lot, Katie's fingers suddenly went rigid around my hand.

"Nate, look," Katie breathed.

Across Lions Way beyond the fence opening, the tall eighteen-year-old boy glanced casually down the road to make sure Officer Whitehead wasn't watching, fished a small flip-top cardboard cigarette pack out of his jacket pocket, and extended the closed box straight toward sixteen-year-old Brittany.

Even across the quiet lawn and two lanes of pavement, we could see his coaxing grin as he waggled the box toward her hand, gesturing for her to take one for the walk home.

My stomach knotted hard. Remembering our Aunt Liz's hacking cough up in Newport, remembering how fiercely all three of us used to hold our breath just to hurry past that exact patch of Macgregory Street asphalt back in May, and knowing that our own daily neuropathy prescription carried a lethal warning label against tobacco and alcohol alike, I braced myself for the worst—convinced that in her desperation to belong to Tyler's crowd, Brittany was about to reach out and take it.

She didn't.

The eager-to-please half-smile vanished from Brittany's face in an instant. Straightening her spine in her plum sweater, Brittany lifted her right hand squarely in front of her chest with her open palm facing the box like a stop sign and gave a firm, unmistakable shake of her head, stepping half a pace back along the flush concrete strip.

The eighteen-year-old boy shrugged, slipped the closed box back into his jacket pocket, and turned back toward the senior girl as Brittany pivoted away to adjust her backpack strap.

As she turned, Brittany's eyes swept across the chain-link gate and the white stripes of Lions Way—and locked straight onto Katie and me sitting side by side in our black SwiftChairs at the paved edge of the student handicap parking lot across the grass.

For three quiet seconds, nobody moved. Behind her silver wire-rimmed lenses, Brittany's cheeks flushed from across the road—half embarrassed to have been caught lingering at Smoker's Corner at all, and half softened by a sudden flash of the girl who had spread an apartment guide across our laps on Earlton Court back in May. Lifting her hand a few inches from her hip, Brittany gave Katie and me a small, shy wave before walking away down Macgregory Street toward Earlton Court.

Back behind us by Stairway A, two sharp, friendly toots of a bus horn echoed down the sidewalk as Bus 417 finally pulled forward to the hydraulic loading zone and Miss Gloria waved her clipboard in the air for us.

"Ready to roll back to the lift?" I asked softly, giving Katie's left hand a squeeze before easing my right thumb onto my joystick to turn smoothly on the handicap-lot pavement back toward the sidewalk.

"Yeah," Katie said—and as we hummed side by side up the walkway toward Stairway A in the slanting gold November sunlight, the heavy sadness that had weighed on her voice five minutes earlier had lifted into quiet, stubborn hope.

Once we paused in line behind the lowered steel elevator platform of Bus 417 while the driver unclipped the safety chain, Katie looked across at me with earnest certainty in her blue eyes.

"Did you see her face when she put her hand up just now, Nate?" Katie whispered beneath the hydraulic hum of the lift. "Even standing right in the middle of Tyler's friends with nobody else around to back her up, she wouldn't touch that pack. She hasn't turned into them—she's just lonely walking out that gate by herself now that our chairs can't cross that grass and those curbs. The sweet cousin we grew up with is still right inside her, and we aren't giving up on her."

"Not a chance," I agreed quietly, squeezing my twin sister's hand one more time before releasing her fingers for thirty seconds to guide my SwiftChair onto the waiting yellow bus lift toward home.
