## Thursday, November 8, 2001, 2:00 PM — Eight Spare Minutes Before the Stairway A Lift & Six Inches of Yellow Concrete

"Nate, Katie—Bus 417 is stuck third in line behind two slow tie-downs, so I've got at least eight minutes before I can lower our lift!" Miss Gloria, the cheerful bus aide on our afternoon Special Needs school bus, called over the rumble of idling diesel engines outside Stairway A as she tapped her aluminum clipboard against her mittened palm in the crisp November air. "Don't wander out of sight of the lane!"

"Just rolling down the Lions Way stripe to stretch our wheels, Miss Gloria—we'll be right back before our bus pulls up!" Katie called back warmly, nudging her right-hand joystick forward as I matched her speed inch for inch beside her.

Five months had changed every single rule of two o'clock dismissal at Northwood High School.

Back in May at the end of tenth grade, walking off campus with our maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher had required nothing more than Dad's standing walker note on file in the Main Office and lifting our sneakers over a curb. Once our legs had given out for good in the B-Wing hallway in September and Dr. Carter had prescribed our compact black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs, however, we no longer rode a regular fifty-four-passenger yellow bus—and we couldn't simply roll off school grounds unannounced. Under our Section 504 transportation plan, Katie and I were assigned to Bus 417, a short yellow lift-equipped bus that loaded right outside Stairway A. Every weekday afternoon at two o'clock, the driver and Miss Gloria checked each wheelchair student onto the hydraulic platform by name on a printed manifest before dropping Katie and me off at the aluminum front-porch ramp of our single-story brick ranch on Arvin Road under the unattended home self-release waiver Dad had signed. Leaving campus any other way would have required an adult signing us off the manifest in the Main Office before fourth block ended so Special Needs Dispatch wouldn't radio a missing-student alert across Kempsville.

Even if Dad had signed ten Main Office walker notes, though, Katie and I already knew the real reason we hadn't seen the inside of Brittany's living room on Earlton Court once since September.

"There she goes toward the fence," Katie said quietly beside me, nodding past the row of parked faculty sedans as our ten-inch solid drive wheels hummed smoothly down the paved edge of Lions Way toward the residential perimeter fence. "Maybe if we catch her before she crosses Macgregory Street, we can ask if she wants Aunt Karen to drop her over at Arvin Road on Saturday after you and Heather get back from the mall."

All autumn long, watching Brittany drift away from our corner cafeteria table and Mrs. Periwinkle's library toward Tyler's loud senior crowd in the Commons had left an ache in both of our chests that neither of us liked to talk about out loud. Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve hadn't drifted an inch—Aunt Karen still called Dad twice a week to check on us, and she and Uncle Steve had come over to Arvin Road two Sundays ago with homemade lasagna to admire how smoothly our SwiftChairs turned in the hallway—yet at school, sixteen-year-old Brittany acted as if stopping at our lunch table for more than five seconds might get her kicked out of her new circle.

Steering side by side across the white painted stripes of the Lions Way crosswalk, Katie and I eased our right index fingers back to neutral, bringing our small front caster wheels to a dead stop half an inch from the edge of the pavement.

Right in front of our footrests rose a sheer, unbroken six-inch vertical wall of yellow-painted concrete.

There was no sloped ADA curb cut at the crosswalk, no flared concrete apron, and no way around the galvanized chain-link fence stretching across the grass on both sides of the narrow pedestrian opening beside the gray concrete utility pole. For an ambulatory teenager in sneakers, six inches of vertical concrete was an unconscious half-step on the way home. For two fifty-pound motorized power wheelchairs with five-inch front casters, that uncut yellow curb might as well have been a locked steel vault door.

Katie reached her left hand across the narrow gap between our armrests and laced her fingers tightly through my right hand, both of us staring across that six-inch concrete drop onto the flush asphalt turnaround of Macgregory Street fifteen feet away.

"Six inches of concrete," I murmured under my breath, shaking my head at the sheer stupidity of whoever had painted white crosswalk stripes straight into an uncut vertical curb. "All those afternoons we walked through this exact gate to Earlton Court, I never once stopped to think that a wheelchair couldn't get through here at all."

"And because we aren't walking out that gate beside her at two o'clock anymore," Katie whispered, her blue eyes tracking across the chain-link mesh toward the asphalt turnaround, "she's walking straight into them all by herself every single afternoon."

## Thursday, November 8, 2001, 2:06 PM — An Open Palm on Macgregory Street & The Cousin We Still Knew

Out on the public asphalt dead-end turnaround of Macgregory Street—with golden-brown and crimson November oak leaves lining the lawns of the brick ranch houses behind them, and Officer Whitehead still walking down the far curve of Lions Way forty yards back—Brittany had stopped near the edge of Smoker's Corner instead of walking straight down toward Earlton Court.

Adjusting the strap of her backpack over her plum ribbed sweater, Brittany stood listening with an anxious, eager-to-please half-smile behind her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as two older seniors—a tall eighteen-year-old boy in a dark unbranded jacket who hung around Tyler's locker every morning and a senior girl with dark hair—laughed over something from fourth block.

Beside me on Lions Way, Katie's fingers suddenly went rigid around my hand.

"Nate, look," Katie breathed.

Out on the Macgregory Street turnaround, the tall eighteen-year-old boy glanced casually down the fence line to make sure Officer Whitehead was still forty yards up the bus loop, fished a small flip-top cardboard cigarette pack out of his jacket pocket, and extended the closed box straight toward sixteen-year-old Brittany.

Even across fifteen feet of autumn air, his coaxing voice carried cleanly through the chain-link gate.

"Come on, Fletcher, you hang out on the corner with us every afternoon anyway," the senior boy drawled with an easy grin, waggling the pack toward her hand. "Take one for the walk home—nobody's looking."

My stomach knotted hard. Remembering our Aunt Liz's hacking cough up in Newport, remembering how fiercely all three of us used to hold our breath just to hurry past this exact patch of asphalt back in May, and knowing that our own daily neuropathy prescription carried a lethal warning label against tobacco and alcohol alike, I braced myself for the worst—convinced that in her desperation to belong to Tyler's crowd, Brittany was about to reach out and take it.

She didn't.

The eager-to-please half-smile vanished from Brittany's face in an instant. Straightening her spine in her plum sweater, Brittany lifted her right hand squarely in front of her chest with her open palm facing the box like a stop sign and gave a firm, unmistakable shake of her head.

"No thanks—keep that away from me," Brittany said clearly, taking half a step backward toward the center of Macgregory Street. "I told you guys, I don't smoke. I hate cigarette smoke."

The eighteen-year-old boy shrugged, muttered something dismissive under his breath as he slipped the closed box back into his jacket pocket, and turned back toward the senior girl.

As Brittany turned away from them to adjust her backpack strap, her eyes flicked toward the narrow opening in the chain-link fence—and locked straight onto Katie and me sitting side by side in our black SwiftChairs behind the uncut yellow curb on Lions Way.

For three quiet seconds, nobody moved. Behind her silver wire-rimmed lenses, Brittany's cheeks flushed pink—half embarrassed to have been caught lingering with the Smoker's Corner crowd at all, and half softened by a sudden flash of the ten-year-old girl who had climbed under our living-room blanket during the Blizzard of '96. Lifting her hand a few inches from her hip, Brittany gave Katie and me a small, shy wave before turning down the residential stretch of Macgregory Street toward Earlton Court.

Back up by Stairway A, two sharp, friendly toots of a bus horn echoed down the walkway as Bus 417 finally pulled forward to the hydraulic loading zone and Miss Gloria waved her clipboard in the air for us.

"Ready to roll back to the lift?" I asked softly, giving Katie's left hand a squeeze before easing my right thumb onto my joystick to execute a synchronized three-point turn on the smooth asphalt of Lions Way.

"Yeah," Katie said—and as we hummed side by side back toward Stairway A in the slanting gold November sunlight, the heavy sadness that had weighed on her voice five minutes earlier had lifted into quiet, stubborn hope.

Once we paused in line behind the lowered steel elevator platform of Bus 417 while the driver unclipped the safety chain, Katie looked across at me with earnest certainty in her blue eyes.

"Did you see her face when she put her hand up just now, Nate?" Katie whispered beneath the hydraulic hum of the lift. "Even standing right in the middle of Tyler's friends with nobody else around to back her up, she wouldn't touch that pack. She hasn't turned into them—she's just lonely walking out that gate by herself now that our chairs can't get over that curb. The sweet cousin we grew up with is still right inside her, and we aren't giving up on her."

"Not a chance," I agreed quietly, squeezing my twin sister's hand one more time before releasing her fingers for thirty seconds to guide my SwiftChair onto the waiting yellow bus lift toward home.
