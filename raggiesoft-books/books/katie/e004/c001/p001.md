## Tuesday, December 31, 2002, 9:30 PM — The Broken Ground Rules & The Gas-Station Run

"Brittany, Dad laid down the ground rules before he walked out that front door—nobody is allowed over here while he's up in Rhode Island, and there is zero alcohol allowed in this house!" I said sharply at nine-thirty on New Year's Eve, sitting in my compact SwiftChair power wheelchair in the front hallway beside Katie's chair as our seventeen-year-old maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher pulled the front door open to the cold December night.

Tonight was supposed to be about saving her.

It was Tuesday, December 31, 2002—the middle of our Senior year at Northwood High School, just over three months before Katie and I would turn eighteen on April 8. Ever since the Friday afternoon in September of our Junior year when the borrowed time in our legs had finally run out in the hallway at school, both Katie and I had used our compact power wheelchairs full-time from the moment we woke up until we went to bed at night.

Back when we were kids, Brittany had grown up down on her family's old rural farm off Blackwater Road—zoned for Blackwater Elementary and Pungo Ridge Middle while Katie and I went to Northwood Elementary and Morrison Middle—bonding deeply with the two of us during all the afternoons Grandma and Dad watched her at our house whenever Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve worked odd shift hours. Then, in the summer before ninth grade, Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve had finally sold the farm and moved to Earlton Court right across the Lions Way fence from Northwood High School. For our first two years of high school, Brittany, Katie, and I had done everything together—eating lunch at our corner table in Mrs. DeMarco's cafeteria, whispering over homework in Mrs. Periwinkle's library, walking through Smoker's Corner to Brittany's living room on Earlton Court, and promising to get an apartment and live together as three when we grew up. Even after Dad divorced our mother in August 2001, Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve had stood loyally beside Dad, Katie, and me, and Dad had made sure Brittany stayed welcome in our home. Once Katie and I moved into our SwiftChairs in eleventh grade and the uncut vertical yellow curb at the Macgregory Street gate cut us off from walking home with her, however, Brittany had fallen in with Tyler's hard-partying crowd out at Smoker's Corner. Still, Katie and I had never forgotten watching through that chain-link fence last autumn when one of Tyler's friends held out a pack of cigarettes to Brittany and she shook her head firmly and refused to touch it—convincing both of us that the sweet cousin we loved was still inside her somewhere.

Up in Newport, Rhode Island—Dad and Grandma's hometown, where Grandma had moved back home in 1998 to live near Dad's sister Aunt Elizabeth and our older cousin Chelsea—Grandma had come down with a bad winter bronchitis over Christmas and couldn't travel south to Virginia on the ARTS train. Because hauling two heavy power wheelchairs nine hours north up Interstate 95 into New England winter weather for a brief New Year's visit would have been exhausting, Katie and I had stayed home in Virginia Beach while Dad drove up to Newport, Rhode Island to check on Grandma, Aunt Liz, and Chelsea for the New Year. Holding onto the memory of Brittany turning down that cigarette at Smoker's Corner, Katie and I were the ones who had asked Dad and Aunt Karen to let Brittany spend New Year's Eve at our house while Dad was out of state—both so an able-bodied person would be under the roof in case of a winter power outage and because we wanted to try to rescue her from Tyler's crowd. In our minds, if we could just pull Brittany away from those kids for one safe, warm New Year's Eve—eating pizza, drinking sparkling apple cider, and laughing together the way the three of us used to in ninth and tenth grade—maybe she would come back to us. Before Dad backed his Holt sedan down the driveway to head north for Rhode Island, he had stood right in the center of our living room and laid down the law: the front door was to stay deadbolted, nobody else was allowed over at the house, and there was zero alcohol allowed under his roof.

Instead, with Dad four hundred and fifty miles away in Newport, Brittany had picked up the cordless kitchen phone and invited the exact wrong crowd we had been trying to rescue her from straight to our front door.

Now, standing on our aluminum front porch ramp in the porch light were five teenagers from that hard-partying Northwood High crowd—along with a tall, scruffy twenty-one-year-old guy named Derek who had just used his vertical driver's license to do a run to the corner gas station out on Military Highway, carrying two heavy plastic grocery bags clinking with glass bottles and cardboard twelve-packs.

"Oh, chill out, Nate—don't be such a pair of babies," Brittany scoffed, waving Derek and the other four kids past our wheelchairs into our small living room. "Uncle David is all the way up in Newport, Rhode Island with your grandma and Aunt Liz until tomorrow night! We'll throw the empties in the outside trash bin in the morning and nobody will ever know unless you two snitch."

"This isn't your house, Brittany, it's ours!" Katie said firmly, her left hand reaching across our armrests to grip my right hand as Derek dumped the plastic bags onto our kitchen counter with a loud crash of bottles. "Our dad works his tail off to pay the rent on this place, and we don't want strangers drinking in our living room!"

"Well, they're already here, and Derek already spent forty bucks at the gas station," Brittany shot back, grabbing two plastic cups from the dish rack as one of her friends cranked the volume knob on our living-room stereo up until the bass rattled the window blinds. "If you two don't want to party, go play on your computers in your room and leave us alone."

## Tuesday, December 31, 2002, 11:15 PM — Hiding Behind a Locked Bedroom Door

By eleven-fifteen that night, our living room had devolved into a loud, sour-smelling war zone.

Katie and I had stayed near the hallway threshold as long as we could to make sure nobody broke Dad's things, but as the gas-station bottles emptied over the next ninety minutes, Brittany's guests turned into every kind of drunk at once.

Over on the living-room sofa, one boy in a hooded sweatshirt was already passed out cold with his mouth open and an empty can rolling across the carpet beneath his sneakers, while a girl beside him was slumped sideways over the armrest, half-conscious and groaning that the room was spinning. Near the kitchen doorway, Derek and another guy in a baseball cap had turned mean and belligerent—shouting profanities inches from each other's faces and shoving kitchen chairs out of the way in an angry argument over who owed whom ten dollars for the gas-station run. And right in the middle of the rug, Brittany and her friend Jenna were completely loopy—stumbling into the coffee table, slurring every third word, and cackling at the top of their lungs as they spilled sticky soda and liquor across the floor.

For Katie and me, the smell of stale alcohol drifting down the hallway and the sound of angry, slurred shouting inside our own home hit every raw nerve left behind by our mother on the cruise ship sixteen months earlier.

When Derek slammed his palm against the kitchen wall so hard that the framed family photos in the hallway crooked on their nails, Katie looked at me with wide eyes and gripped my wrist.

"Nate, I don't want to be out here near them anymore," Katie whispered over the roar of the stereo and the shouting. "If Derek or that other guy trips over our wheelchairs while they're fighting, neither of us can stand up to get out of the way."

"Grab that unopened box of dry cereal off the lower pantry shelf so we have food for the rest of the night and don't have to come back out here," I told her immediately, snagging two cans of ginger ale on my lap tray while Katie tucked the family-size box of toasted oat cereal onto her lap. "We're locking ourselves in your room for the night."

As we backed our two compact power wheelchairs down the short hallway toward Katie's bedroom at the back corner of the house, one of Brittany's guests—a loudmouth senior from Northwood High named Tyler who had been chugging out of a plastic cup by the stereo—spotted us retreating and stumbled down the hall after us.

"Hey! Where do you two think you're rolling off to?" Tyler slurred, thrusting a red plastic cup toward my face as I steered Katie inside the bedroom ahead of me. "Come on, Prairie, stop acting like you're too good for the rave and take a drink!"

"We don't drink, Tyler—stay out of our hallway," I told him flatly.

Before Tyler could take another step, I backed my power chair across the threshold into Katie's bedroom, swung the solid wooden bedroom door shut right in his flushed, angry face, twisted the brass privacy lock until it clicked, and wedged the heavy rubber doorstop beneath the bottom gap.

On the other side of the oak panel, Tyler banged his fist twice against the door and yelled spitefully over the bass of the stereo, *"Fine, lock yourselves in a bedroom all night, you stuck-up losers! See if anybody cares!"*

For good measure, once Katie and I used our armrests to transfer onto the edge of her bed side by side beneath the framed blue butterfly print from our old house, I used my joystick to inch my fifty-pound power wheelchair right up against the inside of the doorknob like a steel roadblock.

Even through the closed door, we could still hear the muffled thudding of boots, the angry shouting from the kitchen, and the loopy, off-key shrieking from the living room.

Pulling Katie's green-and-gold quilt around both of our shoulders, I opened the top flaps of the cardboard cereal box between us so we had handfuls of dry cereal to munch on for dinner, switched the small thirteen-inch television on her dresser to the GBS New Year's Eve broadcast from Times Square with the volume turned low, and wrapped my right arm around my twin sister's shoulders while my left hand held hers.

## Wednesday, January 1, 2003, 12:00 AM — Why I Never Had a Twenty-First Birthday Drink

"Ten minutes to midnight," Katie murmured, resting her blonde head against my shoulder as the crowd in Times Square cheered on the small screen and another loud crash of glass echoed from the kitchen on the other side of our locked bedroom door. "Nate, we literally asked Dad to let Brittany come over tonight because we wanted to rescue her from that crowd. Back in ninth and tenth grade, the three of us did everything together—we used to talk about getting an apartment and living together as three when we grew up. And instead, the first chance she got, she brought that exact crowd into our house and told us to go hide in a bedroom."

"I know, Katie—and you can't rescue somebody who chooses that crowd over the family who tried to look out for her," I said quietly, squeezing her hand while glancing at the cordless extension handset on Katie's nightstand beside the two unopened cans of ginger ale we had brought into the bedroom on our lap trays. "If Dad were anywhere in Hampton Roads tonight, we would have quietly called him from the bedroom phone an hour ago and asked him to come to the house immediately. With him nine hours north in Newport with Grandma, Aunt Liz, and Chelsea, calling him right before midnight wouldn't get him down Interstate 95 tonight—it would just scare him half to death while he's stuck in Rhode Island. First thing in the morning, though, we're calling him, and Dad is going to hit the roof."

For a long moment, I sat there listening to the contrast between the two sides of that bedroom door: out in the living room, kids our age were passed out on the floor, screaming at each other in drunken rage, or stumbling around out of their minds—while in here, Katie and I were safe, clear-headed, and holding onto each other.

Up until that exact night at seventeen years old—four months shy of my eighteenth birthday in April—I had always assumed that when it came to alcohol, I was just waiting until my twenty-first birthday like everybody else to have my first legal drink. I was a rule-follower by nature; I didn't break laws, so I had figured that once I turned twenty-one, I'd probably try a glass of beer or wine just to see what the big deal was.

Sitting on the edge of that bed with Katie's hand in mine, listening to Derek's slurred shouting and Brittany's loopy giggling through the drywall—and remembering the look on our mother's face on Deck 4 of the *Sunward Tropics* right before she landed in the ship's brig—something settled permanently inside my chest.

"Katie?" I said softly as the digital clock on the television screen ticked to 11:59 PM and the glittering crystal ball began its sixty-second descent down the flagpole in Times Square.

"Yeah, Nate?" Katie asked, looking up at me.

"You know how I always said I was waiting until my twenty-first birthday to have my first legal drink?" I said, popping the tabs on our two cold cans of ginger ale and handing one to my sister. "Listening to what's going on in our living room tonight—some of them passed out cold, some of them furious and trying to fight each other, some of them acting like they lost half their brains—I don't ever want to end up like that. Not even once. When we turn twenty-one, I'm not having a twenty-first birthday drink. I'm never touching the stuff for the rest of my life."

Katie looked at the can of ginger ale in her hand, then looked up into my eyes with a fierce, unmistakable pride.

"Me neither, Nate," Katie said, clinking her ginger ale can gently against mine just as *12:00 AM — HAPPY NEW YEAR 2003* flashed across the small screen and confetti snowed over Times Square. "Why would either one of us ever put something in our bodies that turns people into *that*—when we can stay sharp, write our code, and take care of each other?"

"Happy New Year, Katie," I whispered, setting my ginger ale on the nightstand and pulling my twin sister into a tight, two-armed hug under the quilt.

"Happy New Year, Twin Brother," Katie whispered back, hugging me tight beneath the quilt while the drunken shouting in the living room finally began stumbling out toward the street—the two of us holding fast behind our locked bedroom door until morning light, when one quiet phone call up to Newport, Rhode Island was going to make sure Brittany never set foot inside our home again.
