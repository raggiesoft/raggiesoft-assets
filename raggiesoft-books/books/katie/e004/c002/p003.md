## Wednesday, January 8, 2003, 9:05 AM — Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen

"Mr. Callahan, before the tardy bell rings, would it be okay if Nate and I moved back to the corner table by the network cabinet for the spring semester?" Katie asked quietly at five minutes past nine on Wednesday morning, rolling her compact black SwiftChair up beside mine in front of the teacher's oak desk in Room 204.

Mr. Callahan paused with a stack of laminated spring syllabus sheets in his hands and looked over the rims of his reading glasses—first at the wide accessible front table beside the hallway door where Katie, Heather, and I had sat shoulder to shoulder every Gold Day from September until Christmas break, and then down at the dark hollows under my eyes.

"Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen have the same raised knee clearance under the tabletop, Katie," Mr. Callahan said gently, keeping his voice low enough that the three early-arriving seniors hanging up winter coats by the chalkboard couldn't overhear. "Did the three of you want to split up into two project teams for the spring database unit, or is everything all right?"

"Just Katie and me on a two-person team this semester, sir," I said, keeping my gaze steady on the edge of his blotter so my voice wouldn't waver. "We won't fall behind on the lab checkpoints."

For two quiet seconds, Mr. Callahan studied the way Katie's right hand was already resting protectively over my left wrist between our armrests. Whatever he guessed about high school Januarys, he had the grace not to pry.

"Thirteen and Fourteen are yours," Mr. Callahan said with a brief, understanding nod, tapping four keys on his master desktop terminal to reassign our network drive logins. "Go ahead and get logged in before the bell."

"Thank you, Mr. Callahan," Katie murmured.

Side by side, we reversed cleanly out from his desk, steered down the wide linoleum aisle past rows of humming beige CRT monitors, and parked our powerchairs beneath the frosted rear windows and the felt blue-and-gold *Northwood Lions* and *Class of 2003* pennants pinned above the bulletin board. I clicked my wheel locks into place, angled our shared seventeen-inch monitor three-quarters toward Katie's eyes and mine, and tapped our password into the gray *Quantum Basic 6.0* startup prompt.

At nine-oh-nine, sixty seconds before the second-block bell, Heather Collins walked briskly through the open doorway with her green canvas binder tucked against her ribs.

Even without turning my head, I caught her reflection in the angled glass bezel of our monitor. She came through the door with her shoulders squared and her chin lifted hard—clearly braced to tell me to move my wheelchair away from hers—and then stopped dead in the front aisle when she saw nothing beside Workstation Three except two empty, scuffed patches of waxed floor where Katie's tires and mine had rested for four straight months.

Slowly, Heather glanced toward the back of the lab.

The instant her eyes found the two of us tucked quietly beside the beige server cabinet in the far corner, I shifted my focus straight onto our glowing cursor and kept every muscle in my face still. She had told me at eight-oh-five on Monday morning not to call her house, not to wait at the bus loop, and never to talk to her again. I wasn't going to break a single word of that instruction, and I wasn't going to give her a reason to think I was begging.

Up front, the bell rang. Heather sank slowly onto the plastic chair at Workstation Three all by herself, staring at the blank monitor in front of her while Mr. Callahan stepped to the whiteboard, introduced the January relational-table assignment, and clicked off the front bank of fluorescent tubes so the class could get to work.

Down at the bottom of the canvas backpack hanging behind my backrest, my round silver portable CD player sat zipped inside its padded pouch with Crimson Node's debut disc still resting on the spindle from the night before. At Northwood High in 2003, slipping foam headphones over your ears during a class period was the fastest way in the building to earn an automatic afternoon detention slip and get your player confiscated until Friday dismissal.

I didn't need the headphones anyway.

The moment the room hushed to the soft clatter of twenty keyboards and my eyes brushed the back of Heather's empty front row, my mind pressed play on Track Eight all by itself.

It wasn't a vague hum or a half-remembered chorus the way people talked about getting a radio jingle stuck in their heads. Ever since Katie and I were little kids on Arvin Road, any song I knew by heart could play inside my skull with every single instrument in its exact stereo place—as vivid and loud as if studio speakers were wired straight behind my eardrums. Right there under the pale winter window light of Room 204, the cold, exposed piano intro of *Unhandled Exception* echoed note for note through the quiet chamber of my head without a single drumbeat underneath it.

*The screen went dark without a single sound,* the trembling voice sang clear as day inside my mind while my fingers typed field definitions across the screen on pure muscle memory. *I'm searching for a signal that cannot be found. I checked the routing, I ran the trace, but there is just a shadow in your physical space. No warning lights, no system alert—just the sudden, silent gravity of the hurt.*

My throat tightened so sharply that my right hand stalled over the Enter key.

Right on cue, without needing to ask what was wrong, Katie slid her warm right hand across the narrow gap between our armrests and laced her fingers firmly through my left.

Inside my head, the mournful piano shattered into that relentless hundred-and-thirty-beat-per-minute snare drum and pulsing bassline, driving straight into the second verse as if the song had been written from the ceiling tiles of Room 204: *Player Two holds my hand as the temperature drops—we wait for the ping, but the heartbeat just stops!*

"Keep breathing with me, Nate," Katie whispered barely above the hum of the server cabinet fans, her thumb brushing once across my knuckles while her left hand reached over to finish typing the variable declaration on our shared keyboard. "One line at a time. Nobody up front gets to touch us back here."

I nodded once, swallowing hard against the ache behind my ribs while the raw, stripped-bare bridge tore through my head—*I never got to say goodbye, I never understood why, I am sitting in the wreckage just watching the sky*—until the fast drums cut out at the four-minute-and-nineteen-second mark, leaving only those three cold piano lines before the burst of static slammed the door shut:

*No rollback. No restore point. Just empty space.*

## Tuesday, February 11, 2003, 11:48 AM — Cafeteria Table Twelve & Cousin Hannah's Green Parka

"Trade you half my cinnamon applesauce for the unbroken pretzel twists at the bottom of your bag," Katie offered at twelve minutes to noon on a sleety Tuesday in the second week of February, sliding her blue insulated lunch pouch across the accessible end of Table Twelve near the wide double doors of the Northwood Commons cafeteria.

"Deal, as long as you didn't shake the salt off them," I replied, managing the faintest corner of a real smile as I popped the tab on two cold green cans of ginger ale—one for Katie and one for me—and set them beside my open spiral notebook of Hampton Roads bus headways.

Five weeks had passed since that Monday morning outside Room 204.

Every B-Lunch since January sixth, Katie and I had parked our SwiftChairs side by side right here at Table Twelve. We didn't sit with a noisy crowd, and there was certainly no mysterious blonde party girl hovering around my wheelchair—because there had never been another girl in my life to begin with. Every morning on the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority lift bus down Military Highway, every hallway pass between blocks, every afternoon coding session at Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen, and every evening around our living-room coffee table on Arvin Road with Dad, the only girl beside me was my twin sister.

One narrow aisle to our right, Heather sat near the end of Table Ten picking quietly at a cup of yogurt beside two girls from her first-block AP English class. Over the past three weeks, Katie had noticed before I did that the hard, righteous glare Heather had worn during the second week of January had vanished—replaced by a quiet, puzzled frown whenever she saw Katie and me rolling down the B-Wing corridor strictly by ourselves, day after day after day.

Six feet past Heather's shoulder at the noisy varsity table along the brick pillar, Tyler dropped his backpack onto an empty chair, leaned both elbows on the laminate tabletop in his letterman jacket, and lowered his voice into the exact theatrical, mock-sympathetic stage whisper that still made my stomach turn whenever I heard him in the hall.

"Look, Megan, I seriously hate to be the guy who has to tell you this," Tyler said loudly enough for three surrounding tables to hear every syllable, flashing a smug, knowing grimace across the table at senior girls' soccer captain Megan Porter. "Especially right before Valentine's Day and all. Seriously, though, somebody's gotta look out for you."

Megan paused with a turkey sandwich halfway to her mouth and raised one dark eyebrow. "Tell me what, Tyler?"

"Saturday night around nine-thirty out behind the movie theater at Kempsville Plaza," Tyler said, leaning in as two of his buddies snickered into their soda cups. "I walked past your boyfriend's blue pickup in the back row by the light pole. Lucas wasn't at home studying like he told you, Megan. He had his arms wrapped tight around some pretty redhead in a forest-green parka right beside the passenger door, and then the two of them climbed into his cab together and drove off toward Independence Boulevard. Thought you deserved to know what kind of guy you're dating."

At Table Twelve, Katie's hand froze halfway to her ginger ale can. My own breath caught in my chest. It was word-for-word the exact same poison—the same fake concern, the same invented sleaze built out of a ten-second glimpse from across a parking lot.

Except Megan Porter didn't crumble, cry, or turn on her boyfriend.

Instead, Megan set her sandwich neatly back onto its wax paper, stared across the table at Tyler as if he had rocks rattling around inside his skull, and let out a sharp, ringing laugh that cut clean across our entire corner of the cafeteria.

"A pretty redhead in a forest-green parka?" Megan repeated, her voice carrying clearly past every silent tray within twenty feet. "You mean his nineteen-year-old cousin *Hannah*, Tyler—his mom's sister's daughter who goes to Virginia Dominion University and called Lucas on Saturday night because she blew a front tire behind the Kempsville cinema and needed his truck jack?! I was literally on the kitchen landline with Lucas's mom while he drove over there to put her spare doughnut on!"

Half of Megan's soccer teammates burst out laughing around the table, while Tyler's ears turned the color of a fire alarm box.

"Hey, whatever, man," Tyler muttered defensively, shoving back from the table and snatching up his tray as his smirk evaporated. "All I'm saying is it looked sketchy from across the lot—"

"What's sketchy is you making up trashy cheating stories every time you see a guy standing next to a girl in his own family!" Megan shot right back, shaking her head in open disgust as Tyler beat a hasty retreat toward the dish-return window. "Honestly, Tyler, get a life! Everybody who actually knows Lucas knows the only women in his life are his mom, his cousin Hannah, and me!"

*Clang.*

Six feet across the aisle at Table Ten, a metal cafeteria spoon slipped out of Heather's fingers, hit the rim of her plastic tray, and clattered onto the linoleum floor.

Heather didn't bend down to pick it up.

When I glanced sideways for half a second, every trace of color had drained from Heather's cheeks, leaving the bridge of her nose stark white under the cafeteria lights. Her eyes were fixed first on the swinging gray doors of the dish-return alcove where Tyler had just disappeared, and then—slowly, dreadfully—her gaze turned across the six feet of scuffed floor to Table Twelve.

Right to my wheelchair. Right to Katie's blonde hair beside me. Right to our two hands resting side by side between an open zip-top bag of dry toasted oat cereal and two green cans of ginger ale.

I didn't have to guess what was happening behind Heather's wide, horrified hazel eyes. You could see every single piece of January sixth crashing down on her at once: how she had originally told her entire homeroom *That was his twin sister Katie, you idiot!*—how Tyler had looked her dead in the eye with that exact same smirk and sworn it was some random blonde party girl—how for five straight weeks since winter break she had watched me roll through every hallway, classroom, and bus loop at Northwood High without ever once seeing another girl anywhere near my life except my sister—and how she had just watched Megan Porter swat down the exact same lie in ten seconds flat because Megan actually trusted the boy she claimed to know.

Heather's chest rose in a ragged, silent gasp. Her hand trembled visibly against the edge of Table Ten, her lips parting as if she were about to push up from the bench and cross the aisle toward us with tears pooling along her lower lashes.

Beside me, Katie met Heather's eyes squarely across the narrow gap. Katie didn't glare, smirk, or gloat; she just gave one tiny, calm, unmistakable shake of her head and closed her fingers firmly over my hand.

My stomach felt hollow—not victorious, not vindicated, just tired right down to the marrow of my bones. Quietly, without saying a word or holding Heather's gaze a second longer, I looked back down at my bus-schedule notebook, picked up my mechanical pencil, and finished copying the Route 15 afternoon timetable while Heather sank back onto her bench, pressed both trembling palms hard over her mouth, and stared down at her untouched tray in sickened silence.

## Thursday, April 10, 2003, 2:05 PM — No Restore Point at the Bus Loop

Two days after Katie and I turned eighteen, an April afternoon breeze smelling of cut grass and warm Tidewater pine blew through the propped steel doors of the Northwood High bus loop as the final dismissal bell echoed down the first-floor corridor.

All through late February, March, and the first week of April, Room 204 had remained as quiet as a chapel every Gold-Day morning.

Heather never spoke out of turn or caused a scene in Mr. Callahan's lab—she respected the boundary she herself had slammed down on January sixth too much to corner me in front of twenty classmates—yet anyone with eyes could see how badly the truth was eating her alive from the inside out. Whenever she turned in a printed database query at Mr. Callahan's desk, she walked back to Workstation Three with her shoulders hunched small inside her cardigan, glancing once toward our back corner with pale, hollowed-out regret before dropping her eyes to the floor. Twice—once beside the library book-return slot on a rainy Friday in late February, and again twenty minutes ago outside the doorway of Room 204 when second block let out—she had lingered three paces off to the side of the hallway traffic with a small folded square of notebook paper gripped tightly between her fingers, taking half a breath as if she were praying I might slow my SwiftChair down long enough to let her speak.

Both times, Katie and I simply rolled past at a steady walking pace, looking straight ahead toward the elevator.

I didn't hate Heather. Even after three months of sitting in the back row with *Unhandled Exception* echoing inside my skull whenever the classroom went quiet, I never wished a single bad thing on her, and whenever guys in my third-block government class noticed we weren't sitting together anymore and fished for gossip, I shut them down with a flat *"It didn't work out"* and changed the subject. What we had shared back in the autumn—holding hands across the second-floor bridge of Chesapeake Bay Mall, cheering our hundred-percent compiler grade before Christmas, hugging tight on our Arvin Road sofa while she told me my bond with my twin sister was the best part of who I was—had been completely real when it happened.

And that was the exact reason there was nothing left to say over a folded piece of notebook paper in April.

If eighteen months of knowing my heart, sitting on our couch with my dad and my sister, and hearing me promise never to touch alcohol could be wiped out in sixty seconds by a vindictive liar's smirk in homeroom—without even letting Katie and me finish two sentences in our own defense—then whatever bridge used to connect Workstation Three to Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen didn't have a single plank left standing. As the final cold piano chord of Track Eight always reminded my brain before the static hit, there was no rollback, and there was no restore point.

"Ramp's coming down on Bus Nine-Twelve, birthday boy," Katie said warmly beside me on the sun-warmed concrete curb, giving my left hand two quick squeezes as our blue-and-silver Route 15 low-floor bus pulled smoothly up to the yellow accessible boarding square and knelt with a familiar pneumatic sigh. "Dad left a note on the counter saying he picked up fresh strawberries to go with the leftover birthday cake once we get back to Arvin Road."

"Save me the corner piece with the extra blue frosting," I said, the knot in my chest easing as the hydraulic ramp settled flush against the concrete in front of our front casters.

Behind us near the brick columns of the main entrance, students surged out toward the senior parking lot and the waiting row of buses. Neither Katie nor I looked back over our shoulders toward the doorway. Letting go of each other's hands only long enough to steer up the ramp one right after the other, we locked our SwiftChairs into the twin wheelchair bays behind the driver, laced our inner hands right back together as the doors hissed shut, and rode home into the spring sunlight side by side.
