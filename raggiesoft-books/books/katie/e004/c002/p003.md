## Wednesday, January 8, 2003, 9:05 AM — Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen

"Mr. Callahan, before the tardy bell rings, would it be okay if Nate and I moved back to the corner table by the network cabinet for the spring semester?" Katie asked quietly at five minutes past nine on Wednesday morning, rolling her compact black SwiftChair up beside mine in front of the teacher's oak desk in Room 204.

Mr. Callahan paused with a stack of laminated spring syllabus sheets in his hands and looked over the rims of his reading glasses—first at the wide accessible front table beside the hallway door where Katie, Heather, and I had sat shoulder to shoulder every Gold Day from September until Christmas break, and then down at the dark hollows under my eyes.

"Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen have the same raised knee clearance under the tabletop, Katie," Mr. Callahan said gently, keeping his voice low enough that the three early-arriving seniors hanging up winter coats by the chalkboard couldn't overhear. "Did the three of you want to split up into two project teams for the spring database unit, or is everything all right?"

"Just Katie and me on a two-person team this semester, sir," I said, keeping my gaze steady on the edge of his blotter so my voice wouldn't waver. "We won't fall behind on the lab checkpoints."

For two quiet seconds, Mr. Callahan studied the way Katie's right hand was already resting protectively over my left wrist between our armrests. Whatever he guessed about high school Januarys, he had the grace not to pry.

"Thirteen and Fourteen are yours," Mr. Callahan said with a brief, understanding nod, tapping four keys on his master desktop terminal to reassign our network drive logins. "Go ahead and get logged in before the bell."

"Thank you, Mr. Callahan," Katie murmured.

Side by side, we reversed cleanly out from his desk, steered all the way down the wide linoleum aisle past every row of humming beige CRT monitors, and parked our powerchairs right against the painted cinderblock rear corner wall beneath the frosted windows and the felt *Northwood Lions* and *Class of 2003* pennants—with nobody behind our backrests except the humming beige network cabinet. Because we each still had our own individual lab worksheets and written reflections to turn in alongside our two-person database project, Katie rolled up to Workstation Thirteen while I locked my brakes right beside her at Workstation Fourteen. Angling each of our seventeen-inch CRT monitors toward our own eyes, we logged into *Quantum OS XN* as the screens in the rows ahead of us flickered to life with blue title bars—half the seniors typing essay headers in *WritePad 2003* and the rest scrolling research pages in their internet browsers before opening *Quantum Basic 6.0*.

At nine-oh-nine, sixty seconds before the second-block bell, Heather Collins walked briskly through the open doorway up front with her green canvas binder tucked against her ribs.

Even without turning my head, I saw her stop just inside the front aisle across two rows of glowing monitors. She had come through the door with her shoulders squared and her chin lifted hard—clearly braced to tell me to move my wheelchair away from hers—only to freeze beside Workstation Three when she saw nothing next to her chair except two empty, scuffed patches of waxed floor where Katie's tires and mine had rested for four straight months.

Slowly, Heather looked all the way back toward the rear corner of the lab.

The instant her eyes found Katie and me tucked side by side at Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen against the back wall, I shifted my focus straight onto my own glowing monitor and kept every muscle in my face still. She had told me at eight-oh-five on Monday morning not to call her house, not to wait at the bus loop, and never to talk to her again. I wasn't going to break a single word of that instruction, and I wasn't going to give her a reason to think I was begging—even if seeing her standing up there alone sent a dull, physical ache straight through my ribs.

Up front, the bell rang. Heather sank slowly onto the plastic chair at Workstation Three all by herself, opening a blank document window on her screen while Mr. Callahan stepped to the whiteboard, introduced the January relational-table assignment, and clicked off the front bank of overhead tubes so the class could get to work.

Down at the bottom of the canvas backpack hanging behind my backrest, my round silver portable CD player sat zipped inside its padded pouch with Crimson Node's debut disc still resting on the spindle from the night before. At Northwood High in 2003, slipping foam headphones over your ears during a class period was the fastest way in the building to earn an automatic afternoon detention slip and get your player confiscated until Friday dismissal.

I didn't need the headphones anyway.

The moment the room hushed to the clatter of twenty keyboards ahead of us and my eyes brushed the back of Heather's maroon cardigan two rows forward, my mind pressed play on Track Eight all by itself.

It wasn't a vague hum or a half-remembered chorus the way people talked about getting a radio jingle stuck in their heads. Ever since Katie and I were little kids on Arvin Road, any song I knew by heart could play inside my skull with every single instrument in its exact stereo place—as vivid and loud as if studio speakers were wired straight behind my eardrums. Right there in the back corner of Room 204, the cold, exposed piano intro of *Unhandled Exception* echoed note for note through the quiet chamber of my head without a single drumbeat underneath it.

*The screen went dark without a single sound,* the trembling voice sang clear as day inside my mind while my fingers typed my individual schema header into Workstation Fourteen on pure muscle memory. *I'm searching for a signal that cannot be found. I checked the routing, I ran the trace, but there is just a shadow in your physical space. No warning lights, no system alert—just the sudden, silent gravity of the hurt.*

My throat tightened so sharply that my right hand stalled over my Enter key.

Right on cue, without needing to ask what was wrong, Katie paused her typing beside me, slid her warm left hand across the narrow gap between our armrests, and laced her fingers firmly through my right.

Inside my head, the mournful piano shattered into that relentless hundred-and-thirty-beat-per-minute snare drum and pulsing bassline, driving straight into the second verse as if the song had been written from the ceiling tiles of Room 204: *Player Two holds my hand as the temperature drops—we wait for the ping, but the heartbeat just stops!*

"Keep breathing with me, Nate," Katie whispered barely above the hum of the corner server cabinet, her thumb brushing once across my knuckles while her right hand rested near her own keyboard beside her glowing document window. "One line at a time. Nobody up front gets to touch us back here."

I nodded once, swallowing hard against the ache behind my ribs while the raw, stripped-bare bridge tore through my head—*I never got to say goodbye, I never understood why, I am sitting in the wreckage just watching the sky*—until the fast drums cut out at the four-minute-and-nineteen-second mark, leaving only those three cold piano lines before the burst of static slammed the door shut:

*No rollback. No restore point. Just empty space.*

## Tuesday, February 11, 2003, 11:48 AM — Cafeteria Table Twelve & Cousin Hannah's Green Parka

"Trade you half my cinnamon applesauce for the unbroken pretzel twists at the bottom of your bag," Katie offered at twelve minutes to noon on a sleety Tuesday in the second week of February, sliding her blue insulated lunch pouch across the accessible end of Table Twelve near the wide double doors of the Northwood Commons cafeteria.

"Deal, as long as you didn't shake the salt off them," I replied, managing the faintest corner of a real smile as I popped the tab on two cold green cans of ginger ale—one for Katie and one for me—and set them beside my open spiral notebook of Hampton Roads bus headways.

Five weeks had passed since that Monday morning outside Room 204.

Every B-Lunch since January sixth, Katie and I had parked our SwiftChairs side by side right here at Table Twelve. We didn't sit with a noisy crowd, and there was certainly no mysterious blonde party girl hovering around my wheelchair—because there had never been another girl in my life to begin with. Every weekday morning when Mr. Henderson and Miss Gloria loaded our wheelchairs onto Bus 417 at Arvin Road and lowered us onto the covered walkway at Stairway A, every hallway pass between blocks, every afternoon coding session side by side at Workstations Thirteen and Fourteen, and every evening around our living-room coffee table with Dad, the only girl beside me was my twin sister.

One narrow aisle to our right, Heather sat near the end of Table Ten picking quietly at a cup of yogurt beside two girls from her first-block AP English class. Over the past three weeks, Katie had noticed before I did that the hard, righteous glare Heather had worn during the second week of January had vanished—replaced by a quiet, puzzled frown whenever she saw Katie and me rolling down the B-Wing corridor strictly by ourselves, day after day after day.

Six feet past Heather's shoulder at the noisy varsity table along the brick pillar, Tyler dropped his backpack onto an empty chair, leaned both elbows on the laminate tabletop in his letterman jacket, and lowered his voice into the exact theatrical, mock-sympathetic stage whisper that still made my stomach turn whenever I heard him in the hall.

"Look, Kristen, I seriously hate to be the guy who has to tell you this," Tyler said loudly enough for three surrounding tables to hear every syllable, flashing a smug, knowing grimace across the table at senior girls' soccer captain Kristen Bauer. "Especially right before Valentine's Day and all. Seriously, though, somebody's gotta look out for you."

Kristen paused with a turkey sandwich halfway to her mouth and raised one dark eyebrow. "Tell me what, Tyler?"

"Saturday night around nine-thirty out behind the movie theater at Kempsville Plaza," Tyler said, leaning in close with a greasy, practiced grin as two of his buddies snickered into their soda cups. "I walked past your boyfriend's blue pickup in the back row by the light pole. Gavin wasn't at home studying like he told you, Kristen. He had his arms wrapped tight around some pretty redhead in a forest-green parka right beside the passenger door, and then the two of them climbed into his cab together and drove off toward Independence Boulevard. Thought you deserved to know what kind of guy you're dating—so dump the zero before Friday and let me take you out for Valentine's Day instead."

At Table Twelve, Katie's hand froze halfway to her ginger ale can. My own breath caught in my chest. Not only was the story word-for-word the exact same opening bait—the same fake concern, the same invented sleaze built out of a ten-second glimpse from across a parking lot—he was shamelessly using it as a sleazy rebound pickup line right in front of us.

Kristen set her sandwich neatly back onto its wax paper, stared across the table at Tyler as if he had rocks rattling around inside his skull, and let out a sharp, incredulous laugh.

"A pretty redhead in a forest-green parka, you idiot?" Kristen said loud enough for four tables to hear—using almost the exact words Heather had originally used in Homeroom 212 back on January sixth. "That was Gavin's nineteen-year-old cousin *Hannah*, Tyler—his mom's sister's daughter who goes to Virginia Dominion University and called Gavin on Saturday night because she blew a front tire behind the Kempsville cinema and needed his truck jack!"

Stung at being called an idiot in front of the varsity table, Tyler didn't back down—instead, his lip curled into that exact same practiced, pitying smirk as he doubled down straight into his second-stage lie.

"Keep telling yourself that, Bauer!" Tyler scoffed loudly, leaning forward on his forearms so everyone around them would hear him override her. "I know what Gavin's cousin looks like—that wasn't his cousin! That was some totally different redhead, and nobody hugs their cousin like that before hopping into a pickup cab together! Seriously, ditch him and come out with me Friday night—"

That was the exact moment where Heather had cracked in Homeroom 212.

Kristen Bauer didn't budge a millimeter.

"Oh, save the gross pickup act and stop lying through your teeth, Tyler—you've literally never met his cousin Hannah in your life!" Kristen shot right back without flinching, her clear voice ringing across our entire corner of the cafeteria. "It *was* his cousin Hannah! I helped Gavin's mom pick out that exact forest-green parka for Hannah's birthday at Emerald Creek Mall, and I was sitting on the kitchen landline on three-way speaker with Gavin's mom *and* Hannah while he bolted her spare doughnut tire on in the theater lot!"

Around the varsity table, half of Kristen's soccer teammates burst out laughing—and the tall midfielder sitting beside Kristen rolled her eyes in open disgust.

"Every girl in the senior class knows your slimy little routine by now, Tyler!" Kristen's teammate called out over the laughter while Tyler's ears turned the color of a fire alarm box. "You did the exact same thing to two different girls on varsity track last fall! Every single time you want to get with a girl who already has a boyfriend, you make up some fake story about seeing her guy with another woman so you can swoop in and ask her out!"

"Hey, whatever, man," Tyler muttered defensively, shoving back from the table and snatching up his tray as his smirk evaporated completely. "All I'm saying is it looked sketchy from across the lot—"

"What's sketchy is a total sleazebag making up trashy cheating stories about guys standing next to their own sisters and cousins just because nobody single will go near you!" Kristen called right after him, shaking her head in disgust as Tyler beat a hasty retreat toward the dish-return window—a public roasting so brutal that word of it echoed down every locker bay before fourth block, making Tuesday, February eleventh the absolute last time Tyler ever dared try that stunt on a girl at Northwood High. "Honestly, Tyler, get a life! Everybody who actually knows Gavin knows the only women in his life are his mom, his cousin Hannah, and me!"

*Clang.*

Six feet across the aisle at Table Ten, a metal cafeteria spoon slipped out of Heather's fingers, hit the rim of her plastic tray, and clattered onto the linoleum floor.

Heather didn't bend down to pick it up.

When I glanced sideways for half a second, every trace of color had drained from Heather's cheeks, leaving the bridge of her nose stark white under the cafeteria lights. Her eyes were fixed first on the swinging gray doors of the dish-return alcove where Tyler had just disappeared, and then—slowly, dreadfully—her gaze turned across the six feet of scuffed floor to Table Twelve.

Right to my wheelchair. Right to Katie's straight blonde hair beside me—the exact blonde hair Tyler had glimpsed from behind in our hallway on New Year's Eve and twisted into a party stranger. Right to our two hands resting side by side between an open zip-top bag of dry toasted oat cereal and two green cans of ginger ale.

I didn't have to guess what was happening behind Heather's wide, horrified hazel eyes. You could see every single piece of January crashing down on her at once: how she too had initially pushed back in Homeroom 212 saying *That was his twin sister Katie, you idiot!*—how Tyler had looked her dead in the eye with that exact same *Keep telling yourself that!* smirk, doubled down insisting it wasn't Katie, and tried to hit on her himself once she broke things off—how for five straight weeks since winter break she had watched me roll through every hallway, classroom, and bus loop at Northwood High without ever once seeing another girl anywhere near my life except my sister—and how she had just realized she hadn't merely fallen for a rumor, she had thrown us away over a serial sleazebag's copy-paste pickup routine while Kristen Bauer saw straight through every word because Kristen actually trusted the boy she loved.

Heather's chest rose in a ragged, silent gasp. Her hand trembled visibly against the edge of Table Ten, her lips parting as if she were about to push up from the bench and cross the aisle toward us with tears pooling along her lower lashes.

Beside me, Katie met Heather's eyes squarely across the narrow gap. Katie didn't glare, smirk, or gloat; she just gave one tiny, calm, unmistakable shake of her head and closed her fingers firmly over my hand.

My stomach felt hollow—not victorious, not vindicated, just aching right down to the marrow of my bones because watching the truth hit her six weeks too late didn't bring back a single minute of what we had lost. Quietly, without saying a word or holding Heather's gaze a second longer, I looked back down at my bus-schedule notebook, picked up my mechanical pencil, and finished copying the Saturday Route 15 timetable while Heather sank back onto her bench, pressed both trembling palms hard over her mouth, and stared down at her untouched tray in sickened silence.

## Thursday, April 10, 2003, 2:05 PM — Stairway A, The Numbered Stalls, & What Still Hurts

Two days after Katie and I turned eighteen, an April afternoon breeze smelling of cut grass and warm Tidewater pine greeted us as we rode the interior elevator down from our separate fourth-block second-floor classes and rolled side by side through the wide double doors of Stairway A at the head of Northwood High's long covered concrete walkway.

![At two-oh-five on a sunny April afternoon outside Northwood High School—where the two-story 1992 red-brick building and covered concrete walkway continue from Stairway A past Stairway B toward Stairway C beyond the left edge of the frame—eighteen-year-old twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie roll side by side in matching black SwiftChair power wheelchairs along the wide concrete sidewalk outside Stairway A toward the lowered right-side hydraulic lift of short yellow Special Needs Bus 417 pulled alongside the unbroken yellow curb, while across the wide asphalt drive lane departing students exiting Stairways A, B, and C walk across the white crosswalk toward the regular yellow school buses parked side by side in their assigned numbered stalls and Heather Collins glances back over her shoulder with quiet sorrow.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/apr_2003_northwood_stairway_a_special_needs_bus_heather_regular_stalls.jpg)

Ever since Northwood High had opened in 1992, afternoon dismissal split the spacious asphalt bus loop into two completely separate paths. Every student leaving the building came out through Stairway A, Stairway B, or Stairway C along the long covered concrete walkway. Because Katie and I rode the school elevator down from our second-floor classes—and because there was no curb ramp cut into the raised yellow concrete curb outside Stairway A—Mr. Henderson always pulled Bus 417, our short yellow Special Needs school bus, right up on the asphalt curb lane facing Stairway A so the steel hydraulic wheelchair lift on the right side of the bus bridged straight over the unbroken curb onto the flat concrete sidewalk beside Miss Gloria and her aluminum clipboard. Across the wide asphalt drive lane, streams of students pouring out of Stairways A, B, and C stepped off the covered sidewalk onto the painted crosswalks and headed straight for the two dozen full-size fifty-four-passenger yellow school buses idling side by side in their assigned white-painted numbered diagonal parking stalls.

All through late February, March, and the first week of April, Room 204 had remained as quiet as a chapel every Gold-Day morning.

Heather never spoke out of turn or caused a scene in Mr. Callahan's lab—she respected the boundary she herself had slammed down on January sixth too much to corner me in front of twenty classmates—yet anyone with eyes could see how badly the truth was eating her alive from the inside out. Whenever she turned in a printed database assignment at Mr. Callahan's desk, she walked back to Workstation Three with her shoulders hunched small inside her cardigan, glancing once toward our back corner with pale, hollowed-out regret before dropping her eyes to the floor. Twice—once beside the library book-return slot on a rainy Friday in late February, and again earlier that day outside Room 204 when second block let out—she had lingered three paces off to the side of the upstairs hallway traffic with a small folded square of notebook paper gripped tightly between her fingers, taking half a breath as if she were praying I might slow my SwiftChair down long enough to let her speak.

Both times, Katie and I simply rolled past at a steady walking pace, looking straight ahead toward the second-floor elevator.

Now, further down the teal-trimmed sheltered walkway past the grass island, Heather stepped out from under the concrete canopy with her dark backpack slung over her shoulders—leaving the school building behind and walking out across the wide asphalt toward the row of regular yellow buses parked in the numbered middle stalls.

Halfway out toward the regular buses, Heather slowed her stride and turned her head back over her right shoulder toward Stairway A, watching Katie and me roll toward Bus 417.

I didn't hate Heather. Even after three months of sitting in the back corner of Room 204 with *Unhandled Exception* echoing clear as day inside my skull whenever the keyboards went quiet, I didn't have an ounce of malice toward her in my body. And standing on principle didn't mean my heart had turned to stone. The honest truth—the part I hadn't even said out loud at the dinner table—was that part of me still missed her terribly. I missed the sound of her laugh across our living room on Arvin Road, I missed the warmth of her arms wrapped tightly around my shoulders right out here on this sidewalk four days before Christmas, and I missed the gentle girl who had held my hand on the second-floor bridge of Chesapeake Bay Mall and told me my bond with Katie was the best part of who I was.

Yet even at eighteen, looking down at the yellow safety strip on Bus 417's lowered lift platform so my eyes wouldn't meet hers across the asphalt, I knew both things were true at the exact same time: part of me still missed Heather with an ache deep enough to steal my breath, and there was still no way back across what had broken on January sixth. If eighteen months of knowing my character, sitting on our couch with my dad and my sister, and hearing me promise never to touch alcohol could be erased in sixty seconds by Tyler's smirk in homeroom without even letting Katie and me finish two sentences in our own defense, then no folded note in April could rebuild that trust. There was no rollback, and there was no restore point.

"All clear on the platform for my birthday twins!" Miss Gloria called warmly over the diesel purr of Bus 417, tapping her clipboard as she unhooked the yellow safety chain for Katie first. "Come on aboard before those big buses out in the middle stalls start pulling out!"

"Thanks, Miss Gloria," Katie said gently, giving my left hand two steady squeezes before releasing my fingers just long enough to guide her SwiftChair smoothly onto the hydraulic lift and ride up into the cabin ahead of me.

Thirty seconds later, once Mr. Henderson raised me up behind her, clicked all eight floor tie-down hooks tightly onto our wheelchair frames, and pulled the folding lift doors shut, Bus 417 eased forward around the northwest turnaround curve—gliding right past the front bumpers of the regular yellow buses lined up down the middle of the loop as Heather climbed the front steps of the bus in Stall Nine without looking back again.

Staring out through the tinted school-bus glass as the red brick of Northwood High slipped behind the pine trees along Lions Way, I felt my throat close up all over again—and before I could stop it, a hot, silent tear spilled over my lower lashes and traced a slow line down my cheek. Knowing you could never go back didn't make the missing stop; sitting there in the warm April sun, I could already tell that losing Heather was the kind of quiet scar that wouldn't simply vanish after graduation, the kind that would still catch me off guard out of nowhere and sting my eyes whenever an old memory or a piano intro crossed my mind.

Right beside me across the narrow center aisle of Bus 417, Katie didn't tell me to cheer up, didn't say *I told you so,* and didn't pretend it didn't hurt. She just reached her right hand quietly across the gap between our tied-down armrests, brushed the tear off my thumb with hers, laced her fingers firmly through my left hand, and held on with me in the warm spring quiet all the way home to Arvin Road.
