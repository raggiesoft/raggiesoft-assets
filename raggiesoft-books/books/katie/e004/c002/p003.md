## Wednesday, September 17, 2003, 3:15 PM — Unplugging Apartment A6, Seeing Michael Sad Across the Hall, & Two Layers of Sandbags at the Top of the Lobby Ramp

![Outside the Greenway Court apartment building in Norfolk on Wednesday afternoon, September 17, 2003 ahead of Hurricane Isabel under overcast gray skies and rising wind, eighteen-year-old fraternal twins Nathaniel and Katie Prairie sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands while their father David Prairie steadies their chairs on the front walkway as Michael Ragsdale waves goodbye with his backpack on the sidewalk.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/sep_2003_hurricane_isabel_greenway_court_sandbags_evacuation_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Make sure both twenty-four-volt battery chargers are in the front zipper pocket of the duffel bag, Nate," our forty-five-year-old father said inside Apartment A6 at three-fifteen on Wednesday afternoon, September 17, 2003, lifting our desktop computer tower off the workstation desk and setting it high on the center of our full-sized bed mattress beside our seventeen-inch CRT monitor. "On the radio coming through the tunnel, Coastal Virginia Transit Authority just announced they're suspending all bus service across Norfolk and Virginia Beach tonight ahead of Hurricane Isabel, and the Weather Channel says the storm surge up the Elizabeth River could hit eight feet above normal low tide tomorrow afternoon."

Sitting in her compact black SwiftChair power wheelchair beside the open door of our under-the-counter dorm refrigerator, eighteen-year-old Katie handed me the last carton of milk and the cheese drawer to pack into Dad's blue insulated cooler.

"We unplugged the microwave and the toaster on the rolling cart too, Dad," Katie said, wiping the inside of the dorm fridge dry with a dish towel before propping its door open an inch so it wouldn't mildew if the power went out. "And Nate already rolled up our navy-blue sleeping bag from the square closet floor so we can bring it to Arvin Road."

"Good thinking, sweetheart," Dad smiled, zipping the sleeping bag into a heavy plastic protective bag and resting his hand comfortably on the rear push-handle of Katie's wheelchair to help ease her chair back two feet around the cooler in the narrow kitchenette aisle. "Besides getting you two away from the Elizabeth River flood zone, our house on Arvin Road is already registered on Commonwealth Power's Medical Priority list for your electric wheelchairs, so if the storm knocks the power lines down, we'll be on the priority list to get our electricity turned back on."

Neither Katie nor I ever liked being touched by anyone we didn't know. With strangers, classmates, or neighbors, even an unexpected pat on the shoulder or someone reaching for our wheelchair push-handles made our skin crawl and set off every alarm bell in our autistic nervous systems. Between the two of us, of course, it was the exact opposite—Katie and I held each other's hands across our armrests whenever we rolled side by side and hugged each other tight inside our shared sleeping bag every night—and both of us were completely comfortable when our dad helped maneuver our wheelchairs over tight thresholds or into the floor tie-downs of his van.

When Dad pulled our solid wooden hallway door open so Katie and I could roll our two SwiftChairs out into the back of the first-floor corridor with our backpacks strapped behind our seats, the heavy brass deadbolt of Apartment A5—directly across the narrow hall from our door—clicked shut at the exact same moment.

Standing in front of Apartment A5 with a canvas backpack slung over his shoulders and his brass key ring in his hand was eighteen-year-old Michael Ragsdale.

Both Katie and I noticed the change in him immediately.

Back in mid-July and August, whenever we had seen Michael in the hallway or out by the grassy field across Greenway Court, Ashley Raybourn had been right beside him—her shoulder pressed against his, steadying his uneven Cerebral Palsy gait and grounding him the exact same way Katie and I grounded each other. At the end of August, however, Ashley had packed her bags and left Norfolk to start her freshman year away at college. Michael's mom had already picked up his one-eyed black Lab rescue Archie earlier that morning to keep the dog safe during the hurricane, leaving Michael all by himself in the empty mirror-image studio across the hall.

Even behind his glasses, you could see how sad and hollowed-out Michael looked without Ashley there.

Katie reached her right hand across the gap between our wheelchairs and laced her fingers tightly through my left hand. Both my sister and I felt a quiet, genuine ache in our chests for our old Delaney Street and summer-camp neighbor—yet both of us also knew there was nothing we could do to help him. Neither Katie nor I could go over and give someone a hug without our own sensory wiring locking up, and even if we could have, we knew that nobody in the hallway could replace the safe person he was missing.

"Hey, Nathaniel. Hey, Katie, Mr. Prairie," [Michael said quietly, shifting the strap of his backpack](https://books.raggiesoft.com/rachel/michaels-senior-year/hurricane-isabel-the-three-bus-evacuation/three-buses-to-kemps-river-drive-crown-burger-sharing-cousin-marias-twin-bed). "Are you guys heading out to your dad's house in Virginia Beach before the wind picks up?"

"Just locked up A6 and lifted the computer onto the bed," I told him warmly from my chair while Katie gave him a gentle, sympathetic wave with her free hand from her armrest. "Dad's taking us over to Arvin Road for the storm. Are you getting out of West Ghent before CVTA shuts the buses down tonight?"

"Yeah," Michael nodded, glancing once down the hallway toward the front door. "I'm walking up to Hampton Boulevard right now to catch the Route 2 bus downtown, transferring to the Route 6, and then taking the Route 12 out to Kemps River Drive. My cousin Maria is meeting me there—she's going to take me to Crown Burger to grab food before everything closes, and then I'm staying at her house in the downstairs bedroom until the storm passes."

Just then, heavy footsteps clomped down the interior stairs from the second floor, and the twenty-something guy who lived in Apartment B5—directly upstairs from Michael's apartment—squeezed past us carrying two plastic bags of ice and a cardboard case of beer back up toward the second-floor landing.

"You first-floor folks are evacuating already?" the neighbor from B5 called down cheerfully over the stair railing. "A bunch of us up on the second floor are throwing a hurricane party tonight! We've got candles, flashlights, and three coolers on the landing!"

Dad shook his head with a polite, dry smile as the B5 door slammed upstairs. "A hurricane party two blocks from the Elizabeth River with an eight-foot storm surge in the forecast. Come on—let's get down the ramp while the streets are still dry."

When the four of us reached the end of the first-floor hallway at the top of the lobby ramp, we saw why our landlords, Dave and Deb, had parked their pickup truck right up against the front curb walk.

Working side by side in canvas work gloves inside the front entrance door, Dave and Deb were stacking two full layers of heavy burlap sandbags right across the top of the lobby ramp and the three shallow rubber-coated steps—forming a solid, double-tiered barrier separating the lower entrance lobby and the wall of metal mailboxes from the elevated first-floor apartment corridor.

"Wait right there a second, David—let me leave the center gap at the top of the ramp open until Katie and Nathaniel roll down!" Dave called out, wiping the sweat from his forehead as he and Deb steadied the burlap sacks and Dad placed his hands smoothly on the rear push-handles of my chair and then Katie's to help guide our front caster wheels past the two layers of sandbags, down the modular lobby ramp past the mailboxes, and out through the front glass door. "The city says the river tide tomorrow afternoon is going to push higher than the 1933 hurricane. Even if floodwater pushes through the front glass door into the lower lobby and reaches the mailboxes, two layers of sandbags right here at the top of the lobby ramp will keep it out of the first-floor hallway and out of A5 and A6!"

"Thank you for sandbagging the top of the ramp, Dave, Deb," Katie said earnestly from her wheelchair as we rolled out onto the dry concrete front walkway toward Dad's silver wheelchair-accessible minivan.

Out on the sidewalk in front of the circular red-brick planter, Katie and I lifted our hands to wave goodbye to Michael as he turned up Greenway Court toward Hampton Boulevard beneath the overcast gray clouds and rising wind to catch his first bus.

"Stay safe at your cousin's house, Michael!" Katie called out.

"You two stay safe at your dad's place too!" Michael called back, giving us a small wave before walking up the block.

Once Dad deployed the side fold-out aluminum ramp of his minivan, helped guide both of our SwiftChairs into the middle bay, and clicked all eight floor tie-down straps onto our frames, Katie reached across the narrow aisle between our chairs and held my hand the whole drive across the Elizabeth River to Dad's house on Arvin Road.

## Thursday, September 18 – Friday, September 19, 2003 — Losing Power for Two Days, Dead Wheelchair Batteries, Feeling Undignified Crawling on the Floor, & Dad Crawling With Us at Eye Level

By two o'clock on Thursday afternoon, September 18, 2003, the overnight rainbands had given way to the full eyewall gusts of Hurricane Isabel—battering all of Hampton Roads with howling seventy-mile-per-hour winds and horizontal sheets of torrential rain.

At ten minutes past two, a pole transformer two streets over toward Military Highway exploded with a low green flash against the dark afternoon clouds, and the electricity inside Dad's single-story brick ranch on Arvin Road went dead.

Two years earlier, right after our legs gave out in September 2001 and Dr. Carter prescribed our full-time electric wheelchairs, Dad had filed a physician-signed Serious Medical Condition certification with Commonwealth Power so that our house on Arvin Road carried an official Medical Priority flag on our residential meter. During ordinary summer thunderstorms or winter ice squalls, that priority flag meant our street always came back online within three or four hours.

Hurricane Isabel, however, was no ordinary storm.

As we listened to the emergency broadcasts on Dad's battery-powered weather radio Thursday evening while eating grilled cheeseburgers and hot dogs that Dad cooked on the charcoal grill under the sheltered back patio overhang before the food in the dark freezer could thaw, the announcers reported that nearly two million customers across Virginia had lost electricity at once. Hundreds of century-old oaks and tall Virginia pines had snapped across the primary high-voltage transmission lines and substations along Military Highway and Indian River Road. Even with a Medical Priority flag on our meter on Arvin Road, line crews couldn't energize our neighborhood tap line until tree crews sawed through the fallen trunks and rebuilt the main Kempsville feeder circuit first.

That Thursday night, when the peak of the hurricane roared across Virginia Beach—rattling the windowpanes and sending pine limbs thudding across the roof—Katie and I rolled down the hallway to our old bedroom, spread our shared navy-blue sleeping bag across the floor of the bedroom closet just like we did in our square closet at Apartment A6, transferred down into the sleeping bag together, and held each other tight with the closet door nearly shut until the howling wind finally began to subside toward dawn.

Throughout Thursday night and Friday morning, Katie and I tried everything we knew to stretch the charge on our twenty-four-volt SwiftChair batteries—switching our joystick controllers completely off whenever we stopped moving in the living room or kitchen. Yet twenty-four-volt gel-cell batteries weren't built to run for two days without a wall outlet.

At two-thirty on Friday afternoon, as I was turning my wheelchair around in the hallway outside the bathroom, the battery gauge on my right armrest dropped to a single flashing red LED and let out three sharp warning beeps. Half a second later, the electromagnetic motor brakes clicked shut, and my chair died completely on the hallway carpet.

Twenty minutes later, right beside the living-room sofa, Katie's joystick controller let out the exact same three beeps and went dark.

Dad disengaged the manual freewheel levers on the rear motors so our two dead powerchairs could be pushed out of the center of the hallway, but an unpowered electric wheelchair weighs nearly two hundred pounds and has small, thick motor-drive tires with no manual handrims. Neither Katie nor I could push our own dead chairs an inch, and even Dad could barely wrestle one heavy dead frame across the carpet at a time.

To get to the bathroom, the kitchen, or the living room, Katie and I had no choice: we had to slide down out of our seats onto the floor and crawl everywhere on our hands and knees.

I had never felt anything so undignified in my eighteen years of life.

Just three months ago, Katie and I had rolled across the stage in our caps and gowns to receive our Advanced Studies Diplomas from Northwood High School. We were eighteen-year-old college freshmen at the Community College of Hampton Roads with our own apartment lease in Norfolk—and now, simply because the power company hadn't gotten the street lines back up yet, the two of us were reduced to dragging ourselves down the hallway on our hands and knees like toddlers, our knees rubbing against the carpet and our palms pressing against the cool bathroom linoleum.

When Katie crawled back out of the hallway bathroom on Friday afternoon and sat back against the hallway wall beside me, her cheeks were burning bright red and her blue eyes were shining with hot, silent tears of sheer humiliation.

"I hate this, Nate," Katie whispered, pulling her knees up and hiding her face against my shoulder as I wrapped my arm around her on the hallway carpet. "We're eighteen years old. It feels so undignified having to crawl around on the floor like babies just to use the bathroom."

Down at the end of the hall, Dad stood in the kitchen doorway holding a battery lantern. He looked at the two dead black powerchairs parked uselessly against the wall, and then he looked down at his eighteen-year-old son and daughter sitting huddled together on the floor.

Dad didn't say a word about looking on the bright side, and he didn't stay standing up on his two feet looking down at us.

Instead, Dad set the lantern down on the carpet, walked over to the sofa, and pulled all three thick sofa cushions, a pile of pillows, and two folded blankets down onto the living-room rug. He carried the blue insulated cooler of ice and ginger ale off the kitchen counter and set it right on the floor beside the cushions. Then he walked to the hall closet, pulled out a stack of our favorite board games and card games—*Solo*, *WordSmith*, *Real Estate Baron*, *Stage 10*, and *Suspect*—along with two of his yellow legal pads and a handful of mechanical pencils, and set them in the center of the floor cushions.

And then our forty-five-year-old father dropped right down onto his own hands and knees on the hallway carpet and crawled over to Katie and me so that he was completely at eye level with the two of us.

Katie lifted her head from my shoulder, blinking in surprise through her wet eyelashes as Dad crawled right alongside us into the living room on his hands and knees.

"If my two favorite people in the world are traveling on hands and knees until Commonwealth Power fixes the feeder line," Dad said gently, looking straight into Katie's eyes and mine from the exact same height on the carpet, "then I'm traveling on hands and knees right here with you. Nobody in this house is looking down on anybody."

A shaky laugh broke through Katie's tears as she reached out to squeeze my hand, and the crushing knot of embarrassment in my chest loosened all at once.

For the rest of Friday afternoon, Friday night, and Saturday morning, Dad stayed down on the floor at eye level with us—crawling to the kitchen on his hands and knees whenever we crawled, eating peanut-butter sandwiches and potato chips together on the sofa cushions, sketching HTML table layouts on his yellow legal pads, and playing marathon lantern-lit rounds of *Solo*, *WordSmith*, and *Real Estate Baron* where Katie mercilessly bankrupted both Dad and me before bedtime.

And on Friday night before Katie and I crawled into the bedroom closet to sleep in our shared sleeping bag, Dad jotted a note in capital letters at the top of his yellow legal pad by the glow of the lantern:

"First thing next week," Dad promised us from the carpet, "we are getting Dr. Carter to write an order for two lightweight, collapsible manual wheelchairs with push-rims as backups—and we'll look into those clip-on joystick power attachments they make for manual frames too. That way, if a battery ever dies or a storm ever knocks the power out again, you two will always have backup wheels you can push yourselves without ever having to crawl on a floor."

## Saturday, September 20 – Thursday, October 2, 2003 — Assessing Greenway Court (The Sandbags Saved the Building!), Grocery Shopping With Dad, & Setting Up a Medical Priority Flag for Apartment A6

By early Saturday afternoon, September 20, city chainsaw crews had cleared enough fallen oak trees off Military Highway, Indian River Road, and the Midtown approaches to make the main roads safe for traffic again—and at one-thirty, Dad's cell phone chimed with a message from Dave and Deb letting tenants know the floodwaters around Greenway Court had receded enough to come check our apartments.

Helping Katie and me out to the minivan so we could sit comfortably in the bucket seats while our dead SwiftChairs stayed at Arvin Road waiting for electricity, Dad drove us across the Elizabeth River into West Ghent to assess the damages on Greenway Court.

As we turned off Hampton Boulevard, the streets around our neighborhood were littered with snapped tree limbs, tangled utility wires, and drying river mud. When Dad parked in the front lot and walked up the concrete path to unlock the front glass entrance door of our Greenway Court building while Katie and I looked through the lower lobby glass from the doorway, we saw right away how close our building had come to disaster—and what had saved it.

During Thursday afternoon's peak storm surge, a huge volume of Elizabeth River water had pushed through the front glass entrance door into the lower lobby, leaving a dried brown silt waterline across the brick walls and getting right into the lower lobby mailboxes.

Yet right at the top of the lobby ramp and the three shallow rubber-coated steps above the mailboxes, the two layers of heavy burlap sandbags that Dave and Deb had stacked inside the doorway had held like a dam—stopping the floodwater cold and preventing a single drop of water from entering the first-floor hallway or any of the apartments. When Dad unlocked the wooden door of Apartment A6 and checked inside from floor to ceiling, our carpet, our furniture, our square closet, and our computer on the bed were one hundred percent dry.

Up on the second-floor iron walkway, our upstairs neighbor from Apartment B5—who had stayed behind with a few other tenants to throw that hurricane party on Thursday—leaned over the railing and called down to us.

"You guys are lucky you went to your dad's house!" the neighbor from B5 told us, shaking his head. "At high tide Thursday afternoon, the river came all the way up over Greenway Court and the parking lot! If you had stayed in the building, you would've been trapped on an island—and if Dave and Deb hadn't put those two layers of sandbags at the top of the lobby ramp, the whole first floor would've flooded!"

Because the electricity was out on Greenway Court just like the rest of the block, Dad locked Apartment A6 back up and drove Katie and me back to Dad's house on Arvin Road to wait for the power to come back on.

And at six-fifteen that very Saturday evening—barely two hours after we got back to Kempsville—the amber strobe lights of a Commonwealth Power bucket truck flashed through the front bay window on Arvin Road.

Now that the main Kempsville feeder line had been repaired, the utility dispatcher's computer had flagged our Medical Priority account on Arvin Road and sent a two-man line crew straight down our street to clear a pine branch and snap a new fuse into our pole transformer. Five minutes later, the ceiling lights, the refrigerator, and the central air conditioning inside the house on Arvin Road hummed back to life—and Dad immediately plugged both of our twenty-four-volt SwiftChair battery chargers into the living-room wall outlet.

Rolling across the living room in our fully charged power wheelchairs on Sunday morning felt like getting our freedom back.

We spent the next few days at Dad's house on Arvin Road—Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, and Wednesday—enjoying normal everyday life with Dad while waiting to give West Ghent's utility crews plenty of time to restore power in Norfolk. We ate Dad's hot home-cooked breakfasts, worked on our CCHR computer science assignments now that our classes were preparing to resume, and on Wednesday afternoon, September 24, rode with Dad to the neighborhood supermarket on Indian River Road to go grocery shopping—picking out fresh milk, orange juice, eggs, bread, sliced honey ham, and cheddar cheese so we could restock our dorm fridge when we moved back into Apartment A6.

From the grocery store parking lot, Dad drove us straight across the river to Greenway Court with our insulated bags of cold groceries in the back of the minivan.

When we turned down Redgate Avenue toward Greenway Court, our hopes soared: right across the way, the multi-story complex of Tidewater General Hospital—the region's only Level 1 Trauma Center—was lit up from top to bottom with full electrical power.

"Look, Nate—Tidewater General has all its lights on!" Katie smiled, squeezing my hand across our wheelchair armrests as Dad helped guide our two SwiftChairs up the front walkway and over the lobby ramp into our Greenway Court building. "Our power has got to be back on by now!"

Yet the moment we unlocked Apartment A6 and I reached up from my wheelchair to flip the wall switch, nothing happened.

Six full days after Hurricane Isabel had passed, the power failure on Greenway Court was still in progress.

Out in the front lobby, where Dave was wiping down the metal mailboxes, we found out why.

"We thought for sure the lights would be back on by today, Dave, especially with Tidewater General lit up right across the way," Dad said, resting his hands on our rear push-handles.

"Tidewater General is the Level 1 Trauma Center, so it's on a dedicated hospital priority feed," Dave sighed, shaking his head in frustration. "Meanwhile, we're still sitting here in the dark. I can't confirm it officially with the engineers at Commonwealth Power, David, but the line crews out on Hampton Boulevard told us our building's power grid is physically attached to the Midtown Tunnel circuit. When forty-four million gallons of Elizabeth River water flooded the Midtown Tunnel on Thursday, it shorted out the whole feeder—and they told us it's going to take crews two full weeks to figure out how to cut the grid so that we're separated from the Midtown Tunnel before our power comes back on."

Standing in the dim first-floor hallway behind our wheelchairs, Dad looked from the dark doorway of Apartment A6 to Katie and me—realizing at once why Dad's house on Arvin Road had gotten its electricity back in forty-eight hours while our Greenway Court building was still sitting unprioritized at the bottom of a tangled tunnel circuit.

"When we switched the Apartment A6 electric bill into your names in July," Dad said, pulling his cell phone and his pocket notebook from his jacket, "the Medical Priority flag stayed attached only to my meter on Arvin Road. Commonwealth Power doesn't have a Medical Priority flag on file for Apartment A6—so their outage system doesn't even know there are two full-time electric wheelchair users living in this building."

Right there that afternoon—before we loaded our bags of groceries back into the minivan and returned to Dad's house on Arvin Road for another week—Dad called Dr. Carter's office and Commonwealth Power's Critical Care desk, helping Katie and me set up an official Medical Priority flag on our electric account for Apartment A6 on Greenway Court so that our Norfolk apartment would never be left behind in an extended power outage again.

And true to what the line crews had told Dave, our Greenway Court building stayed completely without electricity for two full weeks—until Commonwealth Power finally figured out how to cut and isolate our neighborhood grid from the flooded Midtown Tunnel on Thursday, October 2, 2003, allowing Katie and me to finally roll back into our dry, brightly lit studio sanctuary across the hall from Michael.

## Wednesday, November 12, 2003, 5:30 PM — Dad's Microwave Cart, Shared Code, & A Happy Year in Our Studio Sanctuary

By November and winter of our eighteenth year—with the hurricane debris cleared from West Ghent and our studio humming with warm electricity again—life inside Apartment A6 on Greenway Court settled into the happiest, most peaceful routine Katie and I had ever known.

Every morning, after waking up well-rested inside our shared sleeping bag on the carpeted floor of our square closet bedroom—while the full-sized bed out in the main studio stayed smooth and untouched—we used the padded transfer bench in our accessible shower, brushed our teeth side by side at the roll-under bathroom sink, and rolled out to our compact kitchenette. While I popped four slices of whole-wheat bread into the toaster on Dad's rolling utility cart and pulled the milk carton and butter out of the under-the-counter dorm fridge, Katie scrambled eggs on the four-burner stove, and we washed our plates and skillet in the sink the minute we finished eating.

Whenever we headed out our door and down Dave's nonslip aluminum ramp through the locked front lobby, we waved to Michael Ragsdale and his one-eyed black Lab Archie across the hall in Apartment A5 before rolling down Greenway Court and Redgate Avenue to Hampton Boulevard to catch the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Route 2 bus into Downtown Norfolk. Side by side in the front wheelchair bays of the bus, we rode ten minutes downtown to the Martin Building at the Community College of Hampton Roads Norfolk campus for our morning lectures, grabbed hot burgers and fries upstairs at the third-floor food court of The Admiral Center on Fridays, and rode the afternoon Route 2 bus right back home to West Ghent.

In the evenings, while the radiators and the through-wall fan kept our rectangular studio cozy and warm, Katie and I parked our SwiftChairs side by side at our long workstation desk in front of our shared desktop computer. Taking turns at the keyboard while the other sister or brother read aloud from our textbooks or debugged lines of HTML and Quantum Basic code on the seventeen-inch CRT monitor, we had everything we needed under one roof: four locked walls, a full kitchen cart, our own bus passes, and each other.
