## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:35 AM — Purple Gowns Under the Pavilion Colonnade & Two Squeezes Missing

*Click-whir.*

Dad lowered his thirty-five-millimeter film camera from his eye, blinked rapidly twice behind a proud, misty grin, and let out a long breath into the warm Atlantic breeze sweeping down Nineteenth Street across the sunlit concrete plaza of the Virginia Beach Pavilion.

"Hold right there for one more just in case somebody blinked," Uncle Steven called cheerfully beside him, steadying his disposable camera while Aunt Karen smoothed a stray lock of Katie's pale-blonde hair back under the edge of her royal-purple mortarboard cap and dabbed openly at the corner of her eye with a folded paper tissue. "Look at those gold cords, Karen. Both of them."

"We couldn't be prouder of you two if we tried," Aunt Karen said thickly, bending down to wrap Katie and then me in tight, lavender-scented hugs despite the crinkling purple satin sleeves bunched over our shoulders. Nobody needed to say out loud why Aunt Karen's voice caught a little harder around a sea of four hundred purple caps and gowns—having watched our cousin Brittany stop going to class and drop out earlier that spring still sat like a bruise under Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven's ribs—yet neither of them would have missed watching Katie and me roll side by side across that graduation stage for anything in the world.

Twenty minutes earlier inside the cool, cavernous exhibit hall beneath the Pavilion's white steel space-frame roof trusses, Katie and I had eased our compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs up the temporary aluminum stage ramp one right behind the other. When Principal Halloway read *Katie Marie Prairie* followed immediately by *Nathaniel David Prairie*, we had crossed the wooden platform almost hub to hub, accepted our dark-purple leatherette *Advanced Studies Diploma* folders, and reached up in twin synchrony to move our purple-and-gold Class of 2003 tassels from the right side of our mortarboards over to the left while Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven whistled from the accessible family row loud enough to echo off the rafters.

Around us now on the wide outdoor walkway, plastic air horns bleated, camera flashes popped under the blooming pink crape-myrtle trees, and knots of laughing eighteen-year-olds tossed mortarboards toward the cloudless June sky.

"All right, let's beat the parking-lot gridlock before Nineteenth Street turns into a bumper-to-bumper mess," Dad said, slinging his camera strap securely over his shoulder and glancing down at the two of us parked in the dappled shade beside a brick crape-myrtle planter along the Pavilion colonnade. "Steve and Karen and I are going to walk fast over to the west lot and pull the van around to the accessible curb cut right down there by the flagpole. There's cold ginger ale and apple juice on ice in the back—you two good waiting right here in the shade for five minutes?"

"More than good, Dad," Katie smiled, patting the leatherette diploma folder resting across her lap. "Take your time."

The moment Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven disappeared into the stream of families heading toward the west lot, Katie reached over, flipped up the padded inner armrests between our two wheelchairs, and leaned sideways across the narrow gap to wrap both of her purple-robed arms firmly around my shoulders. I hugged my twin sister right back just as tight, resting my cheek against the cool satin of her collar for ten steady seconds until the lingering acoustic buzz of four thousand clapping spectators drained out of both of our chests.

"We finished Northwood, Nate," Katie murmured happily against my shoulder as she sat back, flipped our inner armrests down, and laced her right fingers securely through my left hand. "Next stop, CCHR with Dad."

"No more homeroom bells," I agreed, feeling my shoulders drop an inch—until a familiar, rhythmic three-note hum cut cleanly through the chatter ten feet to my right.

Before I even finished turning my head, a tall, sandy-haired sixteen-year-old boy in a neat navy-blue polo shirt and pressed khakis stepped eagerly out from behind a cluster of parents, pulling his left hand straight out of someone else's fingers as his wide brown eyes locked onto the glowing green battery bars on Katie's and my right-hand joystick controllers.

It was Wesley.

He had shot up at least three inches since our Saturday afternoons at Emerald Creek Mall and our house on Arvin Road, though the smooth blue silicone ring turning against his right palm was the exact same one he had carried since he was fourteen. Beaming from ear to ear at the sight of us, Wesley walked straight up to the right side of my SwiftChair, rocked happily once from heel to toe on his clean gray sneakers, and reached down to tap the back of my knuckles twice in gentle greeting.

My breath caught squarely in the middle of my throat—because two paces behind Wesley, stepping hesitantly into the shade of the crape-myrtle tree in a royal-purple cap and gown identical to ours, stood Heather Collins.

All the color had drained from Heather's cheeks under the bright June sun. She clutched her own diploma folder tightly against her sternum with one hand, her hazel eyes wide and uncertain as she reached her free hand slightly forward toward her cousin's elbow so he wouldn't wander into passing foot traffic.

"Wes, wait a second—" Heather began softly.

Before Heather's fingertips could brush his sleeve, Wesley turned squarely toward her and pulled his arm cleanly out of reach.

The sunlit grin vanished completely from Wesley's face, replaced in an instant by a deep, unmistakable frown that creased his forehead right between his eyebrows. Holding his blue silicone ring tight against his palm, Wesley shook his head sharply at Heather—once, twice—let out a low, indignant vocal protest deep in his throat, pointed once with his whole hand from my wheelchair straight at Heather's chest, and planted his sneakers stubbornly six inches beside my right drive wheel with both arms folded hard across his polo shirt, glaring sideways at his cousin.

I blinked in surprise, glancing from Wesley's fierce scowl up to Heather's stricken face.

Heather swallowed hard, her throat working visibly above the white V-neck collar of her graduation dress under her unzipped gown as tears sprang straight into her lower lashes.

"He's been furious with me since February," Heather admitted in a small, unsteady voice, not even trying to hide the truth as Wesley kept glaring at her from his post beside my wheel. "Every Saturday when my mom and Aunt Linda drive us down Military Highway past Indian River Road toward the mall, Wes points out the window at the Route 15 bus shelter and taps the glass twice for *let's go together*. When I had to tell him that you and Katie weren't meeting us at the glass elevator anymore—and that it was my fault because I yelled at you—he took his framed mall photo off the living-room shelf, carried it into his bedroom so I couldn't touch it, and hasn't held my hand walking into a building without frowning at me since."

Despite the tight knot squeezing my ribs, I looked back down at sixteen-year-old Wesley standing guard beside my armrest. Whatever had broken between Heather and me in Northwood's upstairs hallway, Wesley hadn't broken a single thing.

"Hi, Wesley," I said softly, keeping my voice calm and warm just for him. "Look how tall you got this spring. Katie and I missed seeing you too."

"Hi, Wesley," Katie added gently across my shoulder, offering him a genuine, kind smile that never wavered even when her eyes shifted carefully back up to Heather.

## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:50 AM — An Honest Apology & The Door Neither Twin Could Reopen

![Outside the Virginia Beach Pavilion under blooming crape-myrtle trees after Northwood High School's June 2003 Commencement, eighteen-year-old blonde fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in matching compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs in royal-purple caps and gowns with gold honor cords holding inner hands with guarded, bittersweet expressions as remorseful classmate Heather Collins apologizes to both of them while her sixteen-year-old cousin Wesley folds his arms over his blue silicone ring and frowns angrily at Heather.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/jun_2003_pavilion_graduation_heather_apology_angry_wesley.jpg)

Standing three feet back from our footrests so she wasn't crowding either wheelchair against the brick planter, Heather drew a long, trembling breath and looked directly at both of us—meeting Katie's blue eyes squarely before looking down into mine.

"My parents and Aunt Linda are waiting over by the east crosswalk," Heather said quietly, folding both hands tight around her purple leatherette diploma folder to keep her fingers from shaking. "If you tell me to walk away right now, I won't say another word. I just... I wrote you a note back in February, Nate, and I stood in the upstairs hallway twice trying to hand it to you, and today I realized a piece of notebook paper was the coward's way out. I couldn't walk out of high school for the rest of our lives without looking both of you in the face—you, Nate, and you too, Katie—and saying how deeply sorry I am."

Beside me across our armrests, Katie's fingers tightened steadily around mine, though she didn't interrupt.

"On January sixth, both of you stood right in front of my locker and told me the complete truth," Heather said, her voice cracking slightly over the distant hum of Nineteenth Street traffic while a single tear slipped past her lower lashes onto her cheek. "And I didn't even let you finish two sentences. Then on February eleventh in the cafeteria, I sat six feet away from you and heard Tyler use the exact same disgusting lie word for word on Megan Porter about her boyfriend's cousin—and I watched Megan laugh right in his face because she actually trusted the person she loved. That should have been me, Nate. You never once lied to me in eighteen months. Katie never once lied to me. You protected your sister on New Year's Eve, you kept your promise not to drink, and because my pride got stung in front of twenty kids in homeroom, I threw all of it in your faces and acted like a stranger."

Heather wiped her cheek quickly with the heel of her hand, glancing down for half a second at Wesley—who hadn't unfolded his arms an inch and was still watching her with stern, unblinking brown eyes—before looking earnestly back at Katie and me.

"I know I don't have any right to expect everything to go back to the way it was before Christmas," Heather whispered, hope and dread warring plainly across her face. "I'm just asking... if there is any chance at all, over the summer, that you might let me start at zero and slowly try to earn both of your trust back. Even just thirty minutes at a food-court table with Wes in broad daylight whenever you say so. I swear on everything I care about, I would never doubt either of you again."

For four full seconds, the only sound in our little patch of crape-myrtle shade was the soft rustle of pink blossoms overhead and Wesley's quiet, steady breathing beside my right wheel.

Part of me—the same aching corner of my chest that still knew every note of *Unhandled Exception* by heart and still remembered how safe her arms had felt around my shoulders outside Stairway A four days before Christmas—felt a sharp, dizzy pull behind my sternum. Watching Heather stand there in tears without a single excuse, admitting to both of us out loud that she knew we had told the truth, lifted a cold five-month stone off my lungs that I hadn't realized I was still carrying.

And at the exact same second, right underneath that relief, a cold, instinctive knot of pure terror clamped shut around my stomach—the exact same protective fear I could feel stiffening right through Katie's palm into mine.

"Heather, thank you for coming over here and saying that to both of us," Katie spoke first—not sharply, not triumphantly, her voice quiet and honest and steady beside my shoulder. "Hearing you say out loud that you know Nate told the truth really does mean something. To both of us. It's just... I watched what that Monday morning did to my brother for five straight months. Neither Nate nor I can put ourselves back in a place where every single day we're terrified that the next time somebody spreads a rumor or misunderstands something, you might blow up at us like that all over again."

Heather's breath hitched softly, though she didn't step forward or try to argue over my sister. She looked down at me, waiting to hear it from my own mouth.

I swallowed past the lump in my throat and met her hazel eyes directly.

"Katie's right," I said quietly, keeping my left hand anchored tight in my twin sister's fingers while speaking as gently and plainly as I knew how. "I don't hate you, Heather. Even in January, I never hated you, and I really am grateful you told both of us today that you know we didn't lie. When you slammed your locker at eight in the morning and yelled at us like eighteen months together hadn't meant anything... it scared me in a way I don't know how to undo. If we tried to start over, every time a telephone rang or somebody whispered behind us at a mall table, both Katie and I would spend the whole afternoon bracing for you to snap and throw us away again. We don't trust that it wouldn't happen a second time, and neither of us can live scared like that."

For a long, quiet moment, Heather simply stood there in her purple gown, her lower lip trembling as a second tear spilled down her cheek.

To her credit, she didn't beg, didn't get defensive, and didn't try to tell two autistic eighteen-year-olds that our fear wasn't reasonable. Slowly, humbly, she bowed her head half an inch and nodded.

"Yeah," Heather whispered thickly, wiping both cheeks with the sleeve of her gown before holding her diploma folder back against her chest. "Yeah... I earned that. Both of you deserve to feel safe, Nate. I'm really proud of both of you for graduating with honors, and I hope CCHR goes great for you and your dad."

Turning gently toward my right armrest before Dad's silver accessible van eased up beside the flagpole curb cut forty feet down the sidewalk, I reached out and tapped the back of Wesley's folded forearm twice with two warm, light fingertips.

"It's okay to walk back to the car with Heather now, Wesley," I told him softly, smiling right up into his serious brown eyes. "You never did anything wrong, and Katie and I were really glad we got to ride the glass elevator and eat pretzels with you."

Wesley looked down at me for three quiet seconds, uncrossed his arms just far enough to tap his smooth blue silicone ring twice against the top of my purple sleeve—*two taps, goodbye friend*—and then shot Heather one last pointed, unimpressed frown as he stepped back beside her, pointedly keeping both hands tucked over his ring at his own waist instead of taking hers while allowing her to walk half a step beside his left shoulder toward the east crosswalk.

"Goodbye, Nate. Goodbye, Katie," Heather murmured softly without looking back again as she guided her still-scowling cousin carefully into the flow of families crossing Nineteenth Street.

"Bye, Heather. Bye, Wesley," Katie and I answered quietly together.

Down at the flagpole curb cut, Dad tapped the horn once lightly and stepped out onto the sunny asphalt to unfold the side ramp while Uncle Steven and Aunt Karen waved us forward. Before nudging my right thumb onto the joystick, I glanced sideways at Katie under the pink crape-myrtle shade; without needing a single word, my sister squeezed my hand twice—steady, permanent, and unafraid—and side by side in our purple gowns, the two of us rolled forward toward the van and the rest of our lives.
