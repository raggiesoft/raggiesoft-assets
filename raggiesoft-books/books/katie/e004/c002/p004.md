## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:35 AM — Purple Gowns Under the Pavilion Colonnade & Two Squeezes Missing

*Click-whir.*

Dad lowered his thirty-five-millimeter film camera from his eye, blinked rapidly twice behind a proud, misty grin, and let out a long breath into the warm Atlantic breeze sweeping down Nineteenth Street across the sunlit concrete plaza of the Virginia Beach Pavilion.

"Hold right there for one more just in case somebody blinked," Uncle Steven called cheerfully beside him, steadying his disposable camera while Aunt Karen smoothed a stray lock of Katie's pale-blonde hair back under the edge of her royal-purple mortarboard cap and dabbed openly at the corner of her eye with a folded paper tissue. "Look at those gold cords, Karen. Both of them."

"We couldn't be prouder of you two if we tried," Aunt Karen said thickly, bending down to wrap Katie and then me in tight, lavender-scented hugs despite the crinkling purple satin sleeves bunched over our shoulders. Nobody needed to say out loud why Aunt Karen's voice caught a little harder around a sea of four hundred purple caps and gowns—having watched our cousin Brittany stop going to class and drop out earlier that spring still sat like a bruise under Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven's ribs—yet neither of them would have missed watching Katie and me roll side by side across that graduation stage for anything in the world.

Twenty minutes earlier inside the cool, cavernous exhibit hall beneath the Pavilion's white steel space-frame roof trusses, Katie and I had eased our compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs up the temporary aluminum stage ramp one right behind the other. When Principal Halloway read *Katie Marie Prairie* followed immediately by *Nathaniel David Prairie*, we had crossed the wooden platform almost hub to hub, accepted our dark-purple leatherette *Advanced Studies Diploma* folders, and reached up in twin synchrony to move our purple-and-gold Class of 2003 tassels from the right side of our mortarboards over to the left while Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven whistled from the accessible family row loud enough to echo off the rafters.

Around us now on the wide outdoor walkway, plastic air horns bleated, camera flashes popped under the blooming pink crape-myrtle trees, and knots of laughing eighteen-year-olds tossed mortarboards toward the cloudless June sky.

"All right, let's beat the parking-lot gridlock before Nineteenth Street turns into a bumper-to-bumper mess," Dad said, slinging his camera strap securely over his shoulder and glancing down at the two of us parked in the dappled shade beside a brick crape-myrtle planter along the Pavilion colonnade. "Steve and Karen and I are going to walk fast over to the west lot and pull the van around to the accessible curb cut right down there by the flagpole. There's cold ginger ale and apple juice on ice in the back—you two good waiting right here in the shade for five minutes?"

"More than good, Dad," Katie smiled, patting the leatherette diploma folder resting across her lap. "Take your time."

The moment Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven disappeared into the stream of families heading toward the west lot, Katie reached over, flipped up the padded inner armrests between our two wheelchairs, and leaned sideways across the narrow gap to wrap both of her purple-robed arms firmly around my shoulders. I hugged my twin sister right back just as tight, resting my cheek against the cool satin of her collar for ten steady seconds until the lingering acoustic buzz of four thousand clapping spectators drained out of both of our chests.

"We finished Northwood, Nate," Katie murmured happily against my shoulder as she sat back, flipped our inner armrests down, and laced her right fingers securely through my left hand. "Next stop, CCHR with Dad."

"No more homeroom bells," I agreed, feeling my shoulders drop an inch—until an insistent, unmistakable two-syllable vocalization cut sharply through the crowd noise fifteen feet to my right.

It wasn't a calm three-note hum. It was a firm, commanding sound deep in a teenager's chest, followed immediately by a second sharp vocal burst.

Before I even finished turning my head, a tall, sandy-haired sixteen-year-old boy in a neat navy-blue polo shirt and pressed khakis marched straight out from behind a cluster of parents with his right fingers closed around a smooth blue silicone ring and his left hand gripping a fistful of someone else's royal-purple graduation sleeve—actively pulling a pale, trembling classmate out of the foot traffic and straight toward the shade of our crape-myrtle tree.

It was Wesley—and stumbling half a step behind his tugging hand in a purple cap and gown identical to ours was Heather Collins.

All the color had drained from Heather's cheeks under the bright June sun. She clutched her leatherette diploma folder tightly against her sternum with her free arm, her hazel eyes wide and shimmering with dread, though she wasn't pulling back against her cousin's grip.

"Okay, Wes, okay—I'm coming, I promised you I would," Heather whispered shakily under her breath.

Wesley had shot up at least three inches since our Saturday afternoons at Emerald Creek Mall and our house on Arvin Road. The second he reached the edge of our shade and let go of Heather's purple sleeve, a brief, genuine smile broke through for Katie and me alone: he rocked once on his dark sneakers, reached down beside my armrest, and tapped the back of my knuckles twice in warm greeting.

Then, before Heather could say another word, Wesley stepped right back beside Heather's left shoulder and pivoted squarely to face his cousin.

Every trace of that greeting smile vanished from Wesley's face, replaced in an instant by a deep, furious scowl that creased his forehead right between his eyebrows. Letting out a sharp, indignant vocal sound in his throat, Wesley pointed emphatically with his whole hand at Heather's sternum, swept his arm straight down toward Katie's wheelchair and mine, tapped the side of his clenched fist twice against his open palm, and folded both arms tight across his navy polo shirt over his blue silicone ring—standing beside Heather like an immovable sentinel and glaring straight at the side of her face until she spoke.

I blinked in astonishment, looking from Wesley's unmistakable non-verbal command up to Heather's stricken face.

Heather swallowed hard, her throat working visibly above the collar of her graduation dress as tears sprang straight into her lower lashes.

"He's been furious with me ever since January," Heather admitted in a small, unsteady voice, not even trying to soften the truth while Wesley kept glaring hard at her from six inches off her left elbow. "You were the first real guy buddy Wes ever had in his whole life, Nate. Before we met you and Katie, boys our age either stared at him or walked ten feet around him, and getting together as the four of us at the mall was his favorite part of every single week. The very first Saturday after New Year's when Aunt Linda and my mom drove past the Route 15 shelter on Military Highway and I had to tell him we weren't meeting you and Katie anymore because I screamed at you and broke up with you, Wes lost his temper at me like I've never seen in his life. He took our framed mall photo off the living-room shelf and hid it in his bedroom so I couldn't touch it, and every single week since January—every time he sees my Northwood jacket or a city bus—he grabs my sleeve, makes that exact sound, and points toward Kempsville demanding I go apologize to both of you. The second he spotted your wheelchairs coming out of the hall just now, he wasn't letting me near the car until I walked over here and said it properly."

Hearing that—that out of sixteen years of growing up surrounded by his mom and aunts and older female cousin, I had been the first guy friend Wesley ever really had—sent an ache straight through the middle of my chest. Whatever had shattered between Heather and me outside Room 204 in January, Wesley hadn't forgotten us for a single week.

"Hi, Wesley," I said softly, keeping my voice calm and warm just for him. "Look how tall you got this year. Katie and I missed seeing you too."

"Hi, Wesley," Katie added gently across my shoulder, offering him a genuine, kind smile before her eyes shifted carefully back up to Heather.

## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:50 AM — An Honest Apology, Dashed Hopes, & The Door Neither Twin Could Reopen

![Outside the Virginia Beach Pavilion under blooming crape-myrtle trees after Northwood High School's June 2003 Commencement, eighteen-year-old blonde fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in matching compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs in royal-purple caps and gowns with gold honor cords holding inner hands with guarded, bittersweet expressions as remorseful classmate Heather Collins apologizes to both of them while her sixteen-year-old cousin Wesley folds his arms over his blue silicone ring and frowns angrily at Heather.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/jun_2003_pavilion_graduation_heather_apology_angry_wesley.jpg)

Standing three feet back from our footrests beside her scowling cousin so she wasn't crowding either wheelchair against the brick planter, Heather drew a long, trembling breath and looked directly at both of us—meeting Katie's blue eyes squarely before looking down into mine.

"My parents and Aunt Linda are waiting over by the east crosswalk," Heather said quietly, folding both hands tight around her purple leatherette diploma folder to keep her fingers from shaking. "Wes is right to be mad at me, and he's right to make me stand here. I wrote you an apology note back in February, Nate, and twice I stood in the upstairs hallway trying to hand it to you, and I realized a folded piece of notebook paper was the coward's way out. I couldn't walk out of high school without looking both of you in the face—you, Nate, and you too, Katie—and issuing a real apology."

Beside me across our armrests, Katie's fingers tightened steadily around mine, though she didn't interrupt. Over by Heather's left shoulder, Wesley kept his arms folded over his blue ring, watching every move of Heather's mouth like a hawk to make sure she didn't back out.

"On January sixth, both of you stood right in front of my locker and told me the complete truth," Heather said, her voice cracking slightly over the distant hum of Nineteenth Street traffic while a single tear slipped past her lower lashes onto her cheek. "And I screamed over top of you and didn't even let you finish two sentences. Then on February eleventh in the cafeteria, I sat six feet away from you and heard Tyler use the exact same disgusting lie word for word on Megan Porter about her boyfriend's cousin—and I watched Megan laugh right in his face because she actually trusted the person she loved. That should have been me, Nate. You never once lied to me in eighteen months. Katie never once lied to me. You protected your sister on New Year's Eve, you kept your promise not to drink, and because my pride got stung in front of twenty kids in homeroom, I screamed at you, threw everything we had in your faces, and pushed both of you out of our lives."

Heather wiped her cheek quickly with the heel of her hand and drew a shaky breath, looking earnestly back at Katie and me.

"I know I don't have any right to expect things to go back to how they were before Christmas," Heather whispered. "I'm just asking... if there is any chance at all, over the summer, that you might let me start at zero and slowly try to earn both of your trust back. Even thirty minutes at a mall table with Wes in broad daylight whenever you say so. I swear on everything I care about, I would never doubt either of you again."

Right beside Heather's purple sleeve, Wesley's folded arms loosened by two inches.

Leaning half a step forward onto the balls of his sneakers, sixteen-year-old Wesley turned his smooth blue silicone ring eagerly between both palms and looked straight from Katie's face to mine with wide, shining brown eyes, letting out a soft, rising two-note vocal hum under his breath. You didn't need a single spoken word to read what Wesley was feeling: having finally marched Heather over here to apologize the way he had demanded since January, he was hoping with his whole heart that Katie and I would accept and let them back in.

Seeing that raw, unguarded hope on Wesley's face hurt ten times worse than anything Heather could have said.

Part of me—the same aching corner of my chest that still knew every note of *Unhandled Exception* by heart and still remembered how safe Heather's arms had felt around my shoulders outside Stairway A four days before Christmas—felt a sharp, dizzy pull behind my sternum. Hearing Heather confess out loud without a single excuse that she knew we had told the truth lifted a five-month stone off my lungs.

And at the exact same second, right underneath that relief, a cold, instinctive knot of pure terror clamped shut around my stomach—the exact same protective fear I could feel stiffening right through Katie's palm into mine.

"Heather, thank you for coming over here and saying all of that to our faces," Katie spoke first—not sharply, not triumphantly, her voice quiet and honest and steady beside my shoulder. "Hearing you say out loud that you know Nate told the truth really does mean something to both of us. It's just... I watched what your screaming at that locker did to my brother for five straight months. Neither Nate nor I can put ourselves back in a place where every single day we're terrified that the next time somebody spreads a rumor or misunderstands something, you might blow up at us like that all over again."

Heather's breath hitched softly, though she didn't step forward or try to argue over my sister. Beside her, the hopeful hum in Wesley's throat cut off cleanly as his brown eyes darted down to my face.

I swallowed hard past the lump in my throat, wishing for Wesley's sake there were any other honest answer inside me, and met Heather's hazel eyes directly.

"Katie's right," I said quietly, keeping my left hand anchored tight in my twin sister's fingers while speaking as gently and plainly as I knew how. "I don't hate you, Heather. Even in January, I never hated you, and I really am grateful you told both of us today that you know we didn't lie. When you slammed your locker at eight in the morning and screamed at me like eighteen months together hadn't meant anything... it scared me in a way I don't know how to undo. If we let you back into our lives, every time a telephone rang or somebody whispered behind us at a table, both Katie and I would spend the whole afternoon bracing for you to snap and throw us away again. We don't trust that it wouldn't happen a second time, and neither of us can live scared like that."

For two quiet heartbeats, nobody under the crape-myrtle blossoms moved.

Beside Heather, our gentle refusal didn't just disappoint sixteen-year-old Wesley—it broke his heart right in front of us.

All the eager light went out of his brown eyes at once. His shoulders caved inward inside his navy polo shirt, his lower lip trembled hard, and two fat, silent tears spilled straight over his lower lashes down his cheeks as a small, raw whimper of pure heartbreak caught in his throat. Clutching his blue silicone ring tight against his sternum with both hands, Wesley stared down at the gap between our wheelchairs like the floor had dropped out from under his sneakers—understanding all at once that the four of us weren't ever gathering around that corner mall table again, and that the first real guy buddy he had ever had was walking out of his life for good.

Beside me, Katie let out a tiny, stricken intake of breath. Her fingers convulsed around mine, and when I glanced half an inch sideways, real tears were shimmering along my twin sister's lower eyelids too. My own throat closed up so hard my vision blurred; watching Wesley's heart break when he hadn't done a single wrong thing tore both of us to pieces, and for one agonizing second I almost wished I could swallow my fear just to stop him from hurting.

Yet even through the sting in both of our eyes, Katie didn't take her words back, and neither did I—because Wesley couldn't ride the bus to Emerald Creek Mall without Heather right beside him, and as much as it broke our hearts to hurt him, we had to protect ourselves from spending every Saturday terrified that Heather might snap at us all over again.

Blinking hard through the tears wet on his cheeks, Wesley whipped his head sideways toward Heather.

A fresh wave of fierce, wounded fury blazed straight across Wesley's tear-streaked face, creasing his brow deeper than before. Letting out a sharp, choked sound of sheer anger deep in his chest, Wesley shook his head hard at Heather and jabbed one finger squarely toward our two wheelchairs and right back at Heather's shoulder—furious with her to his core for screaming at me in January and costing him his friend and their Saturday mall afternoons in the first place. Yet just as plainly, he didn't grab her purple sleeve again or demand another word; having stood six inches away watching tears spill down Heather's cheeks while she delivered every word of the formal apology he had insisted on, Wesley understood that she really had tried to apologize, even if trying couldn't put any of it back together.

To Heather's credit, she didn't flinch away from her cousin's anger, didn't beg us, and didn't try to use Wesley's tears to guilt two autistic eighteen-year-olds out of protecting themselves. Slowly, humbly, she bowed her head half an inch and nodded.

"Yeah," Heather whispered thickly, wiping both cheeks with the sleeve of her gown before holding her diploma folder back against her chest. "Yeah... I earned that—from both of you, and from Wes too. You deserve to feel safe, Nate. I'm really proud of both of you for graduating with honors, and I hope CCHR goes great for you and your dad."

Before Dad's silver accessible van eased up beside the flagpole curb cut forty feet down the sidewalk, I swallowed hard past the ache in my throat, leaned slightly forward in my seat, and held my open right hand halfway out toward Wesley at waist height so he could choose whether to touch it.

"You were a really great friend, Wesley," I told him, my voice shaking slightly despite how steady I tried to keep it for him. "You never did anything wrong—not once. Katie and I loved every Saturday the four of us spent riding the glass elevator and eating warm pretzels with you, and we're really sorry we have to say goodbye."

Wesley stared through wet eyelashes at my hand for three aching seconds, let out a shaky, hitched breath through his nose, and reached forward to tap his smooth blue silicone ring twice against the back of my knuckles—a slow, heartbroken *goodbye, buddy*. Stepping back, he wiped his cheek roughly with the back of his wrist, shot Heather one more blistering, red-rimmed glare for driving us away, and folded his arms tight across his chest again so she couldn't hold his hand—though because he had watched her stand here and honestly try, he inclined his chin once toward the east crosswalk and allowed her to walk half a step beside his shoulder back toward Aunt Linda and her parents.

"Goodbye, Nate. Goodbye, Katie," Heather murmured softly without looking back again as she walked carefully beside her heartbroken, still-furious cousin into the stream of families crossing Nineteenth Street.

"Bye, Heather. Bye, Wesley," Katie and I answered thickly together.

For ten quiet seconds after their purple gown and navy polo shirt disappeared into the crowd, neither Katie nor I touched our joysticks. Down at the flagpole curb cut, Dad had just pulled the silver van up to the yellow paint and switched on his hazard lights, still unbuckling his seatbelt before coming around to the side ramp.

Right there in the dappled crape-myrtle shade, Katie flipped up our inner armrests with trembling fingers, leaned straight across the gap between our chairs, and buried her damp face against the shoulder of my purple gown while I wrapped both arms tightly around my sister and blinked back two hot tears of my own.

"I hate that Wes got hurt, Nate," Katie whispered raggedly against my collar. "He looked so hopeful."

"Me too," I breathed back, holding onto her until my chest stopped shaking enough to draw a full breath. "We still couldn't go back, Katie. We can't live scared she'll blow up again."

"No," Katie whispered, lifting her head to wipe both of her eyes with the back of her wrist before smoothing my collar, flipping our inner armrests back down, and lacing her fingers fiercely through mine as Dad stepped out onto the sunny asphalt to unfold the ramp. "We did the right thing protecting each other—even when it hurts."

Without needing another word, my twin sister squeezed my hand twice, and side by side in our purple gowns—carrying both the relief of graduation and the quiet ache of Wesley's broken heart—the two of us rolled forward toward the van and the rest of our lives.
