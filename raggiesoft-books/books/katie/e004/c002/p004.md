## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:35 AM — Purple Gowns Under the Pavilion Colonnade & Two Squeezes Missing

*Click-whir.*

Dad lowered his thirty-five-millimeter film camera from his eye, blinked rapidly twice behind a proud, misty grin, and let out a long breath into the warm Atlantic breeze sweeping down Nineteenth Street across the sunlit concrete plaza of the Virginia Beach Pavilion.

"Hold right there for one more just in case somebody blinked," Uncle Steven called cheerfully beside him, steadying his disposable camera while Aunt Karen smoothed a stray lock of Katie's pale-blonde hair back under the edge of her royal-purple mortarboard cap and dabbed openly at the corner of her eye with a folded paper tissue. "Look at those gold cords, Karen. Both of them."

"We couldn't be prouder of you two if we tried," Aunt Karen said thickly, bending down to wrap Katie and then me in tight, lavender-scented hugs despite the crinkling purple satin sleeves bunched over our shoulders. Nobody needed to say out loud why Aunt Karen's voice caught a little harder around a sea of four hundred purple caps and gowns—having watched our cousin Brittany stop going to class and drop out earlier that spring still sat like a bruise under Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven's ribs—yet neither of them would have missed watching Katie and me roll side by side across that graduation stage for anything in the world.

Twenty minutes earlier inside the cool, cavernous exhibit hall beneath the Pavilion's white steel space-frame roof trusses—after the underclassman symphonic band had looped the slow, brassy strains of *Pomp and Circumstance* for forty straight minutes while four hundred seniors filed into place—Katie and I had eased our compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs up the temporary aluminum stage ramp one right behind the other. When Principal Halloway read *Katie Marie Prairie* followed immediately by *Nathaniel David Prairie*, we had crossed the wooden platform almost hub to hub, accepted our dark-purple leatherette *Advanced Studies Diploma* folders, and reached up in twin synchrony to move our purple-and-gold Class of 2003 tassels from the right side of our mortarboards over to the left while Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven whistled from the accessible family row loud enough to echo off the rafters.

Around us now on the wide outdoor walkway, plastic air horns bleated, camera flashes popped under the blooming pink crape-myrtle trees, and knots of laughing eighteen-year-olds tossed mortarboards toward the cloudless June sky.

"All right, let's beat the parking-lot gridlock before Nineteenth Street turns into a bumper-to-bumper mess," Dad said, slinging his camera strap securely over his shoulder and glancing down at the two of us parked in the dappled shade beside a brick crape-myrtle planter along the Pavilion colonnade. "Steve and Karen and I are going to walk fast over to the west lot and pull the van around to the accessible curb cut right down there by the flagpole. There's cold ginger ale and apple juice on ice in the back—you two good waiting right here in the shade for five minutes?"

"More than good, Dad," Katie smiled, patting the leatherette diploma folder resting across her lap. "Take your time."

The moment Dad, Aunt Karen, and Uncle Steven disappeared into the stream of families heading toward the west lot, Katie reached over, flipped up the padded inner armrests between our two wheelchairs, and leaned sideways across the narrow gap to wrap both of her purple-robed arms firmly around my shoulders. I hugged my twin sister right back just as tight, resting my cheek against the cool satin of her collar for ten steady seconds until the lingering acoustic buzz of four thousand clapping spectators drained out of both of our chests.

"We finished Northwood, Nate," Katie murmured happily against my shoulder as she sat back, flipped our inner armrests down, and laced her right fingers securely through my left hand. "Next stop, CCHR with Dad."

"No more homeroom bells," I agreed, feeling my shoulders drop an inch—until an insistent, unmistakable two-syllable vocalization cut sharply through the crowd noise fifteen feet to my right.

It wasn't a calm three-note hum. It was a firm, commanding sound deep in a teenager's chest, followed immediately by a second sharp vocal burst.

Before I even finished turning my head, a tall, sandy-haired sixteen-year-old boy in a neat navy-blue polo shirt and pressed khakis marched straight out from behind a cluster of parents with his right fingers closed around a smooth blue silicone ring and his left hand gripping a fistful of someone else's royal-purple graduation sleeve—actively pulling a pale, trembling classmate out of the foot traffic and straight toward the shade of our crape-myrtle tree.

It was Wesley—and stumbling half a step behind his tugging hand in a purple cap and gown identical to ours was Heather Collins.

All the color had drained from Heather's cheeks under the bright June sun. She clutched her leatherette diploma folder tightly against her sternum with her free arm, her hazel eyes wide and shimmering with dread, though she wasn't pulling back against her cousin's grip.

"Okay, Wes, okay—I'm coming, I promised you I would," Heather whispered shakily under her breath.

Wesley had shot up at least three inches since our Saturday afternoons at Emerald Creek Mall and our house on Arvin Road. The second he reached the edge of our shade and let go of Heather's purple sleeve, a brief, genuine smile broke through for Katie and me alone: he rocked once on his dark sneakers, reached down beside my armrest, and tapped the back of my knuckles twice in warm greeting.

Then, before Heather could say another word, Wesley stepped right back beside Heather's left shoulder and pivoted squarely to face his cousin.

Every trace of that greeting smile vanished from Wesley's face, replaced in an instant by a deep, furious scowl that creased his forehead right between his eyebrows. Letting out a sharp, indignant vocal sound in his throat, Wesley pointed emphatically with his whole hand at Heather's sternum, swept his arm straight down toward Katie's wheelchair and mine, tapped the side of his clenched fist twice against his open palm, and folded both arms tight across his navy polo shirt over his blue silicone ring—standing beside Heather like an immovable sentinel and glaring straight at the side of her face until she spoke.

I blinked in astonishment, looking from Wesley's unmistakable non-verbal command up to Heather's stricken face.

Heather swallowed hard, her throat working visibly above the collar of her graduation dress as tears sprang straight into her lower lashes.

"He's been furious with me ever since January," Heather admitted in a small, unsteady voice, not even trying to soften the truth while Wesley kept glaring hard at her from six inches off her left elbow. "You were the first real guy buddy Wes ever had in his whole life, Nate. Before we met you and Katie, boys our age either stared at him or walked ten feet around him, and getting together as the four of us at the mall was his favorite part of every single week. The very first Saturday after New Year's when Aunt Linda and my mom drove straight past the Emerald Creek turnoff on Military Highway and I had to tell him we weren't meeting you and Katie anymore because I screamed at you and broke up with you, Wes lost his temper at me like I've never seen in his life. That framed four-by-six photo Aunt Linda snapped of the four of us at Annie's Pretzels two Novembers ago is the only picture Wes has of himself and you together—he marched straight into the living room, took it off the shelf, and hid it in his bedroom so I couldn't touch it. Every single week since January—every time he sees my Northwood jacket or spots a city bus—he grabs my sleeve, makes that exact sound, and points toward Kempsville demanding I go apologize to both of you. All morning inside the hall while the band kept playing *Pomp and Circumstance*, Wes sat on the edge of his seat turning his blue ring and waiting for the ceremony to end, because the second he spotted your wheelchairs coming out the doors just now, he wasn't letting me near the car until I walked over here and said it properly."

Hearing that—that out of sixteen years of growing up surrounded by his mom and aunts and older female cousin, I had been the first guy friend Wesley ever really had—sent an ache straight through the middle of my chest. Whatever had shattered between Heather and me outside Room 204 in January, Wesley hadn't forgotten us for a single week.

"Hi, Wesley," I said softly, keeping my voice calm and warm just for him. "Look how tall you got this year. Katie and I missed seeing you too."

"Hi, Wesley," Katie added gently across my shoulder, offering him a genuine, kind smile before her eyes shifted carefully back up to Heather.

## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:50 AM — Dashed Hopes After *Pomp and Circumstance*, Wesley's Tears, & One Strictly Bounded Chance

![Outside the Virginia Beach Pavilion under blooming crape-myrtle trees after Northwood High School's June 2003 Commencement, eighteen-year-old blonde fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in matching compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs in royal-purple caps and gowns with gold honor cords holding inner hands with guarded, bittersweet expressions as remorseful classmate Heather Collins apologizes to both of them while her sixteen-year-old cousin Wesley folds his arms over his blue silicone ring and frowns angrily at Heather.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/jun_2003_pavilion_graduation_heather_apology_angry_wesley.jpg)

Standing three feet back from our footrests beside her scowling cousin so she wasn't crowding either wheelchair against the brick planter, Heather drew a long, trembling breath and looked directly at both of us—meeting Katie's blue eyes squarely before looking down into mine.

"My parents and Aunt Linda are waiting over by the east crosswalk," Heather said quietly, folding both hands tight around her purple leatherette diploma folder to keep her fingers from shaking. "Wes is right to be mad at me, and he's right to make me stand here. I wrote you an apology note back in February, Nate, and twice I stood in the upstairs hallway trying to hand it to you, and I realized a folded piece of notebook paper was the coward's way out. I couldn't walk out of high school without looking both of you in the face—you, Nate, and you too, Katie—and issuing a real apology."

Beside me across our armrests, Katie's fingers tightened steadily around mine, though she didn't interrupt. Over by Heather's left shoulder, Wesley kept his arms folded over his blue ring, watching every move of Heather's mouth like a hawk to make sure she didn't back out.

"On January sixth, both of you stood right in front of my locker and told me the complete truth," Heather said, her voice cracking slightly over the distant hum of Nineteenth Street traffic while a single tear slipped past her lower lashes onto her cheek. "And I screamed over top of you and didn't even let you finish two sentences. Then on February eleventh in the cafeteria, I sat six feet away from you and heard Tyler pull the exact same two-step lie word for word on Kristen Bauer—even doubling down and swearing that the redhead in the green parka wasn't her boyfriend's cousin when Kristen called him an idiot—and I watched Kristen stand her ground and laugh right in his face because she actually trusted the person she loved. That should have been me, Nate. You never once lied to me in eighteen months. Katie never once lied to me. You protected your sister on New Year's Eve, you kept your promise not to drink, and because my pride got stung in front of twenty kids in homeroom when Tyler doubled down, I screamed at you, threw everything we had in your faces, and pushed both of you out of our lives."

Heather wiped her cheek quickly with the heel of her hand and drew a shaky breath, looking earnestly back at Katie and me.

"I know I don't have any right to expect things to go back to how they were before Christmas," Heather whispered. "I'm just asking if there is any chance at all that you might let me start over from zero and try to earn both of your trust back."

Right beside Heather's purple sleeve, Wesley's folded arms loosened by two inches.

Leaning half a step forward onto the balls of his sneakers, sixteen-year-old Wesley turned his smooth blue silicone ring eagerly between both palms and looked straight from Katie's face to mine with wide, shining brown eyes, letting out a soft, rising two-note vocal hum under his breath. Having sat through that whole endless graduation march waiting for Heather to apologize the way he had demanded since January, Wesley was hoping with his whole heart that Katie and I would say yes and put everything right back the way it used to be.

Seeing that raw, unguarded hope on Wesley's face hurt ten times worse than anything Heather could have said.

Part of me—the same aching corner of my chest that still knew every note of *Unhandled Exception* by heart—felt a sharp, dizzy pull behind my sternum. Hearing Heather confess out loud without a single excuse that she knew we had told the truth lifted a five-month stone off my lungs.

And at the exact same second, right underneath that relief, a cold, instinctive knot of pure terror clamped shut around my stomach—the exact same protective fear I could feel stiffening right through Katie's palm into mine.

"Heather, thank you for coming over here and saying all of that to our faces," Katie spoke first—not sharply, not triumphantly, her voice quiet and honest and steady beside my shoulder. "Hearing you say out loud that you know Nate told the truth really does mean something to both of us. It's just... I watched what your screaming at that locker did to my brother for five straight months. Neither Nate nor I can put ourselves back in a relationship where every single day we're terrified that the next time somebody spreads a rumor or misunderstands something, you might blow up at us like that all over again."

Heather's breath hitched softly, though she didn't step forward or try to argue over my sister. Beside her, the hopeful hum in Wesley's throat cut off cleanly as his brown eyes darted down to my face.

I swallowed hard past the lump in my throat, wishing for Wesley's sake there were any other honest answer inside me, and met Heather's hazel eyes directly.

"Katie's right," I said quietly, keeping my left hand anchored tight in my twin sister's fingers while speaking as gently and plainly as I knew how. "I don't hate you, Heather. Even in January, I never hated you, and I really am grateful you told both of us today that you know we didn't lie. When you slammed your locker at eight in the morning and screamed at me like eighteen months together hadn't meant anything... it scared me in a way I don't know how to undo. We can't reform what we had, Heather. We don't trust that you wouldn't snap at us a second time, and neither Katie nor I can live scared like that."

Just behind us across the plaza, the heavy glass lobby doors swung wide for a departing wave of band students, sending one last booming, brassy reprise of *Pomp and Circumstance* rolling straight out under the colonnade.

Beside Heather, our gentle refusal didn't just disappoint sixteen-year-old Wesley—it broke his heart right in front of us.

All the eager light went out of his brown eyes at once. At the sound of that stately graduation march echoing behind our words, Wesley flinched as if the brass horns had struck him in the chest, pressing one wrist hard against his ear while his shoulders caved inward inside his navy polo shirt. His lower lip trembled hard, and two fat, silent tears spilled straight over his lower lashes down his cheeks as a small, raw whimper of pure heartbreak caught in his throat. Clutching his blue silicone ring tight against his sternum, Wesley stared down at the gap between our wheelchairs like the floor had dropped out from under his sneakers—furious all over again with Heather for screaming at me in January and driving us away, even though he had stood right beside her and understood she truly tried to apologize, and utterly crushed at losing the first real guy buddy he had ever had.

Beside me, Katie let out a tiny, stricken intake of breath. Her fingers convulsed around mine, and when I glanced half an inch sideways, real tears were shimmering along my twin sister's lower eyelids too. My own throat closed up so hard my vision blurred; watching Wesley's heart break through zero fault of his own tore both Katie and me apart right where we sat.

Looking sideways at the silent tears tracking down her sixteen-year-old cousin's cheeks and then back at the tears shining in Katie's eyes and mine, Heather lost the last shred of composure she had been holding onto. Wiping her face quickly with the back of her wrist—not begging for herself, and not trying to undo a single word of our answer—she took half a step back so she wasn't crowding us and looked earnestly at both of us.

"Nate, Katie—please listen to me for thirty seconds," Heather pleaded softly, her voice shaking around her cousin's quiet sniffle. "I accept that you and I are over, Nate. I'm not asking to be your girlfriend again, and I swear I won't ever try to talk you into dating me again. Is there anything—anything at all—that I can do to prove to the two of you that I learned from my mistake, just so Wes doesn't lose his friend because of what I did?"

Beside me, Katie blinked through wet lashes, looking from Wesley's hunched shoulders up to Heather. "What do you mean, Heather?"

"Our family is originally from Minnesota—my mom and Aunt Linda grew up outside Saint Paul—and now that high school is finished, we're going home," Heather explained quietly and plainly, resting a gentle, steadying palm on Wesley's trembling forearm when he didn't pull away from her completely. "Our house closing is on June thirtieth, two Mondays from now. I'm starting college at North Star State University in the fall, and my parents and Aunt Linda are already talking about me taking over Wes's care back home in Minnesota. Because of his disability—and right now because he's still sixteen—Wes can never go anywhere on his own. For the rest of his life, he's always going to need an ADA aide right beside him wherever he goes, and that's going to be me, along with becoming his guardian and conservator once he's old enough. We don't even live on a bus route here, so the only way Wes can see you one more time before we move home is if I drive him to Emerald Creek Mall and stay with him as his aide."

Heather swallowed hard, keeping her hands folded cleanly over her diploma folder.

"Give me one Saturday afternoon in broad daylight before June thirtieth—just as Wes's driver and aide," Heather whispered. "No couples talk, no trying to hold your hand, no excuses. If I say one wrong word or make either of you uncomfortable for a single second, you can roll straight back to the Route 15 bus stop and never speak to me again. Just please don't let Wes leave Virginia thinking you stopped wanting to be his buddy."

Next to Heather, Wesley lifted his tear-streaked face half an inch, his red-rimmed brown eyes fixed nervously on mine over the blue silicone ring clutched against his chest.

Katie turned her head toward me across our armrests. Neither of us needed to say a word out loud to read what the other was thinking: nothing on earth—whether Heather was moving back home to Minnesota in two weeks or staying five minutes down Kempsville Road for the next fifty years—was ever going to make us risk putting my heart back inside a romantic relationship with Heather. Yet leaving sixteen-year-old Wesley crying under a crape-myrtle tree believing he had lost his first guy friend through no fault of his own was something neither my sister nor I could stomach.

Katie gave my left hand two steady, questioning squeezes. Slowly, I squeezed her hand twice right back.

"Next Saturday loses Dad to a CCHR study group, so the Saturday after that—June twenty-eighth, right before you move," Katie said carefully and clearly, setting the boundary in plain English so nobody could misunderstand it. "Nate and I will catch the southbound Route 15 bus down Military Highway that gets into Emerald Creek Mall around twelve-thirty-five. You and Heather are strictly exes, Heather—you're only coming as Wesley's driver and his aide. One raised voice, or one push past that line, and Nate and I leave immediately."

"Agreed—one hundred percent agreed, Katie," Heather breathed out, bowing her head in immediate, humble gratitude without taking a single inch more than my sister had offered. "We'll wait right outside by the Emerald Creek bus stop for the 15 so you don't even have to come looking for us."

Looking straight at Wesley, I leaned slightly forward in my seat and held my open right hand halfway out at waist height.

"Did you hear that, Wesley?" I said gently past the ache still sitting heavy in my chest. "Heather and I aren't getting back together, buddy—that part can't change. You and I are still friends, though. Katie and I will meet you at the bus stop for Annie's Pretzels on the twenty-eighth before you go home to Minnesota."

For two seconds, Wesley stared at my hand as if afraid to believe it was real. Then, letting out a shaky, hitched breath through his nose, he wiped his wet cheek roughly with his left sleeve and reached forward to tap his smooth blue silicone ring twice against the back of my knuckles—a fragile, grateful *two squeezes*—before stepping back beside Heather's shoulder and shooting her one last stern, warning look to make sure she didn't mess this chance up either.

Down at the flagpole curb cut forty feet away, Dad eased our silver accessible van up beside the yellow paint and clicked on his hazard flashers.

"Thank you, Katie. Thank you, Nate," Heather murmured quietly, keeping her respectful distance as she guided Wesley toward the east crosswalk where Aunt Linda and her parents were waiting. "We'll see you at the Route 15 stop on the twenty-eighth."

Once they disappeared into the crowd of purple gowns—and once the last distant notes of *Pomp and Circumstance* faded back behind the closing Pavilion doors—Katie flipped up our inner armrests, leaned straight across the gap between our SwiftChairs, and hugged me tight for five quiet seconds before we rolled side by side toward Dad's ramp.
