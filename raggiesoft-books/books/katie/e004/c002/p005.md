## Saturday, June 14, 2003, 11:52 AM — Dashed Hopes After *Pomp and Circumstance*, Wesley's Tears, & One Strictly Bounded Saturday

"Heather, thank you for coming over here and saying all of that to our faces," Katie spoke first—not sharply, not triumphantly, her voice quiet and honest and steady beside my shoulder. "Hearing you say out loud that you know Nate told the truth really does mean something to both of us. It's just... I watched what your screaming at that locker did to my brother for five straight months. Neither Nate nor I can put ourselves back in a relationship where every single day we're terrified that the next time somebody spreads a rumor or misunderstands something, you might blow up at us like that all over again."

Heather's breath hitched softly, though she didn't step forward or try to argue over my sister. Beside her, the hopeful hum in Wesley's throat cut off cleanly as his brown eyes darted down to my face.

I swallowed hard past the lump in my throat, wishing for Wesley's sake there were any other honest answer inside me, and met Heather's hazel eyes directly.

"Katie's right," I said quietly, keeping my left hand anchored tight in my twin sister's fingers while speaking as gently and plainly as I knew how. "I don't hate you, Heather. Even in January, I never hated you, and I really am grateful you told both of us today that you know we didn't lie. When you slammed your locker at eight in the morning and screamed at me like eighteen months together hadn't meant anything... it scared me in a way I don't know how to undo. We can't reform what we had, Heather. We don't trust that you wouldn't snap at us a second time, and neither Katie nor I can live scared like that."

Just behind us across the plaza, the heavy glass lobby doors swung wide for a departing wave of band students, sending one last booming, brassy reprise of *Pomp and Circumstance* rolling straight out under the colonnade.

Beside Heather, our gentle refusal didn't just disappoint sixteen-year-old Wesley—it broke his heart right in front of us.

All the eager light went out of his brown eyes at once. At the sound of that stately graduation march echoing behind our words, Wesley flinched as if the brass horns had struck him in the chest, pressing one wrist hard against his ear while his shoulders caved inward inside his navy polo shirt. His lower lip trembled hard, and two fat, silent tears spilled straight over his lower lashes down his cheeks as a small, raw whimper of pure heartbreak caught in his throat. Clutching his blue silicone ring tight against his sternum, Wesley stared down at the gap between our wheelchairs like the floor had dropped out from under his sneakers—furious all over again with Heather for screaming at me in January and driving us away, even though he had stood right beside her and understood she truly tried to apologize, and utterly crushed at losing the first real guy buddy he had ever had.

Beside me, Katie let out a tiny, stricken intake of breath. Her fingers convulsed around mine, and when I glanced half an inch sideways, real tears were shimmering along my twin sister's lower eyelids too. My own throat closed up so hard my vision blurred; watching Wesley's heart break through zero fault of his own tore both Katie and me apart right where we sat.

Looking sideways at the silent tears tracking down her sixteen-year-old cousin's cheeks and then back at the tears shining in Katie's eyes and mine, Heather lost the last shred of composure she had been holding onto. Wiping her face quickly with the back of her wrist—not begging for herself, and not trying to undo a single word of our answer—she took half a step back so she wasn't crowding us and looked earnestly at both of us.

"Nate, Katie—please listen to me for thirty seconds," Heather pleaded softly, her voice shaking around her cousin's quiet sniffle. "I accept that you and I are over, Nate. I'm not asking to be your girlfriend again, and I swear I won't ever try to talk you into dating me again. Is there anything—anything at all—that I can do to prove to the two of you that I learned from my mistake, just so Wes doesn't lose his friend because of what I did?"

Beside me, Katie blinked through wet lashes, looking from Wesley's hunched shoulders up to Heather. "What do you mean, Heather?"

"Our family is originally from Minnesota—my mom and Aunt Linda grew up outside Saint Paul—and now that high school is finished, we're going home," Heather explained quietly and plainly, resting a gentle, steadying palm on Wesley's trembling forearm when he didn't pull away from her completely. "Our house closing is on June thirtieth, two Mondays from now. I'm starting college at North Star State University in the fall, and my parents and Aunt Linda are already talking about me taking over Wes's care back home in Minnesota. Because of his disability—and right now because he's still sixteen—Wes can never go anywhere on his own. For the rest of his life, he's always going to need an ADA aide right beside him wherever he goes, and that's going to be me, along with becoming his guardian and conservator once he's old enough. We don't even live on a bus route here, so the only way Wes can see you one more time before we move home is if I drive him to Emerald Creek Mall and stay with him as his aide."

Heather swallowed hard, keeping her hands folded cleanly over her diploma folder.

"Give me one Saturday afternoon in broad daylight before June thirtieth—just as Wes's driver and aide," Heather whispered. "No couples talk, no trying to hold your hand, no excuses. If I say one wrong word or make either of you uncomfortable for a single second, you can roll straight back to the Route 15 bus stop and never speak to me again. Just please don't let Wes leave Virginia thinking you stopped wanting to be his buddy."

Next to Heather, Wesley lifted his tear-streaked face half an inch, his red-rimmed brown eyes fixed nervously on mine over the blue silicone ring clutched against his chest.

Katie turned her head toward me across our armrests. Neither of us needed to say a word out loud to read what the other was thinking: nothing on earth—whether Heather was moving back home to Minnesota in two weeks or staying five minutes down Kempsville Road for the next fifty years—was ever going to make us risk putting my heart back inside a romantic relationship with Heather. Yet leaving sixteen-year-old Wesley crying under a crape-myrtle tree believing he had lost his first guy friend through no fault of his own was something neither my sister nor I could stomach.

Katie gave my left hand two steady, questioning squeezes. Slowly, I squeezed her hand twice right back.

"Next Saturday loses Dad to a CCHR study group, so the Saturday after that—June twenty-eighth, right before you move," Katie said carefully and clearly, setting the boundary in plain English so nobody could misunderstand it. "Nate and I will catch the southbound Route 15 bus down Military Highway that gets into Emerald Creek Mall around twelve-thirty-five. You and Heather are strictly exes, Heather—you're only coming as Wesley's driver and his aide. One raised voice, or one push past that line, and Nate and I leave immediately."

"Agreed—one hundred percent agreed, Katie," Heather breathed out, bowing her head in immediate, humble gratitude without taking a single inch more than my sister had offered. "We'll wait right outside by the Emerald Creek bus stop for the 15 so you don't even have to come looking for us."

Looking straight at Wesley, I leaned slightly forward in my seat and held my open right hand halfway out at waist height.

"Did you hear that, Wesley?" I said gently past the ache still sitting heavy in my chest. "Heather and I aren't getting back together, buddy—that part can't change. You and I are still friends, though. Katie and I will meet you at the bus stop for Annie's Pretzels on the twenty-eighth before you go home to Minnesota."

For two seconds, Wesley stared at my hand as if afraid to believe it was real. Then, letting out a shaky, hitched breath through his nose, he wiped his wet cheek roughly with his left sleeve and reached forward to tap his smooth blue silicone ring twice against the back of my knuckles—a fragile, grateful *two squeezes*—before stepping back beside Heather's shoulder and shooting her one last stern, warning look to make sure she didn't mess this chance up either.

Down at the flagpole curb cut forty feet away, Dad eased our silver accessible van up beside the yellow paint and clicked on his hazard flashers.

"Thank you, Katie. Thank you, Nate," Heather murmured quietly, keeping her respectful distance as she guided Wesley toward the east crosswalk where Aunt Linda and her parents were waiting. "We'll see you at the Route 15 stop on the twenty-eighth."

Once they disappeared into the crowd of purple gowns—and once the last distant notes of *Pomp and Circumstance* faded back behind the closing Pavilion doors—Katie flipped up our inner armrests, leaned straight across the gap between our SwiftChairs, and hugged me tight for five quiet seconds before we rolled side by side toward Dad's ramp.

## Saturday, June 28, 2003, 12:35 PM — Two Nervous Figures Behind the Yellow Curb Strip

*Don't walk away... Thank you so much.*

Even after I clicked the stop button on my silver portable disc player, slipped the lightweight foam headphones off my ears into my canvas lap pouch as southbound Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Bus 914 turned off Military Highway onto the Emerald Creek Mall ring road, Cassidy O'Connell's raw, trembling live voice from The Stardust Engine's *Live in Chicago* album kept echoing straight behind my breastbone.

All week on Arvin Road—while Dad helped Katie and me pack two neat cartons of kitchenware for the ground-floor studio apartment on Greenway Court in Norfolk we were moving into on Tuesday morning—I had replayed Track Four over and over with my eyes shut. Every crack in Cassidy's live vocal sounded like somebody had put a microphone directly against the bruised spot in my own chest: mourning promises people didn't keep, hurting over walls you didn't ask to build, and holding onto the one unbreakable fortress sitting two inches to my left.

Beside me in the forward wheelchair bay, Katie reached across our inner armrests and gave my left hand a steady, grounding squeeze as the air brakes hissed outside the mall's south entrance.

Through the tall tinted windshield, right past the blue-and-white CVTA bus-stop sign in the bright late-June sunshine, two figures were already waiting ten feet back from the yellow concrete curb strip.

Because Heather's neighborhood off Kempsville had no bus route, she had driven Aunt Linda's tan sedan over to the mall early, parked out in the east lot, and walked sixteen-year-old Wesley across the plaza to meet us right where the 15 let off. Neither of them looked calm. Standing in a modest cream summer knit top and jeans with her car keys gripped tight in her closed fist at her side, Heather stood stiff-shouldered as if terrified that breathing too loudly might make the bus pull away again. Half a step closer to the shelter post in a clean sky-blue polo shirt and khaki pants, a canvas daypack slung over one shoulder, sixteen-year-old Wesley rocked fast from heel to toe—both hands clenched white-knuckled around his smooth blue silicone ring against his sternum, his wide brown eyes locked nervously onto the folding front doors as the Elevate-U hydraulic stairwell lift clicked into motion.

Looking down at Wesley's scared face from the top of the lift platform, my throat tightened. You could read every ounce of what he was bracing for right in the set of his shoulders: Nate had been the first real guy friend Wesley ever had in sixteen years, graduation two Saturdays ago had broken his heart once already, and right up until our tires touched the pavement, Wesley was scared to death that his heart was about to get broken all over again.

The steel safety flap folded flat against the warm sidewalk concrete with a metallic *clink*.

Rolling straight off the lift hub to hub with Katie right on my left, I braked three feet in front of Wesley, let out a slow breath to steady my own racing pulse, and offered him a genuine, warm smile—holding my right hand out at waist height with my knuckles turned toward him.

"Hey, Wesley," I said gently. "Told you Katie and I would be on the twelve-thirty-five bus. Ready to go ride the glass elevator?"

For one heartbeat, Wesley just stared at my knuckles. Then all the terrified tension dropped out of his shoulders in a rush; a huge, shaky two-note vocal hum burst out of his chest, a sunlit grin broke across his face, and he tapped the back of my knuckles twice—firm and quick—before tapping Katie's offered hand once in happy greeting too.

"Hi, Wesley," Katie smiled warmly, before glancing politely half an inch higher without letting go of my left hand. "Hi, Heather."

"Hi, Katie. Hi, Nate—thank you both for coming," Heather said quietly, keeping both hands folded cleanly around her car keys and stepping carefully to Wesley's far right side as Wesley reached instinctively for her wrist the way he always did in busy parking-lot foot traffic.

As the four of us turned toward the sliding glass mall entrance and the cream-and-brass hydraulic glass elevator inside, the new shape of our group settled onto the terracotta quarry tiles without anybody needing to explain it. Two years ago, Katie had rolled on my left holding my left hand, Heather had walked flush beside my right wheel holding my right hand, and Wesley had walked on Heather's right in one unbroken line of four. Today, Katie held my left hand, Heather walked purely as Wesley's ADA aide holding Wesley's left hand—and between my right armrest and Wesley's elbow sat eighteen quiet inches of empty floor that nobody crossed.

## Saturday, June 28, 2003, 1:05 PM — Wesley's Plea to Hug It Out & A Question From Homeroom 212

Upstairs under the soaring white tensioned-fabric ceiling cones of the Food Court, right between the warm red-and-gold storefront of Annie's Pretzels and the checkered counter of Crown Burger across the brass-and-glass mezzanine railing, we rolled up to the exact same corner four-top laminate table where the four of us had always sat.

Without needing to be asked, Heather cleared the two lightweight chairs off the left side of the table so Katie and I could ease our SwiftChairs underneath, set down a red plastic tray holding Wesley's warm bite-sized salted pretzel twists and an unbranded apple juice box with the bendy straw already angled cleanly through the foil dot while I set Katie's and my pretzel tray down opposite him—and then, instead of pulling her chair up tight beside the table the way she used to when she was my girlfriend, Heather quietly slid her wooden chair two feet back to Wesley's right and folded both hands neatly in her lap, giving Wesley, Katie, and me the table to ourselves.

Before touching a single pretzel piece, Wesley bent sideways, unzipped the padded front pocket of the canvas daypack resting against his sneaker, and lifted one corner of a bubble-wrapped silver four-by-six picture frame just high enough for Katie and me to recognize the glossy snapshot inside—that single thirty-five-millimeter photograph Aunt Linda had taken of the four of us right here at this exact table back in November of tenth grade. Wesley tapped the glass right over my face twice with a proud, protective hum before zipping the frame safely back inside his pack.

"Aunt Linda packed the rest of the living room into boxes yesterday for the movers," Heather explained softly from her chair beside him, speaking only enough to translate the context without inserting herself. "Wes wouldn't let that frame go anywhere near a cardboard box. It's the only picture he has of himself with you, Nate, so he's carrying it on his lap in the car all the way home to Saint Paul on Monday."

Something hot stung behind the bridge of my nose. I swallowed hard and smiled straight across at him. "Good thinking, Wes. Moving trucks bounce way too much for glass frames."

Wesley took two happy bites of warm salted pretzel, rocked once on his chair, and then paused—his brown eyes shifting slowly from my right hand, resting alone beside my joystick controller, over to Heather's empty hands folded quietly in her lap two feet back.

A deep, unmistakable crease of worry appeared right between Wesley's eyebrows.

Setting his blue silicone ring down beside his tray for three seconds, sixteen-year-old Wesley pointed earnestly with his open hand at my chest, pointed straight sideways at Heather's shoulder, and then wrapped both of his own arms tightly across his sky-blue polo shirt—rocking once in a clear, unmistakable *hug* gesture while letting out a low, pleading two-syllable vocal sound and looking straight into my eyes.

Across our inner armrests, I felt Katie's fingers go completely still inside mine.

Looking straight into that meaningful, pleading look in Wesley's brown eyes nearly broke me right there under the skylight. Wesley wasn't trying to hurt me or ignore our rules. In sixteen-year-old Wesley's concrete, literal mind, the math of the past two years was painfully simple: when Nate and Heather hugged and held hands as boyfriend and girlfriend, Wesley got to come to Emerald Creek Mall with his first guy friend; when Nate and Heather stopped hugging and broke up in January, Nate vanished for five straight months. Wesley wanted to see Heather and me hug it out and reform some semblance of what used to be there simply because he was terrified that if Heather and I weren't boyfriend and girlfriend again, Monday morning was going to swallow up his friendship with me forever.

Sitting three feet from Heather with Cassidy's raw *Live in Chicago* chorus still ringing through my ribs, part of me ached so badly my fingers trembled on my armrest—and yet, right beside that ache, both Katie and I knew the truth down in our bones. Even if Heather's family weren't driving thirteen hundred miles back home to Minnesota on Monday morning—even if she were staying five minutes up Indian River Road for the rest of our lives—Katie and I still had to protect ourselves. Once someone had slammed a metal locker and screamed in our faces without letting us finish two sentences, neither of us could ever hand her that kind of power over our safety again.

Before Katie or I even had to say the hard words out loud, Heather leaned gently forward from her chair, rested two light fingertips on Wesley's forearm to get his eye contact, and slowly shook her head at her cousin.

"No, Wes—put your hands down, buddy," Heather told him in a quiet, steady, eye-level voice with zero baby talk and zero self-pity, even though a faint pink flush of sorrow rose up her throat. "Nate doesn't have to hug me, and Nate and I aren't going back to being boyfriend and girlfriend. Even if we weren't moving home to Minnesota on Monday, we wouldn't be—because I broke Nate's trust in January, and Nate and Katie have every right to protect themselves. You don't need Nate and me to date for Nate to be your friend."

Wesley blinked slowly at Heather, his arms lowering halfway down his chest as his anxious brown eyes darted back across the laminate tabletop to me.

Leaning forward in my SwiftChair, I reached my right hand across the middle of the table so Wesley could see I wasn't pulling away from *him*.

"Heather's right, Wesley," I told him gently, keeping my voice as clear and solid as I knew how. "Heather and I aren't getting back together—that part isn't meant to be. That doesn't mean you lose Katie and me, though. You're my friend all on your own."

For four quiet seconds, Wesley looked from my outstretched knuckles over to Heather sitting quietly back in her chair, and then back to me again. You could watch the two halves of it settle behind his eyes at once: he still wished with everything in him for that old, effortless normalcy between Heather and me, yet looking at both of our faces, he understood it wasn't meant to be. Letting out a quiet, resigned breath through his nose, Wesley picked up his blue silicone ring in his left hand and reached his right hand across the table to tap my knuckles twice—accepting what was real instead of nothing at all.

"Wait—Heather? Nate?" a surprised girl's voice called out six feet to our right along the mezzanine walkway.

All four of us glanced up as Lauren Mitchell—who had sat two desks behind Heather in Homeroom 212 every morning of senior year, including that awful Monday morning on January sixth—stopped beside the brass railing with two department-store bags looped over her wrist, blinking back and forth between our wheelchairs and Heather's chair.

"Hey, congrats again on graduation!" Lauren said with a curious, half-teasing grin, glancing straight from Heather to me. "Wait a second... are you and Nate getting back together?"

At the words *getting back together*, Wesley's chin lifted half an inch in reflex before he caught himself. Beside me, Katie's hand tightened sharply around mine—both of us bracing by sheer muscle memory because twenty kids whispering in Homeroom 212 was the exact spark that had detonated Heather's pride back in January.

Heather didn't flinch, didn't blush, and didn't try to save face for half a heartbeat. Sitting straight in her wooden chair beside Wesley with her hands calm in her lap, she looked directly up at Lauren in the middle of the crowded Saturday Food Court.

"No, Lauren, we aren't," Heather answered clearly and evenly, loud enough that nobody had to strain to hear a syllable. "Even if my family weren't moving back home to Minnesota on Monday, Nate and I wouldn't be getting back together—because back in January I listened to Tyler's disgusting lie in homeroom and screamed at Nate instead of trusting the most honest person in our class. Nate and Katie were just kind enough to come meet my cousin Wes for pretzels before we move."

Lauren blinked twice, all the gossip dropping straight out of her face. "Oh—uh, yeah, everybody figured out by spring what a liar Tyler was. Safe drive up to Minnesota, Heather. Have a great summer, Nate—bye, Katie!"

"Have a good summer, Lauren," Katie answered politely as Lauren hurried off toward the center escalators.

Once Lauren was out of earshot, nobody at our corner table spoke for five full seconds. Beside me, I felt about ten pounds of defensive tension melt quietly out of Katie's shoulders through our joined hands. Nothing Heather had just said changed our answer—romantic trust was still gone for good—yet hearing her stand up in front of someone from Homeroom 212 and put one hundred percent of the truth on her own shoulders meant Katie and I could spend the next forty minutes with Wesley without a single knot of fear in our stomachs.

## Saturday, June 28, 2003, 1:45 PM — A Smooth Toy Bus, Our New Greenway Court Card, & Goodbye Before Minnesota

![Inside the sunlit second-floor Food Court of Emerald Creek Mall beneath white tented fabric ceiling cones in June 2003 with Crown Burger and Annie's Pretzels in the background, eighteen-year-old blonde fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in matching compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding inner hands while Nate reaches across the table to tap sixteen-year-old Wesley Collins's knuckles beside a miniature toy city bus and soft pretzel tray as Heather Collins sits respectfully half a chair back with her hands folded quietly in her lap.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/jun_2003_emerald_creek_mall_food_court_goodbye_wesley_nate_katie_heather.jpg)

Ten minutes before Katie and I needed to head back down the glass elevator for the two-o'clock northbound Route 15 bus up Military Highway, I unzipped the front pocket of my canvas lap pouch and lifted out two small things Katie and I had put together at Dad's desk the night before—strictly for Wesley.

First, I set a smooth-edged, three-inch blue-and-white die-cast miniature city bus keyring with tiny rolling black rubber tires right onto the clean laminate tabletop beside Wesley's red tray.

Wesley's eyes went wide with instant delight. Setting his blue silicone ring onto his knee for a second, he picked up the smooth little bus, rolled its rubber wheels twice back and forth across the tabletop with a happy three-note hum, and then held both his blue ring and the tiny bus comfortably together in his left palm.

"And Nate and I printed and laminated this for your backpack too, Wesley, just like our bus passes," Katie said warmly, sliding a heavy, rounded-corner wallet card sealed in thick clear plastic across to him. On the front, above Wesley's name in bold high-contrast lettering, we had printed two small wheelchair icons beside two solid blue circles representing our two-tap buddy greeting; on the back, Katie had printed our brand-new Norfolk mailing address at Apartment A6 on Greenway Court—where Dad was moving us in on Tuesday morning—along with the household email address tied to our new Norfolk apartment cable-internet account.

"That's for Wesley," I said quietly, glancing briefly over Wesley's shoulder to meet Heather's hazel eyes so the boundary stayed crystal clear on both sides. "Whenever Wes wants to send or get a picture postcard once you're settled back home in Minnesota—buses, snowstorms, lakes, whatever he likes—Aunt Linda or you can help him write us at our new apartment in Norfolk, and I promise Katie and I will always make sure Wes has our address whenever we move."

Heather nodded once from her chair two feet back from the table, her hands still folded quietly in her lap and her eyes bright with unshed sorrow and gratitude that she kept carefully blinked back so she wouldn't make the moment about herself. Seeing her backed away from our corner table like an outsider—in the exact spot where Aunt Linda's camera had once caught the four of us pressed close together—sent one more slow, hollow ache right through my ribs.

"Aunt Linda and I will keep that card right beside his framed photo on his dresser in Minnesota," Heather promised softly, leaning forward just long enough to help Wesley zip the laminated wallet card safely into the front pocket of his daypack beside his November 2001 frame before settling quietly back into her chair again. "And whenever I take over as Wes's guardian and conservator down the road, I'll make sure he never loses it."

Before we turned our wheelchairs toward the glass elevator, sixteen-year-old Wesley glanced over his right shoulder at Heather sitting backed away from the table, and then looked wistfully back across at me—still wanting in his heart to see Heather and me hug it out, even though it simply wasn't meant to be.

Then Wesley's brown eyes lingered slowly on my face, flicking down to the green battery lights on my right-hand controller and back up to Katie and me with a quiet, heavy solemnity that made my throat close up. Postcards in a mailbox weren't warm pretzels across a table, and Minnesota was thirteen hundred miles away; sitting there with his blue silicone ring in his left hand beside his miniature city bus, Wesley was trying his absolute hardest to understand that once we stood up and headed down to the curb today, this was going to be the last time he ever saw me in person.

Reaching across the laminate tabletop one final time, Wesley held his right knuckles steady over the center seam and didn't pull back right away. Smiling past the sting behind my eyes, I reached my right hand straight across to meet him and tapped the back of his knuckles twice—slow, gentle, and unhurried—staying right there with him for three full seconds until he let out a long, brave breath and tapped mine twice in return.
