## Sunday, July 27, 2003, 10:15 AM — Stop Eight in Fishersville: Checking In at Bates-Harding & Stopping Short at Attached Redmond Hall

"Go ahead and print all four names on the visitor ledger right there, folks, and press these adhesive day passes onto your shirts where campus staff can see them," the receptionist behind the sliding glass check-in window instructed as eighteen-year-old Katie, Heather, sixteen-year-old Wesley, and I waited inside the scuffed green-and-white terrazzo lobby of the Bates-Harding Building at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville.

Driving thirty-five minutes south down Interstate 81 from Harrisonburg on a clear July morning had brought us to Stop Eight solely because a high-school transition specialist back in Virginia Beach had handed Aunt Linda a state vocational rehabilitation brochure in May, urging us not to rule out Virginia's residential post-high-school training center for Wesley without letting him see it in person ahead of junior year.

Even while Heather was still signing the paper check-in sheet at the reception counter and handing Katie and me our peel-and-stick visitor badges, the building didn't feel like any college campus we had seen. Built across former World War II Army hospital grounds, the sprawling 1960s and 1970s flat-roofed red-brick complex smelled sharply of antiseptic floor wax and institutional disinfectant; long rows of fluorescent ceiling troffers buzzed overhead; and painted directional stripes ran down the cinder-block walls from the main administration offices toward the medical clinics.

"Right on time—welcome to Roosevelt!" a brisk outreach tour guide said, stepping through the security door beside the check-in desk with a thick laminated binder tucked under her arm. "Before we even step outside to walk across the grounds toward our general student dormitories or the vocational classrooms, our main Bates-Harding Administration building connects directly down this interior corridor to our specialized residential wing—so let's start right down this hall while you have your visitor badges on."

Katie and I traded a quiet, uneasy squeeze across our inner SwiftChair armrests as we rolled down the wide interior hallway behind her. Walking half a step closer to Heather's right sleeve than usual, sixteen-year-old Wesley kept his left hand tucked deep inside his shorts pocket, turning his smooth blue silicone ring faster and faster against his palm as the corridor narrowed toward a heavy pair of wire-mesh fire doors locked by a wall-mounted badge reader.

Centered above the magnetic lock hung a brown engraved plastic sign: *Redmond Hall — Medical Residential Unit*.

"Suppose a student did look at a post-graduation vocational skills track here in two years," Heather asked calmly, pausing ten feet short of the heavy doors with one hand resting lightly near Wesley's forearm. "When family members or a legal guardian enroll at the same time, how do shared family suites or roommate assignments work on campus?"

The tour guide gave a practiced, polite little laugh and shook her head.

"Oh, goodness, no—we don't have family suites anywhere on campus, and every single dormitory here at Roosevelt is strictly gender-separated," she replied matter-of-factly. "Male clients live exclusively on the men's wings and female clients live exclusively on the women's wings, with strict curfews and zero opposite-gender access to dormitory rooms overnight."

Then the tour guide glanced down at the visitor inquiry card clipped to her binder, looked up at sixteen-year-old Wesley, and tapped her pen directly against the *Redmond Hall* sign beside the locked doors.

"And in Wesley's case, because he is non-verbal with developmental daily-living support needs, he wouldn't live out in the regular student dormitories across the courtyard anyway," the guide added smoothly. "Clients with Wesley's profile are required to reside right here inside Redmond Hall—our medical dorm wing attached here to the main administration building—monitored twenty-four hours a day by rotating night-shift orderlies and nurses behind the locked station, while family members sign in at the front desk during daytime visiting hours."

For three silent seconds inside that administration corridor, the buzzing fluorescent lights overhead were the only sound in the room.

Growing up, whenever Wesley got scared in the middle of the night—whether from a clap of summer thunder rattling the windowpane, a bad dream tangling him in his sheets, or strange shadows across an unfamiliar ceiling—he would often start to cry. And ever since he was a little boy, Wesley had instinctively known the one simple, unbreakable truth in his world: he could push back his covers, walk straight down the hall into Heather's bedroom, climb right into bed beside her, and let Heather wrap both arms warmly around him and cuddle with him until his chest stopped shaking and he calmed all the way down.

Heather was Wesley's Safe Person.

Staring through the wire-mesh glass of Redmond Hall at the pale green hospital-style corridor and the badge-locked nurses' desk—realizing in a single heartbeat that if he woke up scared and crying at two in the morning inside those doors, strangers in scrubs would keep him locked away where he could never walk into Heather's room or climb into her bed to be held—sixteen-year-old Wesley broke.

Two hot tears spilled straight down his cheeks, a raw sob tore out of his chest, and Wesley turned squarely into Heather, buried his face against her neck, and cried his eyes out in her arms.

Heather didn't flinch, hush him, or apologize to a soul.

Letting her campus clipboard drop straight onto my lap tray so both of her arms were completely free, eighteen-year-old Heather wrapped her arms fiercely around Wesley's shaking shoulders, cradled the back of his sandy-haired head against her collarbone, and held him tight with the exact steady, unconditional warmth she had given him every scared night of his life—while Katie and I immediately rolled our two black SwiftChairs right in beside Wesley's elbow to shield him from the corridor.

"Prospective students sometimes get emotional seeing the medical unit door," the tour guide started awkwardly, shifting her binder against her hip, "before we walk outside to see the dining hall and—"

"Excuse me, ma'am," Heather said softly, her voice completely polite, level, and immovable as she rubbed a slow, soothing circle across Wesley's back and wiped a tear gently from his cheek with her thumb. Looking straight over Wesley's shoulder into the tour guide's eyes, Heather shook her head once. "Thank you very much for checking us in today, truly—except we won't be continuing the rest of the tour. This is Wesley making his decision, and the answer is no."

We never took a single step past the administration corridor. Handing all four peel-and-stick visitor badges politely back across the Bates-Harding reception counter two minutes later, we rolled straight back out the front ramp into the warm mountain sunshine—where Heather sat sideways on the middle van bench holding Wesley close with a chilled bottle of apple juice until every last tremor left his shoulders, Wesley drew a thick black marker line straight through Stop Eight for himself from corner to corner, and Heather merged south onto Interstate 81 toward the New River Valley.

## Sunday, July 27, 2003, 2:15 PM — Stop Nine in Blacksburg: Falling in Love With the Valley & Watching Our Wheelchair Battery Bars on CPI's Hills

"Okay, Katie... look at those mountains all the way around the sky," I breathed out loud two hours further southwest down the valley, braking my SwiftChair for five seconds along the stone overlook path above the sweeping central oval of Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute in Blacksburg.

After that fluorescent administration hallway back in Fishersville, rolling out into the high two-thousand-foot mountain bowl between the Blue Ridge and the Alleghenies felt like stepping into a postcard. Under a crystal-clear cobalt July sky—ten degrees cooler and drier than Tidewater without a single Open House megaphone anywhere on the grass—the vast emerald expanse of Commonwealth Green stretched out below us, ringed on every side by stately Collegiate Gothic halls built from rough-quarried gray-and-buff limestone blocks the campus map called Ironstone. Crisp mountain air drifted down off the green summer ridges framing the horizon; two golden retrievers trotted happily after a tennis ball across the lower lawn; and sixteen-year-old Wesley—his eyes dry and clear again, munching a cinnamon-sugar pretzel twist beside my right armrest—let out a calm, appreciative two-note hum at the wide-open quiet view.

Honestly, in less than ten minutes, I fell head over heels in love with that valley. Everything about the clean mountain air, the rugged stone arches of Keystone Bridge spanning Commonwealth Mall, and the quiet engineering pride etched into Preston Hall felt like someplace a computer nerd could stare at out a library window for four straight years and never get tired of the view.

Then the four of us turned off the lower oval path to climb the long, continuous paved campus grade rising out of the bowl of Commonwealth Green up the hill toward Kincaid Hall.

Thirty yards up the incline, the pitch of my dual twenty-four-volt SwiftChair drive motors deepened into a steady, straining hum beneath my seat pan. Halfway up the hillside walkway—still a hundred yards short of the Ironstone terrace outside Kincaid Hall's first-floor glass atrium—Katie glanced down at her right-armrest controller pod at the exact second I glanced down at mine.

Both of us had unplugged our chairs at one hundred percent full green charge inside our Harrisonburg hotel room at eight-thirty that morning, and we had barely rolled five flat minutes inside the lobby back in Fishersville. Yet halfway up a single Blacksburg campus hill, the five-segment LED battery gauge beside my joystick flickered from five green bars down to four—and forty yards higher, as the stone path steepened again toward the upper academic buildings, the fourth bar blinked amber and dropped to three.

Reaching the level terrazzo patio outside Kincaid Hall's automatic glass doors, Katie and I parked hub to hub to let our warm motor housings cool while looking back down the long, steep elevation drop crisscrossing every side of the central green.

"You watching your battery meter too, Nate?" Katie asked quietly, lacing her right fingers through my left hand as Heather and Wesley stepped up beside us.

"Dropped two whole bars on one climb in warm July weather," I admitted ruefully, looking out over the gorgeous green mountain ridges with a reluctant tug in my chest before glancing down at my controller pod. "Imagine zig-zagging up and down that bowl four times a day between computer-science labs, Pendleton Library, and Kincaid Commons—especially in January when it's eighteen degrees up here in the mountains and freezing cold cuts lead-acid battery range by another thirty percent. Even with brand-new deep-cycle batteries, these hills would wear our wheelchair batteries out way too fast."

"And one more big stop still left on the clipboard next weekend," Heather smiled gently, resting an understanding hand on my shoulder as sixteen-year-old Wesley tapped the tenth box at the bottom of the sheet with a grin: flying up to Minneapolis–Saint Paul so Katie and I could meet Heather's parents and extended Minnesota family for the first time—and give North Star State University a fair look of its own.
