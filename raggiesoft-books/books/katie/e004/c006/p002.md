## Saturday, October 9, 2004, 1:30 PM — A First Daytime Visit Across the Water & Why I Come Along as Chaperone

"You're sure you don't mind driving all the way down I-64 to pick both of us up next Saturday morning and bringing us back to Norfolk before dark, Brandon?" Katie asked brightly inside Apartment A6 on Greenway Court, easing her compact SwiftChair back six inches from our workstation desk while our forty-six-year-old dad tightened a loose wheel bolt on our rolling microwave cart.

Standing near the rear window in a neatly pressed button-down shirt with his hands tucked politely in his khaki pockets, twenty-two-year-old Brandon Mercer flashed the same smooth, patient smile he had worn during every chaperoned café lunch in Ghent since May.

"Wouldn't be any trouble at all, Katie," Brandon replied easily, glancing warmly from Katie to me and Dad. "My side-ramp van rental has four-point floor tracks for both chairs, my rented house up in Williamsburg is completely single-story just two blocks off North Boundary Street by the train depot, and I'll have both of you back across the bridge-tunnel before supper."

![Inside Apartment A6 at Greenway Court in Norfolk in autumn 2004, nineteen-year-old fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands across their armrests while their father David Prairie checks over their supplies with Brandon Mercer standing politely near the window.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/nov_2004_greenway_court_apt_a6_williamsburg_move_out_packing.jpg)

Sitting in my SwiftChair beside Katie with my fingers laced warmly through hers across our inner armrests, I watched how happy my twin sister looked at finally having a polite guy invite her over for an afternoon date of her own. Ever since Heather and I had carefully rebuilt our trust after graduation—spending almost every weekend together with Katie and seventeen-year-old Wesley between CCHR classes and Northwood High—Katie had cheered for Heather and me wholeheartedly, yet I knew part of her had quietly hoped she might meet someone kind too.

Still, because next Saturday would be Katie's very first time setting foot inside Brandon's place forty-five miles across the harbor—and because Heather was already scheduled to help Aunt Linda take Wesley to his Saturday morning occupational-therapy checkup in Virginia Beach—Katie and I hadn't hesitated for a second on our ground rule: *It's strictly an afternoon day visit, and Nate comes right along the whole time as chaperone.*

To Brandon's credit on the surface, he hadn't blinked once when Katie told him I was coming along.

## Saturday, October 9, 2004, 2:15 PM — Two Laminated Calling Cards Behind Our State IDs

"Brandon, do me a favor and check whether my white toolbox is still sitting on the tailgate of my pickup out front before somebody walks off with a socket wrench," Dad said casually, gesturing toward the hallway door.

"Right away, Mr. Prairie," Brandon nodded, stepping briskly out into the first-floor corridor.

As soon as Brandon's footsteps faded past the lobby mailboxes, Dad set his wrench down on the workstation desk, reached into his flannel shirt pocket, and pulled out two crisp, laminated blue-and-white prepaid telephone calling cards. Pulling up our wooden desk chair so he sat at eye level right in front of Katie's wheelchair and mine, Dad handed one card to Katie and one card to me.

"Before the two of you ride forty-five miles up Interstate 64 next Saturday afternoon, I want both of you to tuck these right behind your Virginia State ID cards in your wallets," Dad said quietly, tapping the toll-free 1-800 access number and the twelve-digit personal identification number printed under the scratch-off silver strip he had already cleared for us on the back of each card. "Each card has five hundred prepaid minutes preloaded on it, and they never expire."

"Even though we both carry our flip phones, Dad?" Katie asked, turning the glossy plastic card over in her fingers.

"Cell towers drop out along the York River and inside brick station walls, batteries die, and Williamsburg is across the water in another area rate zone where neither of you carries rolls of quarters," Dad said, looking steadily from Katie's eyes to mine with calm fatherly instinct. "If you're ever at a bus stop, a store, or a train depot and need to reach me on Arvin Road—or reach Heather and Aunt Linda—you don't need a single coin. You pick up any payphone receiver, dial that 1-800 number for free, punch in your twelve-digit code, and call home. And keep a fresh, unactivated thirty-day CVTA bus pass in your wallets alongside your twenty-four-volt chargers in your backpacks whenever you travel across the bridge-tunnel, even for a four-hour lunch visit."

"Got it memorized already, Dad," I promised, unzipping my wallet, sliding out my vertical Under-21 Virginia State ID card, and slipping the prepaid calling card snugly into the clear plastic window right behind it alongside my spare unactivated thirty-day CVTA magnetic pass and two crisp one-dollar bills, while Katie did the exact same thing with hers.

"Brandon's been polite every time I've met him, kids, and I'm glad you're chaperoning your sister, Nate," Dad said warmly, resting one large, calloused hand over both of our joined hands between our armrests before Brandon walked back down the hall. "Just remember: a good afternoon date ends when *you* say it ends, and if anything forty-five miles from home ever feels sideways for half a second, you call me and Heather and come straight back across the water."
