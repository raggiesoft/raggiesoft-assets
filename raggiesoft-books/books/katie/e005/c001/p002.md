## Saturday, October 16, 2004, 3:35 PM — The Blind Ticket Window at Boundary Street & A Leap of Faith Toward Newport News

"Both of you wait right out here by the bus island and don't crowd me inside the waiting room," Brandon snapped at three-forty-eight on Saturday afternoon, hitching the strap of his overstuffed black canvas duffel bag higher onto his shoulder outside the brick colonial depot of the Williamsburg Transportation Center on North Boundary Street. "The ticket line inside is cramped enough without two wheelchairs blocking the queue, and I need to get the passes sorted out at the counter before the shift change."

Sitting wheel-to-wheel with Nate on the red-brick plaza thirty feet outside the heavy brass-and-glass entrance doors under a crisp, windy October sky, I kept my hands folded calmly over my canvas lap bag to hide my bruised left wrist and didn't argue a word.

"Stay right here," Brandon repeated, glancing down at our chairs as if two nineteen-year-olds without a car or cell phones were bolted to the bricks. "I'll be out in five minutes."

He turned, pushed through the heavy wooden-and-glass doors into the depot, and disappeared inside.

One quick glance through the colonial window panes as we had rolled up from North Boundary Street had already shown Nate and me the exact layout of that historic brick station house: the indoor ARTS ticket counter faced inward across the enclosed waiting room, with solid brick walls and zero line of sight out onto the local bus bays outside where we were sitting. Neither Brandon nor the ticket agent behind the counter could see a thing happening at the curb while the dot-matrix ticket printer chugged through paper stock.

Three seconds after the station doors swung shut behind Brandon's back, air brakes hissed loudly along the curved concrete bus island right in front of our footrests.

A white-and-colonial-blue transit coach with Colonial Triangle Transit Authority painted along its side pulled into the bay, its amber front destination sign illuminating in crisp block letters: <led>GREY LINE | NEWPORT NEWS / LEE HALL</led>.

Nate and I turned our heads toward each other at the exact same instant.

Neither of us knew the Williamsburg or Peninsula bus networks at all—on Wesley's college tour the summer before, Heather had driven Aunt Linda's minivan across the tunnel, and the only Peninsula bus we had ever spotted in our lives was that single `112 NNTC` coach at the Warwick University roundabout. Neither of us had the faintest idea whether it was even possible to string city transit buses together all the way from Williamsburg back across the harbor before nightfall. All we knew from basic Tidewater geography was that Newport News lay southeast straight down the Peninsula toward the water—away from Brandon, away from Canada, and closer to home.

"Newport News goes down the Peninsula," Nate said quietly, his blue eyes locking onto mine without a flicker of hesitation. "Now or never, Katie."

"Let's go," I breathed.

Both of us pushed our right-hand joysticks forward in unison. Spotting two compact black powerchairs rolling straight up to the yellow curb strip, the CTTA Grey Line operator immediately flipped his suspension switch; the front of the bus knelt with a pneumatic sigh, and the aluminum ramp unfolded cleanly onto the brick apron.

Nate rolled up the diamond-plate incline first, and I drove right up behind him thirty seconds later. On Colonial Triangle Transit, the Person with a Disability fare was seventy-five cents: before backing into our respective forward securement bays, Nate and I each unzipped our backpack pockets, fed a crisp one-dollar bill into the farebox, plucked out the printed Change Card loaded with twenty-five cents in CTTA bus fare that the machine issued back to each of us, and let the operator swiftly hook all eight four-point floor tie-down straps onto our frames without looking back once toward the blind brick wall of the station house.

At four-oh-one sharp, the ramp folded upright, the dual doors hissed shut, and the Grey Line pulled out onto North Boundary Street and headed southeast onto Route 60—Pocahontas Trail—toward Newport News, leaving Brandon Mercer standing at the indoor ticket counter behind us.

## Saturday, October 16, 2004, 4:30 PM — Elmhurst Street at Lee Hall, Crown Burger at Warwick & Denbigh, & Calling Heather and Dad

Once the brick walls of Colonial Williamsburg gave way to tall pines and the long four-lane stretch of Pocahontas Trail past Brewster Gardens, the adrenaline humming in my ears gave way to pure timetable problem-solving.

"Excuse me, sir," Nate called forward politely from his securement bay as the Grey Line slowed toward the Newport News city line at Lee Hall. "My sister and I know the CVTA routes over in Norfolk and Virginia Beach, so we've never ridden down the Peninsula before. How do we connect from Lee Hall down toward Downtown Newport News and Norfolk?"

"Tell you the truth, I couldn't tell you how to string Coastal Virginia's routes together down the Peninsula," the CTTA operator called back honestly, glancing into his wide interior mirror. "Most of us Colonial Triangle drivers only know that Elmhurst Street along the side of the shopping center here at Lee Hall is where we meet up with the CVTA bus—I think that 116 bus goes down to the mall, and their driver should have Peninsula pocket schedules up front. Only catch here on Elmhurst Street is that Colonial Triangle and Coastal Virginia run on two completely separate radio systems, so I can't call directly over my radio to let that CVTA operator know I've got two wheelchairs unloading."

Before my stomach could even tighten at the thought of watching a timed transfer pull away while eight floor straps were being unhooked, two riders sitting just ahead of us behind the front wheel-well—a woman in navy hospital scrubs and a shipyard apprentice carrying a canvas tool bag—turned around in their seats with warm, reassuring smiles.

"Don't worry about a thing," the woman in scrubs told us right away. "Both of us are transferring to that 116 bus too. Here at Lee Hall the buses just line up one behind the other right along Elmhurst Street—soon as the front doors open, we'll walk straight up to the CVTA bus and let the driver know there are two wheelchairs coming right behind us."

"We'll make sure he holds for y'all," the shipyard apprentice promised with a friendly nod.

"Thank you both so much!" I called out, reaching across the aisle between our two securement bays to squeeze Nate's fingers as the Grey Line turned off Pocahontas Trail onto Elmhurst Street along the side of the Lee Hall strip shopping center—pulling in directly behind an idling blue-and-silver Coastal Virginia Transit Authority bus signed for Route 116.

The very instant Nate and I saw that familiar CVTA emblem on the bus waiting ahead of us on Elmhurst Street, both of us let out the exact same quiet breath of relief. Throughout our entire time living in the CVTA service district, Nate and I had always carried CVTA thirty-day passes—magnetic passes valid for thirty consecutive days starting from the day of first usage in a farebox rather than thirty days from the date of purchase. Having tucked two fresh, unactivated spares behind Dad's calling cards in our wallets last Saturday meant we were completely good on fare all the way home.

Because Elmhurst Street had only a metal signpost and a ribbon-thin sidewalk squeezed against the grass strip, both our CTTA operator and the waiting CVTA operator purposely stopped several feet out from the curb so their front ramps folded flat onto the level asphalt pavement with plenty of turning room. Rolling smoothly aboard the waiting CVTA 116 after thanking the two riders who had held the bus, we swiped our thirty-day passes for their first day of use with two crisp confirmation beeps, backed into the forward bays while the operator clipped all eight straps tight, and exhaled as the driver keyed his coiled radio handset:

"11601 to Dispatch."

*"Dispatch, go ahead 11601."*

"Dispatch, I've picked up two 10-16s at Lee Hall."

*"10-4."*

Pulling folded paper pocket schedules from the front rack as the 116 rolled south down Warwick Boulevard past Fort Eustis, Nate and I scanned the columns together in thirty seconds flat: skipping the razor-thin `106` connection at Warwick and Denbigh in favor of **`Route 107`** forty-five minutes later gave us time to duck completely out of sight inside the corner **Crown Burger** for cheeseburgers, salty fries, and ginger ale while still delivering us into **Newport News Transit Center (`NNTC`)** at `6:15 PM`—a rock-solid thirty minutes ahead of the final `6:45 PM` cross-water **Expressways `Route 61`**!

True to the printed timetable, southbound `Route 107` (`"10702 to Dispatch... picked up two 10-16s at Warwick and Denbigh"`) rolled us cleanly into `NNTC` at six-fifteen sharp.

Before rolling over to Bay Four, Nate pulled out Dad's laminated blue-and-white 1-800 calling card at the lowered stainless-steel ADA payphone mounted on the brick pillar. First we called **Dad** on Arvin Road—hearing our father's kitchen chair scrape back on the linoleum as we told him Brandon had bruised my wrist, stolen our cell phones, and tried to force me onto a train toward Canada, and that we were boarding the `6:45 PM` Expressways `Route 61` arriving on Monticello Avenue in Downtown Norfolk at `7:12 PM`, transferring to the `7:20 PM` outbound `Route 20` to Glenrock Mall and the `7:45 PM` outbound `Route 15` arriving at Military Highway and Indian River Road at `8:01 PM`.

*"Stay right on that 61 bus so we don't pass each other inside the tunnel tubes,"* Dad told us immediately. *"I will be standing right under the streetlight at Military Highway and Indian River Road at seven-fifty waiting for your 15."*

Without hanging up the calling-card session, Nate pressed the pound key to place one second toll-free call—straight to **Heather's** cell phone down in Virginia Beach.

Heather answered on the very first ring.

*"Hello?"*

"Heather, it's Nate and Katie calling from a payphone at Newport News Transit Center," Nate said quickly, holding the armored receiver between our ears. "Brandon took our cell phones up in Williamsburg and tried to drag Katie onto a train to Canada—we escaped onto the buses and we're boarding the six-forty-five Expressways Route 61 across the bridge-tunnel right now. It lets off on Monticello Avenue in Downtown Norfolk at seven-twelve!"

Over the phone line, we heard seventeen-year-old Wesley let out an urgent, protective two-syllable vocalization right beside Heather's elbow as Heather already grabbed her keys and jacket off Aunt Linda's hall hook.

*"Aunt Linda's minivan is up on the lift getting brakes done until Monday, and the inbound bus stop is right at our corner!"* Heather answered without a half-second of hesitation, her voice fierce and crystal-steady. *"Wes and I have our bus passes in our pockets right now—we're hopping on the inbound bus into Downtown Norfolk this minute, and Wes and I will be standing right at the Monticello Avenue stop waiting when your 61 opens its doors at seven-twelve! Stay right together—we're coming to meet you!"*

## Saturday, October 16, 2004, 6:45 PM – 8:01 PM — Meeting Heather and Wesley at Monticello Avenue, Open Radio on Route 20, & Riding Home as Four

At six-forty-five on the dot—after logging `"two 10-16s at Newport News Transit Center"` over the radio—our fourth bus of the afternoon, high-backed Expressways <led>61 | DOWNTOWN NORFOLK</led>, pulled onto Interstate 64 East and plunged between the gleaming white ceramic tiles of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel beneath sixty feet of Chesapeake Bay water.

When the 61 glided up to the bright curb on Monticello Avenue in Downtown Norfolk at seven-twelve sharp and the front doors hissed open, I looked down the aluminum ramp—and a hot rush of relief stung right behind my eyelids.

Standing three feet back from the yellow curb strip under the downtown streetlamp—cheeks flushed from sprinting the transfer Plaza after riding inbound from Kempsville—stood nineteen-year-old Heather with seventeen-year-old Wesley right beside her shoulder, clutching his smooth blue silicone ring against his navy windbreaker and scanning every window of the bus for us.

The second our front casters cleared the bottom of the ramp, Heather dropped to her knees right between our two SwiftChairs—careful not to bump my bruised left wrist—and wrapped Nate and me together in a fierce, warm hug while Wesley stepped right in beside Nate's right armrest, let out a huge, shaky two-note hum of pure relief, and tapped the back of Nate's knuckles twice and then mine once to make sure for himself that both of us were whole.

"Nobody touches either of you like that—ever," Heather whispered fiercely against Nate's collar before looking at my wrist with blazing hazel eyes and slipping her right hand securely into Nate's while Wesley stood sentinel beside my left wheel for the eight-minute buffer. "You two aren't riding another block alone tonight. All four of us are riding the 20 and the 15 home to your dad's house together."

At seven-eighteen, our fifth bus—outbound CVTA <led>20 | VA BEACH | via GLENROCK MALL</led>—knelt at Monticello Avenue. Katie and I rolled into the two forward wheelchair bays while Heather and Wesley dropped their fares and sat right across the front priority bench holding onto our hands across the narrow aisle. Knowing our zero-buffer `7:45 PM` pulse connection at Glenrock Mall had zero margin for unloading two powerchairs, our veteran Route 20 operator unhooked his open-channel analog radio handset right before turning off Virginia Beach Boulevard into Glenrock Mall:

"Outbound 20 to the outbound 15 at Glenrock Mall, I have two 10-16s—plus two—for you."

*"10-4. Are they heading towards Emerald Creek Mall?"* the waiting Route 15 operator crackled back through the overhead speaker.

"Outbound toward Chesapeake, sir—all four of us are heading towards Indian River Road!" Nate called forward clearly, and the driver relayed `"10-4, heading to Indian River Road"` right as we swung behind the waiting outbound **Route 15** with its ramp already resting on the concrete island!

Rolling aboard our sixth and final bus together—Nate and me locked into the wheelchair bays, Heather's steady hand resting over Nate's forearm, and Wesley sitting right beside my elbow turning his blue ring in calm contentment—we crossed the Elizabeth River bridge into Virginia Beach at eight o'clock sharp.

There, standing beneath the amber glow of the corner streetlight at Military Highway and Indian River Road in his navy windbreaker, stood our dad—dropping to one knee as the ramp touched down at `8:01 PM` to pull Katie and me into his arms while thanking Heather and Wesley with shining eyes for riding out to bring us the rest of the way home.
