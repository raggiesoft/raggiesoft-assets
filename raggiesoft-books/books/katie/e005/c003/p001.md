## Thursday, April 14, 2008, 1:30 PM — The Binders at Eighteenth Street & The "Good Enough" Mentality

"Look at the open three-ring binder on the corner of the staff table, Nate," Katie whispered, leaning slightly across her SwiftChair armrest toward mine as we parked our wheelchairs at the end of the front visitor row inside the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority boardroom on Eighteenth Street in Norfolk. "That's our Tuesday post. They printed the whole thing out and highlighted the timetable chart in yellow."

I followed my sister's gaze across the polished laminate table where CVTA's senior operations staff and the appointed city council commissioners from around Hampton Roads were shuffling their agenda packets before the monthly Transportation District Commission meeting gaveled in.

Sure enough, clipped inside the deputy planning director's black binder—and photocopied into three separate packets along the commissioners' dais—were six pages printed directly from Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit, complete with the coastal-navy header and the custom PHP route-comparison tables I had coded the week before.

Two and a half years had passed since that quiet Sunday night in November 2005 when Katie and I had first launched our blog on BlogSphere from our dining-table desk at Sea Breeze West Apartment 3. In that time, our lives had moved forward together step by step. We had ridden CVTA Route 1 and Route 29 side by side through rain, summer heat, and winter wind to finish our associate degrees at the Community College of Hampton Roads. When our West Ocean View lease ended in March 2007 and Dad took his new systems engineering position up in Tysons Corner, the three of us had moved our permanent family home base up to Dad's two-story brick house on Sunset Drive in Alexandria while Katie and I spent twelve intense months living in the accessible dorms at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville, completing the Web Applications Developer program and mastering PHP and relational database architecture. And just last month, in March 2008, after graduating from Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center at age twenty-two, Katie and I had returned home to Hampton Roads, pooled our earnings, and bought our own accessible single-story home fifty-fifty together in the Kempsville section of Virginia Beach.

And through every single one of those hundred and twenty-four weeks, we had never missed a post on Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit.

What had started as two nineteen-year-old twins quietly jotting down what they wished their regional bus system could be had grown into the first independent transit watchdog Hampton Roads had ever seen. After we rebuilt the site in custom PHP during our final semester at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center, I had written a server-log analyzer to see where our weekday traffic was coming from. The results had made both of us sit up at our desks: every weekday morning between eight and ten o'clock, dozens of page views were coming straight from static IP blocks registered to CVTA headquarters in Norfolk, alongside municipal government networks inside the City Halls of Virginia Beach, Norfolk, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Hampton, and Newport News.

They were reading every word we wrote. They just didn't know what to do with two twenty-three-year-old twins in power wheelchairs who refused to pat them on the back for the status quo.

When the commission chair called the brief public-comment period at the start of the one-thirty afternoon meeting—scheduled, as always, in the middle of a Thursday workday when almost no working commuter could attend—Katie released her joystick brake and rolled smoothly up to the low microphone table at the front of the room, with me rolling right up beside her holding our printed schedule audit.

"Mr. Chairman, commissioners, and CVTA staff," Katie said clearly, her voice steady and polite as every head along the horseshoe dais turned toward our two wheelchairs. "My brother Nathaniel and I are the authors of Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit. We ride CVTA buses in our power wheelchairs every week across Virginia Beach, Norfolk, Chesapeake, and the Peninsula, and we pay our regular thirty-day pass fares just like thousands of other citizens who depend on this network."

Down at the staff table, the senior service planner and the communications director exchanged an immediate, tight-lipped glance, recognizing our names at once.

"Over the past two years," Katie continued, resting her hand on the timetable charts across my lap, "we have documented why riders across Hampton Roads are being left behind—not because our region lacks the roads or the riders, but because this agency and our six member cities have settled for schedules that lock working people out. Look at your own printed Route 1 schedule between Downtown Norfolk and Pembroke East: on the Norfolk side of the line, from Downtown Norfolk through Wards Corner and Ocean View to the Amphibious Base, Route 1 runs every fifteen to thirty minutes and runs late into the night until 2:15 AM, seven days a week. The exact moment Route 1 crosses the city line into Virginia Beach toward Pleasure House Road, Haygood, and Pembroke East, most of the buses turn around at the border, service drops to once an hour, only runs during the day with the last bus leaving Pembroke East at 6:22 PM, and has no Sunday service at all. On Route 25 from Glenrock Mall to the CCHR Virginia Beach Campus, buses run every thirty minutes to Newtown Road at the city line, where half of them turn right back around into Norfolk, leaving the Virginia Beach side down Princess Anne Road to CCHR with hourly daytime-only service that ends at 5:53 PM. At the CCHR Virginia Beach Campus hub, the last Route 12 bus back toward Military Highway and Indian River Road leaves at 5:48 PM. If a citizen rides Route 33 to the Virginia Beach Municipal Center to attend a 6:00 PM City Council meeting and needs to catch Route 12, Route 25, or Route 29 at CCHR to get home, they have to leave City Hall at 5:33 PM—twenty-seven minutes before the council meeting even starts. Across the water, the last southbound Route 61 across the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel still leaves Newport News Transit Center at 6:45 PM, and riders transferring on multi-bus routes are still getting penalized with a second fare because the two-hour paper transfer expires while they wait for sixty-minute local buses."

"We aren't here to bash public transit," I added from my chair beside Katie. "We love public transit. We need public transit to live our lives. We're here because CVTA can be so much better than this—with timed evening transfer pulses at CCHR, Glenrock Mall, and Hilltop, service running until at least ten or eleven o'clock at night, clean transit center restrooms, and a modern smart-card fare system that doesn't punish two-bus commuters—if the leaders on this commission have the political willpower to fund and demand it."

The commission chair offered a polite, practiced nod as the three-minute timer beeped. "Thank you both for your comments. Staff will take your blog submissions under advisement."

Twenty minutes later, when the board recessed into the hallway outside the Eighteenth Street conference room, CVTA's senior service planner and one of the veteran municipal council commissioners walked past our wheelchairs near the water fountain. Stopping beside us with a folder tucked under his arm, the planner offered the tired, defensive half-smile of a bureaucrat who had spent fifteen years guarding an unchallenged routine.

"Look, Katie, Nate," the planner said, lowering his voice as the commissioner nodded beside him, "we read your blog every week. You two obviously put a lot of math into those PHP charts of yours. But you have to be realistic about how transit works in a region like Hampton Roads."

"Realistic about what?" Katie asked, looking straight up at him from her SwiftChair.

The commissioner shrugged his shoulders, palms turned upward in the exact gesture that summed up thirty years of regional transit policy.

"Does the bus run?" the commissioner asked rhetorically. "Yes, the bus runs. Every morning the garages roll the fleet out across all six cities. Do people get to CCHR and the malls and the shipyards during the day? Yes. Is it good enough for what our city councils budget every spring? I guess so. Nobody at City Hall is clamoring to spend millions more running buses late into the night or overhauling transfer rules when the buses are already out there running their routes."

Katie and I looked at each other as the planner and the commissioner patted the folder and walked back into the boardroom.

There it was, spoken right out loud in the hallway of Eighteenth Street: the entire institutional philosophy of the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority.

*Does the bus run? Yes. Is it good enough? I guess so.*

For decades, CVTA and the politicians who funded it had hidden behind that exact complacency. As long as a diesel bus rolled past a stop sign once an hour during banker's hours and the annual federal grant paperwork got signed, the agency and the six city councils treated the box as checked. Until Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit came along, nobody with the technical skills to dissect their Transportation Service Plans—and the daily lived reality of riding those buses in power wheelchairs—had ever stood up in public and told them to *do better*.

As we rolled our wheelchairs out the automatic glass doors of the Eighteenth Street headquarters toward the Granby Street bus stop to catch our ride home, Katie's jaw was set with quiet fire.

"Did you hear what that commissioner said, Nate?" Katie asked as we waited on the concrete sidewalk pad. "'Nobody at City Hall is clamoring to spend money or overhaul the routes because it's good enough.' He just admitted the truth. The problem isn't that CVTA can't be improved. Every single improvement we've written about on the blog is completely doable. The only thing missing is the political willpower from the mayors and city councils who control CVTA's purse strings and sit on that board."

"And half of those city halls are up for election this year," I said, the realization clicking into place as the low-floor CVTA bus pulled up to the curb and kneeled its front suspension.

"Every single one of them has elections in 2008," Katie said as the hydraulic ramp folded down onto the concrete and we rolled up into the front wheelchair securement bays. "And as soon as we get home to Kempsville, we are going to put their political willpower to the test in front of every voter in Hampton Roads."

## Mapping the 2008 Regional Mayoral Elections & Issuing the Open Challenge

Back at our dual computer desks in the spare bedroom office of our new Kempsville house that evening, Katie and I pulled up the official 2008 Virginia State Board of Elections candidate filings across all six member cities of the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority: Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Norfolk, Hampton, and Newport News.

Sipping cold ginger ale from our glasses while my PHP editor and our browser tabs glowed side by side across our monitors, we mapped out the exact 2008 municipal calendar across the six cities.

"Look at how the 2008 mayoral calendar breaks down across the six CVTA cities," I said, pointing to the state election schedule on my screen. "Out of the six cities—Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Norfolk, Hampton, and Newport News—four of them are holding direct elections for Mayor here in 2008, while the other two are mid-term on their mayoral cycle."

Katie rolled her chair closer and read down the certified candidate lists with me.

"Norfolk and Newport News both elected their mayors to four-year terms back in May 2006, so neither Norfolk nor Newport News has a Mayor's race until May 2010—though both cities have City Council seats on the ballot next month on Tuesday, May 6, 2008," Katie noted. "Then, on that same Tuesday, May 6, 2008 spring municipal ballot, three CVTA cities are electing their Mayors: Chesapeake has an open-seat Mayor's race, Portsmouth has a contested Mayor's race, and Hampton has an open-seat Mayor's race across the water."

"And then our own city—Virginia Beach—moved its municipal elections to the fall general election ballot on Tuesday, November 4, 2008," I said, scrolling down to the Virginia Beach filings. "And look at the Virginia Beach Mayor's race: Mayor Myra Overton is running for another term after twenty years as Mayor, and she's facing three challengers in a four-way race for Mayor of Virginia Beach, right alongside the at-large and district City Council races."

Katie looked at Mayor Myra E. Overton's name on the screen and shook her head thoughtfully.

Every lifelong resident of Virginia Beach knew the paradox of Mayor Myra Overton. On a personal level, she was a genuinely sweet, gracious, grandmotherly lady who had gotten her start in civic and political life serving on the city's public library board—a lifelong passion for books and literacy that eventually led the city to rename the Central Library on Virginia Beach Boulevard in her honor. If you met her at a ribbon-cutting or a neighborhood library reading, she couldn't have been nicer.

As a municipal politician overseeing transportation for the largest city in Virginia, however, her twenty-year tenure had been a disaster for anyone who rode a bus. Among daily CVTA riders, bus operators, and local political watchers, there was a famous story that had circulated for years to explain why Virginia Beach's bus service hours were so abysmal compared to Norfolk's. There was no way to confirm she had ever actually said the words out loud, of course, but as the local legend went, if Mayor Overton had her way, the Route 20 bus on Virginia Beach Boulevard would depart Downtown Norfolk, roll east down the Boulevard to Newtown Road at the city line, and turn right back around and stay in Norfolk—to keep the riff-raff out, of course.

Whether that story was literal truth or courthouse folklore, one glance at the printed Route 1 and Route 25 timetables—where frequent, late-night Norfolk buses hit the Virginia Beach border and suddenly shriveled into once-an-hour daytime-only runs that vanished after dinnertime—made it painfully obvious why everyone in Hampton Roads believed it.

Katie leaned forward, resting her forearms on the desk.

"Four mayoral elections across Hampton Roads in 2008—Chesapeake, Portsmouth, and Hampton in May, and Virginia Beach in November—plus City Council races in all six CVTA cities," Katie said. "Every single one of those candidates puts glossy photos on their campaign flyers claiming they care about regional transportation, traffic relief, working families, and citizens with disabilities. Let's see how many of them are actually willing to step out from behind a podium and ride the bus with the two of us."

Opening the PHP administrative dashboard for Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit, Katie and I drafted our landmark open letter together, carefully checking every word before publishing it to the top of the front page and sending personalized copies directly to the official campaign email addresses of every certified candidate for Mayor in Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, and Hampton—as well as the City Council candidates across all six CVTA cities:

> Thursday, April 14, 2008 — An Open Invitation to the 2008 Mayoral & City Council Candidates of Hampton Roads: Come Ride the Bus With Us<br><br>
> Whenever we speak with staff and commissioners at the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority about late-evening bus curfews, ninety-minute routes, expired two-hour paper transfers, and missing sidewalk curb cuts, we hear the same unspoken excuse: Does the bus run? Yes. Is it good enough? I guess so.<br><br>
> For the thousands of citizens across Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Norfolk, Hampton, and Newport News who rely on CVTA buses every single day to get to college, to work, to medical appointments, and to civic meetings—including wheelchair riders like the two of us—"good enough" is not good enough. CVTA can be improved into a first-class regional transit network, and the only ingredient missing is the political willpower of the elected mayors and city councils who fund and govern it.<br><br>
> This year, voters in four of our six CVTA cities—Chesapeake, Portsmouth, and Hampton on May 6, and Virginia Beach on November 4—will elect their next Mayor, while voters across all six cities will elect members of their City Councils. Therefore, Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit is issuing a simple, non-partisan public invitation to every candidate for Mayor in Virginia Beach, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, and Hampton, and every candidate for City Council across the region:<br><br>
> Meet the two of us at a CVTA transit hub, leave your campaign car in the parking lot, pay the regular bus fare alongside us, and ride a real multi-bus loop on CVTA with two daily wheelchair riders. No staged press vans, no special charter buses, and no shortcuts—just the real schedules, the real transfers, and an honest conversation on camera about how we can give Hampton Roads the transit system our citizens deserve.

When I clicked Publish and dispatched the invitation emails across all four mayoral races and six cities, the comment section on Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit lit up within twenty minutes. Daily bus commuters from Ocean View to Kempsville, CCHR students, shipyard workers in Newport News and Portsmouth, and even readers who had followed us over from the Seattle Transit Blog cheered the challenge on and began forwarding the link to local newspaper reporters and campaign managers.

Then we waited to see which politicians actually had the courage to show up.

## Silence Across the Region — And the One Candidate Who Said Yes

Days turned into weeks as the May 6, 2008 municipal elections in Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Hampton, Norfolk, and Newport News approached and passed.

Every morning, Katie and I sat side by side at our desks in Kempsville with our coffee and ginger ale, checking our campaign inbox and phone messages.

From the mayoral campaigns in Chesapeake: silence.
From the mayoral campaigns in Portsmouth: silence.
From the mayoral campaigns in Hampton: a single three-sentence form email from a campaign aide thanking us for our "interest in regional infrastructure" and stating that the candidate's schedule did not permit a multi-hour bus ride, followed by silence.
From the incumbent City Council members and establishment candidates across Norfolk, Newport News, Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Hampton, and Virginia Beach: not a single acceptance.

Even here in Virginia Beach, as the four-way November 2008 Mayor's race heated up through the summer, the twenty-year incumbent mayor's campaign ignored our invitation completely. The wealthy banking-executive frontrunner backed by the courthouse establishment didn't reply. The veteran budget-hawk former councilman didn't accept either. And every single incumbent and establishment candidate running for the Virginia Beach City Council pretended our email didn't exist.

"They really don't care, do they, Nate?" Katie said quietly one warm afternoon at our desks, staring at the spreadsheet on my monitor where almost every single row across Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Hampton, Norfolk, Newport News, and Virginia Beach still read *No Response*. "They want the title of Mayor or City Council member, and they want to sit on regional boards and cut ribbons on highway interchanges, but not one of them is willing to spend two and a half hours riding the bus system they fund."

Just as I reached for the mouse to update our public tracker post on the blog, my email client chimed with a new incoming message.

I blinked at the sender line on the screen, then clicked it open.

"Katie," I said, sitting forward in my SwiftChair. "Look at the inbox. Someone just replied."

Katie rolled her chair right up against mine and leaned in toward the monitor.

The email wasn't from a campaign press secretary or an automated mailing list. It came directly from the fourth candidate on the ballot for Mayor of the City of Virginia Beach—Scott Tanner, a young, thirty-something political-outsider Navy veteran who wasn't part of the entrenched City Hall establishment and didn't have the backing of the big beachfront real-estate donors.

His message was four sentences long:

> From: Scott Tanner for Mayor Campaign<br>
> To: Katie & Nate — Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit<br>
> Subject: Re: Open Invitation to 2008 Mayoral Candidates — Come Ride the Bus With Us<br><br>
> Katie and Nate,<br>
> I read your blog post and your route analyses, and you're right—nobody asking to lead the largest city in Virginia should be afraid to ride the bus system our citizens depend on every day. I accept your challenge. Tell me which transit hub in Virginia Beach to meet you at, what time to be there, and which buses we're riding, and I'll leave my car in the lot and ride the whole loop with the two of you.<br>
> — Scott

Katie stared at the screen for three seconds, and then a slow, fierce grin broke across her face as she reached over and gripped my hand.

"Out of every mayoral candidate in Chesapeake, Portsmouth, Hampton, and Virginia Beach—and every City Council candidate across all six cities—only one person had the guts to say yes," Katie said, her blue eyes shining with determination as she pulled the folded CVTA Virginia Beach route map across our desk. "And he's running for Mayor right here in Virginia Beach. Come on, Nate—get your video camera batteries on the charger. We're going to meet him at the CCHR Virginia Beach Campus bus loop, and we're going to take him on the exact four-bus circuit across Virginia Beach and Norfolk: Route 29 to Hilltop, Route 20 to Glenrock Mall, Route 15 down Military Highway, and Route 12 right back to CCHR."
