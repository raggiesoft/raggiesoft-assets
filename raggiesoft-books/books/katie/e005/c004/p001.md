## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 8:00 AM — Subcommittee Room 4 & The Printed Text of House Bill 663

"Look at Line 18 on Page 1, Nate," my twin sister Katie murmured, tapping her highlighter against the printed bill text resting on her lap tray as she backed her compact SwiftChair into the modified wheelchair bay at the far left end of the House General Laws Subcommittee #4 dais. "'Anatomical sex means the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy.' And then look at Line 54 on Page 2: 'Any person who willfully and knowingly violates this section shall be liable for a civil penalty of not more than fifty dollars,' enforced by a law enforcement officer's summons."

Parked in my matching SwiftChair three feet to Katie's left—right at the edge of the committee rail where I had my laptop open and my compact digital video capture box plugged into the subcommittee room's public audio-distribution jack—I shook my head at the two-page printout of House Bill 663.

It was eight o'clock on a frosty Thursday morning in Richmond during the 2016 session of the Virginia General Assembly. Outside on Bank Street, commuter breath hung in white clouds above the brick sidewalks of Capitol Square, while down here in the cramped, low-ceilinged subcommittee room of the old General Assembly Building, the fluorescent tubes hummed over dark wood-veneer walls and rows of folding metal chairs packed shoulder-to-shoulder with anxious parents, college students from Virginia Dominion University and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute, teachers, and civil-rights advocates who had driven through the pre-dawn dark just to get three minutes at the public microphone.

Nobody in the Capitol press corps expected a major national story to come out of General Laws Subcommittee #4 at eight o'clock on a Thursday morning. In fact, there wasn't a single television network camera tripod in the room—only the single fixed, wide-angle legislative webcast camera mounted high on the back drywall and my little portable capture encoder sitting beside my laptop keyboard. In the Virginia House of Delegates, thirty-year-old second-term Delegate Katie Prairie of the Eighty-Fifth House District in Virginia Beach sat in a thirty-four-seat Democratic minority facing a sixty-six-seat Republican supermajority. Most mornings, contentious social bills like House Bill 663 were quietly rubber-stamped in an early-morning subcommittee before most Virginians had finished their first cup of coffee.

Katie hadn't come down to Subcommittee #4 planning a viral soundbite or a theatrical stunt. She had stayed up until eleven o'clock the night before in our accessible winter-session apartment off Capitol Square reading the committee docket with me over ginger ale, line by statutory line, the exact same way we audited PHP code or Coastal Virginia Transit Authority timetables.

At 8:05 AM, the subcommittee chairman rapped his wooden gavel and called up House Bill 663.

Stepping up to the witness lectern in a dark navy suit with a thick leather folder under his arm, the bill's chief patron, Delegate Martin Cobb—flanked in the front row by his longtime ideological ally, Delegate Robert Marsh—adjusted the gooseneck microphone and launched into his prepared pitch.

"Mr. Chairman and members of the subcommittee," Delegate Cobb began in a solemn, practiced cadence, "House Bill 663 is a simple, common-sense measure to protect privacy and public safety across the Commonwealth of Virginia. This legislation directs the Department of General Services and every local school board and state institution of higher education to ensure that every single-gender public restroom, locker room, and shower facility in any building owned, leased, or controlled by the Commonwealth is used exclusively by individuals whose anatomical sex matches the designated gender on the door—backed by a fifty-dollar civil penalty enforceable by law enforcement summons."

Across the horseshoe table, several majority members gave slow, automatic nods, ready to move the bill along before the eight-thirty bell.

At the accessible end of the dais, Katie raised her hand and leaned forward in her SwiftChair.

"Mr. Chairman?" Katie called out, her voice crisp and steady across the room.

The subcommittee chairman glanced down the table with a faint grimace, knowing he couldn't deny a seated committee member the floor. "The Chair recognizes the gentlewoman from Virginia Beach, Delegate Prairie."

Katie flipped the toggle on her desk microphone so the green indicator ring lit up, lifted the two printed pages of House Bill 663 in her right hand, and looked straight across the green felt table at Delegate Martin Cobb.

"Thank you, Mr. Chairman," Katie said, her voice ringing clearly through the ceiling speakers. "Delegate Cobb, I have read every word of House Bill 663 three times since last night, and I have a very direct operational question for you about how the Commonwealth of Virginia is actually supposed to enforce what you wrote on Page 1 and Page 2 of this bill."

Delegate Cobb stiffened slightly behind the lectern. "The language of the bill speaks for itself, Delegate Prairie. Restrooms in state buildings, public schools, and state colleges will be restricted strictly by anatomical sex, and law enforcement officers are authorized to issue a fifty-dollar civil summons to anyone who enters a restroom that does not match their anatomical sex."

Katie held the printed bill higher so everyone in the packed room could see the numbered lines.

"I can read the text, Delegate Cobb—my brother and I write code for a living, and we pay very close attention to definitions," Katie shot back, the quiet intensity in her voice making every reporter and legislative aide in the back row look up from their notebooks. "Unlike birth-certificate bills, your Line 18 explicitly defines anatomical sex as 'the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy.' Not their driver's license. Not how they are dressed. Their physical anatomy. And then on Page 2, you mandate that a law enforcement officer issue a fifty-dollar civil fine at the restroom door based on that physical anatomy."

Katie released her joystick brake half an inch, rolling her compact black power wheelchair right up flush to the edge of the committee table as her blue eyes locked onto the bill's patron.

"When I was first elected by the people of Kempsville and Virginia Beach to serve in this House of Delegates two years ago," Katie said, her voice rising with raw, unmistakable fire, "I had to fight the facilities bureaucracy in this very Capitol complex just to get an ADA-compliant restroom stall wide enough for my power wheelchair to turn around in and close the door in privacy. Every single day of my life since I was sixteen years old, I have known what it feels like to have strangers stare at my body and treat a basic human necessity like using a public restroom as a public spectacle. And now you come into this committee room with House Bill 663 and tell the women and girls of Virginia that the state government is going to police 'anatomical sex' at the door of every ladies' room from Virginia Beach to Blacksburg for a fifty-dollar fine!"

Inside the cramped room, you could have heard a pin drop. Delegate Cobb's mouth opened slightly at the lectern, his fingers tightening on the edges of his leather folder.

"So tell me, Delegate Cobb—right here on the record!" Katie demanded, shaking the printed pages of House Bill 663 toward the lectern. "How, exactly, is a police officer or a state building inspector standing outside a restroom door at the Community College of Hampton Roads, or Virginia Dominion University, or right upstairs in this State Capitol supposed to verify a citizen's 'physical anatomy' before writing your fifty-dollar ticket? Why on earth should I—or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia—have to pull my pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove I have female parts just to use the ladies' room?!"

An audible gasp rippled across the hearing room, followed half a second later by a sudden, thunderous burst of applause from the packed folding chairs in the citizen gallery.

"Order! Order in the committee room!" the chairman barked, pounding his gavel twice as his face flushed crimson.

At the witness lectern, Delegate Martin Cobb stood completely frozen for four full seconds, his face beet-red under the fluorescent lights as he looked from Katie's power wheelchair to the uneasy, downward stares of his own party colleagues along the dais.

"I—Mr. Chairman, the bill does not—nobody is suggesting—" Delegate Cobb stammered into his microphone, shuffling his papers without answering the question.

"That is literally what the words you printed in House Bill 663 require, Delegate Cobb!" Katie said, her voice steady, clear, and unflinching as the applause echoed off the hallway walls outside. "Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and every citizen in this Commonwealth deserves basic human dignity and privacy without politicians stationing bathroom police at the door to inspect people's anatomy. No woman in Virginia should ever have to pull her pants down to prove she has female parts just to use a public restroom, and this bill has no business passing out of this room!"

## 8:32 AM to 1:15 PM — From a 95-Second Captioned Clip to a Coast-to-Coast Explosion

When Katie switched off her microphone and leaned back in her SwiftChair, her hands were still trembling slightly from the adrenaline.

Neither of us had any idea what had just happened beyond the four walls of Subcommittee Room 4. To us, it was simply Katie refusing to let a cruel, poorly written bill slide through an eight-o'clock morning hearing unchallenged.

Looking down at my laptop screen at 8:22 AM while the next witness was being called, I saved the raw digital video recording from my capture box. The entire exchange between Katie and Delegate Cobb ran ninety-five seconds.

Because Katie and I had spent the past ten years building accessible web tools—and because I knew that most people checking their phones or work computers during the morning commute watched video clips with the sound muted—I opened Quantum Code right there on my lap tray, typed out a 100-percent verbatim, frame-accurate WebVTT closed-caption file for the ninety-five-second video, burned high-contrast open captions along the bottom frame as a fallback, and uploaded the raw, unedited clip to Katie's legislative website, StreamTube, and The Grid at 8:32 AM with a simple, factual title:

> Delegate Katie Prairie (D-Virginia Beach) Questions HB 663 Patron on How Virginia Would Enforce "Anatomical Sex" Restroom Fines (Feb. 4, 2016)

"Clip is live with full captions," I whispered, leaning across my armrest to squeeze Katie's right hand as the subcommittee recessed at 8:45 AM so members could head over to the Capitol for morning caucus meetings.

"Thanks, Nate," Katie sighed, taking a sip from her travel mug of ginger ale as we rolled out toward the underground Capitol tunnel connecting the General Assembly Building to the historic Capitol up the hill. "At least the students and parents who drove all the way here this morning know somebody on that committee was willing to fight for them, even if the majority rams it through anyway."

## Thursday Evening & Friday, February 4–5, 2016 — From Richmond and Norfolk Local News to Friday Live Interviews

That was how little either of us expected the clip to go viral.

At 9:15 AM on Thursday morning, while Katie was inside the House Democratic Caucus room on the first floor and I was sitting in my SwiftChair outside in the marble corridor checking my laptop, the first local ripple began.

Two veteran Capitol beat reporters—one from the Richmond daily paper and one from our hometown paper in Norfolk—had seen a legislative aide watching my captioned ninety-five-second video clip on a phone in the basement cafeteria. Within ten minutes, both reporters embedded the video on their state-politics blogs and posted the direct quote on The Grid:

> Virginia Beach Del. Katie Prairie leaves HB 663 patron speechless in early-morning subcommittee: "Why on earth should I—or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia—have to pull my pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove I have female parts just to use the ladies' room?"

Throughout Thursday afternoon, while Katie sat on the carpeted floor of the House Chamber for the noon legislative session and I watched the analytics dashboard from the third-floor wheelchair gallery, the clip spread organically across Virginia—shared from neighbor to neighbor in Virginia Beach, Norfolk, Chesapeake, Hampton, Newport News, and Richmond. Because I had burned full open captions along the bottom of the frame, office workers and college students at Virginia Dominion University, the Community College of Hampton Roads, and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute could watch the entire exchange on mute during their lunch breaks.

By 4:30 PM Thursday afternoon, when Katie and I rolled back into her fifth-floor legislative office in the General Assembly Building, the assignment desks at the local television news stations in Richmond and Norfolk had all downloaded the captioned video feed.

At 5:00 PM and 6:00 PM on Thursday evening, every single local television news broadcast in Richmond and back home in the Norfolk–Virginia Beach market led their Capitol coverage by playing Katie's subcommittee speech in full. Sitting side by side in our wheelchairs in our Richmond winter-session apartment over dinner, Katie and I watched the Hampton Roads evening news anchors play the clip of Katie holding up the printed pages of House Bill 663 from her SwiftChair while Delegate Martin Cobb stood frozen and beet-red at the lectern—and before the six-o'clock broadcast was even half over, Katie's legislative voicemail and our shared press inbox were flooded with requests from the Richmond and Norfolk stations asking to interview her live on Friday.

On Friday morning, February 5, a row of local television microwave trucks was parked along the curb of Capitol Square outside the General Assembly Building.

Rolling out onto the brick walk beside me in the crisp winter sunshine after Friday's brief morning floor session, Katie gave back-to-back live interviews from her compact SwiftChair to the Richmond and Norfolk news crews. She didn't use canned partisan talking points or sound like a rehearsed career politician; she held up the exact same printed two-page copy of House Bill 663 and explained in plain, steady language why policing "anatomical sex" at the door of public restrooms for a fifty-dollar fine was an insult to every woman, every disabled citizen, and every transgender Virginian in the Commonwealth.

That Friday afternoon was when the story broke containment out of Virginia and exploded nationwide.

The national network affiliate feeds at GBS, GNN, and Beacon News picked up the Friday lunchtime packages straight from their Richmond and Norfolk local stations, pairing Thursday's ninety-five-second subcommittee clip with Katie's poised Friday interviews on the Capitol lawn. By Friday evening at 6:30 PM, the GBS Evening Report and Global News Network were airing the story coast-to-coast; by 11:35 PM Friday night, all three national late-night talk-show hosts were roasting Virginia's fifty-dollar "anatomical inspection" summons in their opening monologues; and by Saturday morning, my analytics counter showed the clip surging past three million views as the Virginia Chamber of Commerce and the Virginia Hospitality and Travel Association began frantically calling House leadership in a panic that House Bill 663 was making the Virginia General Assembly a national laughingstock.

Then, at 2:15 PM on Saturday afternoon, my cell phone rang with a Washington, D.C. area code.

It was the senior booking producer for Meet the Nation—GBS Network's flagship Sunday-morning political affairs broadcast, the most-watched Sunday-morning news program in the United States.

## Sunday, February 7, 2016, 9:00 AM — "If It's Sunday, It's Meet the Nation" & Killing the Bill by Voice Vote

At 8:40 AM on Sunday morning, Katie and I rolled our compact SwiftChairs up the studio ramp of the local GBS television affiliate in Richmond.

Inside the quiet, darkened broadcast studio, the floor crew had cleared away the standard swivel anchor chair from the center of the remote-interview set so Katie could roll her own SwiftChair right up to the mark in front of the illuminated Virginia State Capitol backdrop. When a well-meaning stage manager asked if the cameraman should crop the shot tight around Katie's shoulders so viewers wouldn't see her wheelchair controls, Katie shook her head politely and rested her left hand right on her joystick armrest.

"Keep the frame wide enough to show my chair, please," Katie told him warmly. "A lot of the people watching this morning have never seen a legislator in a power wheelchair on a Sunday morning show, and I don't hide who I am."

Parked four feet to the left of the main camera beside the studio floor monitor with my laptop on my lap tray, I watched the red countdown clock tick down to 9:00 AM Eastern as the iconic brass orchestra fanfare of Meet the Nation came through the studio speakers.

On the monitor, the veteran Washington moderator looked straight into Camera One from the network desk in D.C.

"Good morning," the moderator announced across millions of living rooms from coast to coast. "If it's Sunday, it's Meet the Nation. Later this hour, we will hear from the presidential campaigns ahead of Tuesday's primary—but we begin this Sunday morning with a ninety-five-second moment from a state legislative basement in Richmond, Virginia that has been watched more than four million times across the country in the last seventy-two hours, igniting a national debate over so-called 'bathroom bills,' personal privacy, and civil rights."

On the national network feed, Meet the Nation rolled the exact ninety-five-second video clip I had captured and captioned on my laptop in Subcommittee #4 on Thursday morning—showing Katie in her power wheelchair holding up the printed pages of House Bill 663 and demanding of Delegate Martin Cobb why she, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove she has female parts just to use the ladies' room.

When the clip cut back to the split-screen studio feed, the lower-third graphic flashed across television screens nationwide:

> LIVE FROM RICHMOND, VA<br>
> DELEGATE KATIE PRAIRIE (D-VIRGINIA BEACH)<br>
> VIRGINIA HOUSE OF DELEGATES — 85TH DISTRICT

"And joining us live this morning from Richmond is the lawmaker in that video—State Delegate Katie Prairie, Democrat of Virginia Beach," the moderator said. "Delegate Prairie, welcome to Meet the Nation."

"Good morning, and thank you for having me," Katie said, her posture upright, calm, and completely natural in her SwiftChair under the studio lights.

"Delegate Prairie, on Thursday morning you were sitting in an eight-o'clock House General Laws subcommittee hearing with no television cameras in the room, and by today your question to the bill's patron has become national news," the moderator said. "The sponsors of House Bill 663 argue they are simply trying to protect privacy in public restrooms and schools. Why did you zero in so forcefully on the bill's definition of 'anatomical sex'?"

"Because when you write laws that govern eight million people and carry a fifty-dollar law-enforcement summons, words matter," Katie answered smoothly, looking straight into the lens without a single note in front of her. "My twin brother Nate and I are software developers by trade, and when you write code—or when you write a state statute—you have to trace how an instruction actually executes in the real world. House Bill 663 defines 'anatomical sex' on Page 1 not by an identification card or how someone lives their daily life, but strictly as 'the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy,' enforced by a fifty-dollar police summons at the door of every state building, college, and public school restroom in Virginia."

Katie leaned slightly forward in her chair, her voice ringing with the exact same moral clarity that had silenced Subcommittee #4 on Thursday morning.

"As someone who has used a power wheelchair full-time since I was sixteen years old—and who had to fight just to get an ADA-accessible bathroom stall inside our own State Capitol complex—I know firsthand that the last thing any citizen needs at a restroom door is government surveillance," Katie continued on live national television. "You cannot enforce an 'anatomical sex' law at a public ladies' room door without either profiling women by how they look or demanding an invasive physical inspection. Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and no woman or girl in Virginia should ever have to pull her pants down to prove she has female parts just to use the bathroom. Our constituents sent us to Richmond to fix our roads, expand public transit, fund our schools, and clear our disability Medicaid waitlists—not to turn police officers into bathroom inspectors."

Watching the network monitor from beside the camera, I could see the Washington panel nodding around the table in D.C. Over the next six minutes, Katie fielded every follow-up question from the moderator and the national roundtable—including the frantic warnings from the Virginia Chamber of Commerce and tourism boards that House Bill 663 was threatening conventions and jobs across the state—with such effortless, common-sense precision that when the commercial break cue light finally flashed, the Richmond studio crew broke into spontaneous applause.

By Monday morning, after Katie's live appearance on Meet the Nation had been replayed all Sunday afternoon across cable news and printed on the front pages of every newspaper in Virginia, House Bill 663 was politically radioactive.

The next time House General Laws Subcommittee #4 convened to take final action on the docket, the lower-level hearing room wasn't half-empty anymore. Every television camera crew in Richmond and Washington was crammed along the side walls, lenses pointed directly at the committee table.

Nobody on the majority side of the dais wanted their name attached to a recorded roll-call vote on a bill that had just been dismantled on Meet the Nation—and nobody wanted to go home to their districts and explain on camera why they had voted for a fifty-dollar "anatomical inspection" fine.

When the subcommittee chairman called House Bill 663 up for its disposition vote, the majority didn't even debate it.

"Is there a motion to lay House Bill 663 on the table?" the chairman asked briskly.

"So moved," a nervous majority member muttered without looking up from his desk.

"All in favor of laying the bill on the table say aye—all opposed say no—the ayes have it, and House Bill 663 is laid on the table!" the chairman announced in a single breath, banging his gavel to kill his own party's bathroom bill on an unrecorded voice vote before anyone could demand a roll call.

Standing by the witness lectern as his bill died without a single recorded vote, Delegate Martin Cobb—alongside an equally furious Delegate Robert Marsh, who was already muttering under his breath that he would bring his own "Physical Privacy Act" back next session—glared down the dais at his own party's committee members, gathered up his folders, and snapped into the open air as he stormed out of the room:

"I'm going to pray that you all get courage!"

At the accessible end of the committee table, Katie watched the bill's patron stomp out the door, turned her head toward my wheelchair beside the rail, and gave me the quietest, proudest twin smile in the world as the college students and families in the gallery broke into relieved cheers. What had begun at eight o'clock on a Thursday morning in a windowless basement subcommittee room had carried all the way from the local evening news in Richmond and Norfolk to the Sunday morning desk of Meet the Nation—proving once and for all that when Katie Prairie spoke up for civil rights from her SwiftChair, the whole nation had to listen.
