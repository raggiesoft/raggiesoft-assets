## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 6:00 PM — The Evening News in Richmond and Norfolk

"Hand me the pepper shaker, Nate," Katie said at six o'clock that Thursday evening, setting two warm plates of baked chicken and rice down on the kitchen table of our accessible one-bedroom winter-session apartment at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts on West Broad Street after we had ridden the 5:35 PM westbound JRTC Route 6 bus home from Ninth and Broad. "And turn on the television so we can see the weather forecast for the weekend."

We had spent the entire afternoon from two until five-thirty back in Katie's fifth-floor office in the General Assembly Building, meeting with special-education parents from Virginia Beach and reviewing coastal flooding amendments with our legislative aide, with both of our laptops closed during the meetings.

Picking up the remote control from the corner of the table, I clicked on the wall-mounted television—tuned first to the Richmond local six-o'clock news broadcast, with my laptop open beside my water glass streaming our hometown six-o'clock broadcast from Norfolk so we could keep up with Hampton Roads news while we were away in Richmond.

I barely had time to set the remote down before both my sister and I froze with our forks halfway to our mouths.

On the television screen on the wall—and simultaneously on the Norfolk local news stream playing on my laptop—was the exact ninety-five-second video clip I had captured from the third-floor House gallery at lunchtime.

*"—and our top story from the State Capitol tonight,"* the Richmond evening anchor announced over the footage of Katie sitting upright in her compact SwiftChair on the House Floor holding up the two printed pages of House Bill 663, *"a fiery floor exchange in the House of Delegates this afternoon over House Bill 663, the controversial bathroom bill restricting public restrooms by 'anatomical sex,' after Virginia Beach Delegate Katie Prairie brought the House Chamber to a standstill with a single question for the bill's patron."*

Before our eyes, both the Richmond station and the Norfolk station aired the floor clip in full—every word of Katie asking Delegate Martin Cobb why she, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove she had female parts just to use the ladies' room, followed by the four seconds of Delegate Cobb standing frozen and beet-red behind his desk microphone before the Majority Leader hurriedly passed the bill by for the day.

Katie stared at the television screen, her blue eyes wide. "Nate... they're playing the whole floor clip on the six-o'clock news. In Richmond and back home in Norfolk."

I reached over and woke my laptop browser window, refreshing the server dashboard for Katie's legislative archive and The Grid.

My jaw dropped.

"Katie, look at the traffic logs," I said, turning the laptop screen toward her wheelchair. "The two Capitol beat reporters from the Richmond daily paper and the Norfolk paper embedded your captioned clip on their blogs at two-thirty this afternoon while we were in constituent meetings. Because the open captions were burned right into the video frame, thousands of office workers and college students at Virginia Dominion University, CCHR, and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute watched it on their phones during the afternoon and shared it across the state. That's how the assignment desks at the Richmond and Norfolk TV stations grabbed it for the five-o'clock and six-o'clock broadcasts!"

Before Katie could even answer, both of our cell phones on the kitchen counter started vibrating nonstop, one call and email notification after another chiming across the room.

I rolled over to the counter, picked up both phones, and looked at the caller IDs and the shared press inbox.

"It's the Capitol news producers for all three local stations in Richmond and the Hampton Roads bureaus in Norfolk," I told Katie, reading the timestamps scrolling down the screen. "Every single one of them is asking if you'll do a live on-camera interview outside the Capitol tomorrow morning."

## Friday, February 5, 2016, 11:00 AM to Saturday Afternoon — Friday's Local News Interviews & The Call from Washington

On Friday morning, February 5, a row of local television microwave trucks from Richmond and Norfolk was parked bumper-to-bumper along the brick curb of Capitol Square outside the General Assembly Building.

Rolling out onto the sunlit brick walkway beside me in the crisp winter air at eleven o'clock, thirty-year-old Katie parked her compact SwiftChair in front of the cluster of local station microphones while I stood by two feet to her left with our legislative binder on my lap tray.

One after another, Katie gave back-to-back live interviews to the Richmond and Norfolk television crews. She didn't use canned partisan talking points or sound like a rehearsed career politician; she held up the exact same yellow-highlighted two-page printout of House Bill 663 she had carried onto the House Floor on Thursday and explained in plain, steady language why policing "anatomical sex" at the door of public restrooms for a fifty-dollar fine was unworkable, invasive, and an insult to every woman, every disabled citizen, and every transgender Virginian in the Commonwealth.

"When you write a law that carries a fifty-dollar police summons based on 'a person's physical anatomy,' you have to answer honestly how that law gets enforced at a public restroom door," Katie told the Norfolk anchor live on the Friday noon broadcast. "Nobody in Virginia sent us to Richmond to turn police officers and school principals into bathroom inspectors."

That Friday afternoon was when the story crossed the Potomac River and exploded coast-to-coast.

The national network news desks at GBS, Global News Network, and Beacon News pulled the Friday lunchtime broadcast packages directly from their Richmond and Norfolk local affiliates—pairing Thursday's ninety-five-second House Floor clip with Katie's poised Friday interviews on the Capitol lawn. By Friday evening at 6:30 PM, the national network evening broadcasts were airing the clip from New York to Los Angeles; by 11:35 PM Friday night, all three national late-night talk-show hosts were roasting Virginia's fifty-dollar "anatomical inspection" summons in their opening monologues; and by Saturday morning, my server analytics counter showed the captioned floor clip passing three million views as the Virginia Chamber of Commerce and the Virginia Hospitality and Travel Association began calling House leadership in a panic that House Bill 663 was threatening conventions and tourism jobs across the state.

Then, at 2:15 PM on Saturday afternoon, while Katie and I were sitting side by side at our apartment dining table answering constituent emails from Virginia Beach, my cell phone rang with a Washington, D.C. area code.

"Hello, Delegate Katie Prairie's office, this is Nathaniel Prairie speaking," I answered.

*"Hi, Nathaniel—this is the senior booking producer for Meet the Nation at GBS News in Washington,"* the voice on the line said urgently. *"Every political desk in the country is talking about your sister's floor speech on House Bill 663. We want to book Delegate Prairie live from our Richmond affiliate studio tomorrow morning at nine o'clock as our lead guest at the top of the broadcast. Can the two of you be at the studio by 8:35 AM?"*

I muted the phone for two seconds and looked across the table at my twin sister.

"Katie," I whispered, "it's GBS News in Washington. They want you live on Meet the Nation tomorrow morning."

## Sunday, February 7, 2016, 9:00 AM — "If It's Sunday, It's Meet the Nation" & Laying the Bill on the Table

At 8:35 AM on Sunday morning, Katie and I rolled our compact SwiftChairs up the studio ramp of the local GBS television affiliate in Richmond.

Inside the quiet, darkened broadcast studio, the floor crew had cleared away the standard swivel anchor chair from the center of the remote-interview set so Katie could roll her own SwiftChair right up to the tape mark in front of the illuminated Virginia State Capitol backdrop. When a well-meaning stage manager asked if the camera operator should crop the shot tight around Katie's shoulders so national viewers wouldn't see her wheelchair controls, Katie shook her head politely and rested her left hand squarely on her joystick armrest.

"Keep the frame wide enough to show my chair, please," Katie told him warmly. "A lot of the people watching across the country this morning have never seen a legislator in a power wheelchair on a Sunday morning show, and I don't hide who I am."

Parked four feet to the left of Camera One beside the studio floor monitor with my laptop resting on my lap tray, I watched the red digital countdown clock tick down to 9:00 AM Eastern as the iconic brass orchestra fanfare of Meet the Nation echoed through the studio earpieces.

On the monitor, the veteran Washington moderator looked straight into the main camera from the network desk in D.C.

"Good morning," the moderator announced across millions of living rooms from coast to coast. "If it's Sunday, it's Meet the Nation. Later this hour, we will hear from the presidential campaigns ahead of Tuesday's primary—but we begin this Sunday morning with a ninety-five-second moment from the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates in Richmond that has been watched more than four million times across the country in the last seventy-two hours, igniting a national debate over so-called 'bathroom bills,' personal privacy, and civil rights."

On the national network broadcast, Meet the Nation rolled the exact ninety-five-second video clip I had recorded and captioned from the third-floor public gallery on Thursday afternoon—showing Katie down on the House Floor in her power wheelchair holding up the printed pages of House Bill 663 and demanding of Delegate Martin Cobb why she, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove she had female parts just to use the ladies' room.

When the clip cut back to the live split-screen studio feed, the lower-third graphic flashed across television screens nationwide:

> LIVE FROM RICHMOND, VA<br>
> DELEGATE KATIE PRAIRIE (D-VIRGINIA BEACH)<br>
> VIRGINIA HOUSE OF DELEGATES — 85TH DISTRICT

"And joining us live this morning from Richmond is the lawmaker in that video—State Delegate Katie Prairie, Democrat of Virginia Beach," the moderator said. "Delegate Prairie, welcome to Meet the Nation."

"Good morning, and thank you for having me," Katie said, her posture upright, calm, and completely natural in her SwiftChair under the studio lights.

"Delegate Prairie, on Thursday afternoon you rose at your desk on the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates to ask a single question about House Bill 663, and by Friday evening your remarks had made national news," the moderator said. "The sponsors of the bill argue they are simply trying to protect privacy in public restrooms and schools. Why did you zero in so forcefully on Line 18's definition of 'anatomical sex'?"

"Because when you write laws that govern eight million people and carry a fifty-dollar law-enforcement summons, words matter," Katie answered smoothly, looking straight into the camera lens without a single note in front of her. "My twin brother Nate and I are software developers by trade, and when you write code—or when you write a state statute—you have to trace how an instruction actually executes in the real world. House Bill 663 defines 'anatomical sex' on Page 1 not by an identification card or how someone lives their daily life, but strictly as 'the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy,' enforced by a fifty-dollar police summons at the door of every state building, public college, and public school restroom in Virginia."

Katie leaned slightly forward in her chair, her voice ringing with the exact same moral clarity that had hushed the House Chamber on Thursday afternoon.

"As someone who has used a power wheelchair full-time since I was sixteen years old—and who had to fight just to get an ADA-accessible bathroom stall inside our own State Capitol complex—I know firsthand that the last thing any citizen needs at a restroom door is government surveillance," Katie continued on live national television. "You cannot enforce an 'anatomical sex' law at a public ladies' room door without either profiling women by how they look or demanding an invasive physical inspection. Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and no woman or girl in Virginia should ever have to pull her pants down to prove she has female parts just to use the bathroom. Our constituents sent us to Richmond to fix our roads, expand public transit, fund our schools, and clear our disability Medicaid waitlists—not to turn police officers into bathroom inspectors."

Watching the network return monitor from beside the camera, I could see the Washington journalists around the roundtable in D.C. nodding as they took notes. Over the next six minutes, Katie fielded every follow-up question from the moderator and the panel—including the urgent warnings from the Virginia Chamber of Commerce and hospitality leaders that House Bill 663 was threatening conventions and jobs across the state—with such effortless, common-sense precision that when the commercial-break cue light finally flashed, the Richmond studio crew broke into quiet applause.

By Monday morning, after Katie's live appearance on Meet the Nation had been replayed all Sunday afternoon across cable news and printed on the front pages of every newspaper in Virginia, House Bill 663 was politically radioactive.

Nobody in the House majority leadership wanted their members forced to cast a recorded roll-call vote on the House Floor on a bill that had just been dismantled on Meet the Nation—and nobody wanted to go home to their districts and explain on television why they had voted for a fifty-dollar "anatomical inspection" fine.

Before the noon floor session even convened on Monday, the majority recommitted House Bill 663 back to the General Laws subcommittee room downstairs to dispose of it quietly. This time, every television camera crew in Richmond and Washington was crammed shoulder-to-shoulder along the side walls.

When the chairman called House Bill 663 up for final disposition, the majority didn't even debate it.

"Is there a motion to lay House Bill 663 on the table?" the chairman asked briskly.

"So moved," a majority member muttered without looking up from his desk.

"All in favor of laying the bill on the table say aye—all opposed say no—the ayes have it, and House Bill 663 is laid on the table!" the chairman announced in a single breath, banging his gavel to kill his own party's bathroom bill on an unrecorded voice vote before anyone could demand a roll call.

Standing by the lectern as his bill died without a single recorded vote, Delegate Martin Cobb—alongside an equally furious Delegate Robert Marsh, who was already muttering under his breath that he would bring his own "Physical Privacy Act" back next session—glared down the dais at his own party's members, gathered up his folders, and snapped into the open air as he stormed out the door:

"I'm going to pray that you all get courage!"

At the accessible end of the room, Katie watched the bill's patron stomp out into the hallway, turned her head toward my wheelchair, and gave me the quietest, proudest twin smile in the world as the college students and families in the room broke into relieved cheers.
