## Monday, March 5, 2007, 7:15 AM — The Medication Line, Auto Shop & Woodworking, & "Door Greeter!?"

![Inside the 1960s painted cinder-block Vocational Evaluation break room in the Bates-Harding Building at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in March 2007, twenty-one-year-old twins Nathaniel and Katie Prairie sit parked wheel-to-wheel in their collapsible manual wheelchairs outfitted with armrest joysticks, spoked push-rim wheels, and rear push-handles, locking hands across their armrests and loudly expressing their displeasure as evaluator Ms. Hollis and aide Mr. Miller try to separate them during social time.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/mar_2007_rrc_voc_eval_social_break_refusing_separation_katie_nate.jpg)

"We need our breakfast," Katie said as we rolled side by side out of the Barton Hall lobby toward the dining hall breezeway.

"I'm guessing breakfast is going to be just as nasty as dinner was last night," I replied as the automatic doors slid open into the crisp March mountain air.

Twenty minutes later, after pushing watery scrambled eggs and limp toast around our plastic plates, we rolled down the corridor to join the long single-file line of Level 2 clients waiting outside the dispensary window for our morning maintenance medication.

"This is so stupid," Katie whispered in exasperation as we inched forward in our joystick-outfitted wheelchairs. "We're twenty-one years old, and we're both used to managing our own medication bottles back home in our apartment."

When we reached the counter, the dispensary nurse leaned toward the opening and spoke to us in a high-pitched, sing-song voice as if we were seven-year-olds learning how to swallow a chewable vitamin. "Good morning, friends! Ready to take our morning pills with a big sip of water?"

I looked straight across the counter at her. "Ma'am, we have associate degrees and we've managed our own prescriptions in our own apartment in Norfolk for three and a half years," I said flatly. "You don't need to talk down to us like children. Just hand us the paper cups and check our names off the clipboard."

The nurse's patronizing smile vanished immediately, and she handed over our pleated paper cups in silence.

"I don't think I'm going to like it here," I said quietly to Katie as we rolled away from the window, slipping my arm around her shoulders.

"I already miss the apartment," Katie whispered back, leaning against my arm.

Right then, angry screams echoed from the main entrance doors down the hall.

"You are not my boyfriend anymore, and I don't ever want to see you again!" a girl shouted, shoving through the doors.

"Get out of my life too, jerk!" a boy shouted right back at her.

Down the hallway from us, another student sighed and shook his head. "Those two. Always fighting. Always arguing."

Katie looked over at me with a wry, grateful smile. "I'm glad we're siblings, Nate."

I pulled her into a warm hug across our armrests and whispered, "Yeah. No boyfriend-girlfriend drama between us."

At eight-thirty sharp, we followed the directional signs into the Vocational Evaluation wing of the Bates-Harding Building and parked side by side at a scarred laminate table at the front of the intake room. At the dry-erase board stood the lead evaluation specialist, Ms. Hollis, holding a thick stack of yellow checklists while her evaluation aide, Mr. Miller, passed them down the rows.

"Welcome to Vocational Evaluation," Ms. Hollis announced briskly. "Before any client at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center is permitted to enter a vocational training classroom, you will spend the next two full weeks right here in Voc Eval completing three mandatory requirements under Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services guidelines. First, you will demonstrate independent-living skills to our staff—making your dorm bed with proper corners, doing a load of laundry, and preparing a basic meal in the test kitchen. Second, you will rotate with Mr. Miller through our campus trade shops—auto mechanics, woodworking, nursing assistant, food service, and retail—so we can determine which trade is the best fit for you. And third, our team will grade your daily socialization and appropriate social skills."

Katie raised her hand immediately. Neither of us had been prepared for any of this—not the rules, not the baby-talk at the dispensary window, and certainly not two weeks of mandatory Vocational Evaluation. We had zero interest in the rehabilitation side of RRC; we had come to Fishersville strictly for the Web Applications Developer class, fully expecting to treat the center like college—go to class, sleep in the dorm, and be done with it.

"Excuse me, Ms. Hollis?" Katie said. "Nathaniel and I already have our associate degrees from the Community College of Hampton Roads, we lived in our own off-campus apartments in Norfolk for three and a half years, and our vocational counselor in Hampton Roads specifically sent us here for the Web Applications Developer PHP and SQL track. We've been writing HTML since seventh grade and Quantum Basic since high school. Why do we need to spend two weeks touring auto mechanics, woodworking, and nursing?"

Ms. Hollis tapped her pen against her clipboard with a tight, practiced smile. "Everyone follows the same two-week evaluation protocol here, Katie. Nobody skips the trade rotations. Mr. Miller, take the group down to the auto mechanics bay first."

For the next three hours, Katie and I sat side by side in mounting disbelief as Mr. Miller led our cohort from one manual-labor shop to the next. In the auto mechanics bay, we sat parked beside a hydraulic lift smelling burnt motor oil while an instructor demonstrated a heavy pneumatic impact wrench that neither Katie nor I had the upper-body strength to lift off a workbench, let alone use standing under a car chassis. Forty-five minutes later, Mr. Miller marched the group into the woodworking shop, where fine oak sawdust drifted over our lap trays and screaming table saws vibrated through the concrete floor. Right before lunch, we were wheeled into the nursing assistant training lab to watch students practice two-person transfers lifting a hundred-and-fifty-pound plastic mannequin onto a hospital bed.

As soon as Mr. Miller led the group back into the Bates-Harding evaluation room at eleven-thirty, I steered straight up to Ms. Hollis's desk with Katie right beside me.

"Ms. Hollis, that tour makes zero sense for the two of us," I said bluntly. "We're both in wheelchairs with progressive neuromuscular weakness in our legs and arms. We can't stand under a hydraulic lift in an auto shop, we can't stand at a table saw in a woodworking shop, and we certainly can't lift hospital patients in nursing school. We came here to write server-side PHP and SQL code."

Ms. Hollis looked down at our wheelchairs—never once glancing at our CCHR transcripts in her folder—and put on a bright, syrupy smile.

"Now, Katie, Nathaniel, here at Roosevelt we have to evaluate realistic physical alternatives in case the stamina demands of software engineering prove too taxing," Ms. Hollis said, tapping her pen on the desk blotter. "If the computer track doesn't pan out, we have a wonderful partnership with ValueMart right down the road in Waynesboro! We could train Katie to be a ValueMart Door Greeter—it has very minimal physical requirements, and you just sit in the vestibule and smile at shoppers—and Nathaniel, with your polite voice, we could place you in our Food Service Prep or stockroom tagging track!"

Katie stared at her in utter, stunned disbelief.

"Door greeter!?" Katie repeated out loud, her voice ringing off the painted cinder-block walls so loudly that every student in the room turned around to look. "My brother and I have associate degrees with honors! We write HTML, CSS, and Quantum Basic by hand, we run a regional transit analysis website read by city councilors across six cities, and you look at two people in wheelchairs and think our career ceiling is sitting by the sliding doors at ValueMart saying hello to shoppers?!"

"I'm simply explaining all of the options DARS offers our clients," Ms. Hollis said stiffly, her cheeks reddening as Katie and I exchanged a look of pure disgust and rolled back to our table.

## Tuesday, March 6, 2007, 9:15 AM — "Do Not Grab Our Wheelchairs!" & Refusing Separation at Social Break

"All right, group, let's head down the hall to the independent-living test kitchen so we can check off your meal-prep rubric!" Mr. Miller called out at nine-fifteen on Tuesday morning as Katie and I closed our testing binders and powered on our right-armrest joysticks to roll out into the Bates-Harding corridor.

Before we had even cleared the doorway, Mr. Miller walked briskly up behind the two of us without saying a word, reached out, and clamped his right hand onto Katie's rear rubber push-handle and his left hand onto my rear rubber push-handle to shove both of our wheelchairs down the hallway himself.

The sudden manual shove against our motorized wheel hubs jolted both of our cross-brace frames forward at once.

"Hey—take your hands off our wheelchairs right now!" I barked loudly, my voice echoing down the terrazzo corridor as both Katie and I clicked our drives off and slammed our manual wheel brakes locked, stopping Mr. Miller dead in his tracks.

"Excuse me!" Katie shouted, twisting around in her seat so every evaluator and student in the corridor heard her displeasure. "Do not grab my wheelchair! Our chairs have joysticks on the armrests—we drive ourselves! Grabbing somebody's wheelchair without asking is no different from grabbing our bodies in the middle of the hallway!"

Mr. Miller jerked both hands back as if the rubber grips had burned his palms, his face turning pink. "Oh—now wait a second, Katie, Nathaniel, I was just trying to be helpful and get everyone down to the test kitchen on time—"

"If you want us at the test kitchen, you tell us where the door is with your voice, just like you tell every walking student in this hallway!" I said loudly, refusing to lower my volume by a single decibel. "In our community, a wheelchair is an extension of the person sitting in it. You wouldn't walk up behind a standing twenty-one-year-old woman and shove her down the hall by her shoulders without asking, and you do not put your hands on our push-handles to push us around this center!"

Mr. Miller muttered a flustered apology and kept six feet behind us all the way to the test kitchen—where Katie and I briskly fried eggs, toasted bread, scrubbed the skillet clean, and checked off every single independent-living box on the clipboard in twenty minutes flat.

By two-fifteen on Wednesday afternoon, however, during the scheduled Vocational Evaluation social break in the painted cinder-block break room, Ms. Hollis marched across the linoleum toward the quiet corner where Katie and I sat parked wheel-to-wheel drinking two cans of ginger ale and sketching relational database tables—with Mr. Miller trailing right behind her.

"Nathaniel, Katie," Ms. Hollis said, rapping her pen against her clipboard with a disapproving frown, "for the past three days, the evaluation team has noted that the two of you have not separated once—not in the evaluation room, not during break times, not in the cafeteria, and not in the Barton Hall lounge."

Katie and I looked up from our notebook at the exact same second. "That's because we're twins, ma'am," Katie said firmly. "We do everything together."

"Well, while you are in Vocational Evaluation, that needs to change—especially during social times," Ms. Hollis said briskly, gesturing toward two opposite corners of the break room. "Part of your evaluation is proving that you have independent social skills. You two are twenty-one years old—you are supposed to be branching out, making your own individual friends, and going your separate sibling ways during the day. Starting right now for the rest of social break, Nathaniel, you are going over to Table One, and Katie, you are going over to Table Four. Mr. Miller, go ahead and wheel Katie over to Table Four."

Before Mr. Miller could bring his fingers within six inches of Katie's rear push-handles, Katie and I reached across our armrests, locked our hands together in an unbreakable grip, and let the entire room hear our displeasure at full volume.

"Keep your hands off my sister's wheelchair!" I barked across the break room, making every conversation at Tables One through Four stop dead. "We told you yesterday—nobody touches our chairs, and nobody pushes us around!"

"And we are NOT being handled or separated like five-year-olds during break time!" Katie said loudly, her blue eyes blazing straight at Ms. Hollis.

Ms. Hollis blinked, visibly rattled by two Level 2 clients telling her no in front of the entire cohort. "Excuse me, Katie? Nathaniel? Refusing a socialization directive during social time goes down as a deficiency on your Vocational Evaluation rubric."

"Then write it down in capital letters as a deficiency!" Katie shot back loudly enough for the whole wing to hear. "My brother and I are perfectly polite to everyone in this room! Just Sunday afternoon in the Barton Hall lobby, we shared our DVD with half the building and had everyone laughing together! We know how to talk to people, we already passed your bed-making, laundry, and kitchen checks yesterday, and we already have associate degrees! What we do not tolerate is Center staff grabbing our wheelchairs like they own our bodies or trying to pry twin brother and sister apart during social time just to check a box on your clipboard!"

"And we are not sitting in Vocational Evaluation for a second week," I added loudly and evenly, holding Katie's hand tightly across our armrests. "Put the entire two-day Web Applications Developer technical, mathematical, HTML, and logic exam battery on our table at eight-thirty tomorrow morning. Either we test out of Voc Eval in one week and start the Web Applications Developer class on Monday morning, or we will call our father in Alexandria right now, pack our suitcases in Barton Hall, and go home tonight!"

Every student at the four break tables was staring straight at Ms. Hollis and Mr. Miller. Pressing her lips into a thin line, Ms. Hollis motioned for Mr. Miller to step back. "Fine," she said clippedly. "Be at Testing Table Two at eight-thirty sharp tomorrow morning, and we'll see if you can pass the full Web Applications packet in one sitting."

## Thursday, March 8, 2007, 8:30 AM — The Outdated 1990s Exam Battery & Proving We Don't Cheat

When Katie and I rolled into the Vocational Evaluation testing room at eight-twenty-five on Thursday morning and locked our manual wheel brakes side by side at Testing Table Two, Ms. Hollis and Mr. Miller were already waiting for us with matching looks of smug anticipation.

Having watched us refuse to sit at separate tables during Wednesday afternoon's social break, both of them were clearly convinced that the real reason Katie and I insisted on sitting wheel-to-wheel was so we could cheat off each other—either whispering answers back and forth or letting one twin carry the other through the math and coding sections so she could disqualify us both and ship us right back to the ValueMart Door Greeter and Food Service tracks.

Ms. Hollis stepped up to the laminate tabletop and slapped down two three-inch-thick, spiral-bound exam packets, two green optical bubble sheets, two lined legal pads for scratch work, and four sharpened No. 2 pencils.

"Since the two of you flatly refused to sit at separate testing tables," Ms. Hollis announced pointedly, resting her palms on the packets, "I have pulled two different versions of the five-part Web Applications Developer entrance battery. Nathaniel, you have Form A. Katie, you have Form B, where all of the problem sets and multiple-choice items are scrambled in a completely different order. You have until three-thirty tomorrow afternoon to complete all five booklets: Computer Hardware and Operating Environments, HTML and Web Page Design, Applied Algebra, Binary and Boolean Logic, and Relational Database Normalization."

She gestured to Mr. Miller, who pulled a metal folding chair right up to the opposite side of Testing Table Two—barely four feet from our front casters—and sat down holding a clipboard and a digital stopwatch while Ms. Hollis positioned herself directly behind our wheelchairs.

"Mr. Miller will be sitting right here facing your hands and eyes the entire time, and I will be monitoring from behind your chairs," Ms. Hollis said coldly. "If either of you whispers a single word, glances sideways at the other person's booklet or bubble sheet, taps a code on your wheelchair armrests, or shares a single line of scratch paper, both of your exams will be confiscated on the spot for academic dishonesty, you will receive an automatic failure on your DARS evaluation rubric, and you will be permanently disqualified from the Information Technology track. Do we understand each other?"

Katie looked straight up at Ms. Hollis without flinching. "Ma'am, my brother and I graduated with honors from the Community College of Hampton Roads three months ago," she said evenly. "We don't sit together to copy answers. We sit together because we're twins, and neither of us has ever cheated on a test in our lives. Start your clock."

Ms. Hollis pressed the button on her stopwatch. "Begin."

Without looking at each other once, Katie and I rested our inner forearms lightly along our adjacent wheelchair armrests—just enough familiar twin contact to keep our nervous systems calm—and flipped open the heavy cardstock covers of Booklet One at the exact same second.

Ten seconds into page one, I heard Katie let out the exact same quiet, disgusted sigh through her nose that had just escaped mine.

It was March of 2007. Katie and I had been hand-coding HTML since seventh grade at Morrison Middle School in 1997, writing Quantum Basic 6.0 since high school in 2001, and building modern standards-compliant web pages at CCHR—and the official Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services "Web Applications Developer" Vocational Evaluation exam battery sitting on our table hadn't been updated since the mid-1990s.

In Booklet One—Computer Hardware and Operating Environments—there wasn't a single question about modern 2007 web servers, USB flash drives, or broadband ethernet networks. Instead, Question Three asked how many 1.44-megabyte three-and-a-half-inch floppy disks were required to back up an eleven-megabyte directory without compression. Question Nine asked how to set physical plastic jumpers on an ISA expansion card to resolve an IRQ interrupt conflict between a parallel printer port and a serial mouse. Question Fourteen asked how to edit startup configuration files in Q-Core so a desktop computer could free up enough conventional memory below the 640-kilobyte barrier to launch a database utility, and Question Twenty-Two asked us to calculate how many minutes it would take to download a three-megabyte uncompressed bitmap image over a 28.8-kilobit-per-second dial-up telephone modem.

Shaking my head in silent disbelief as I darkened the green bubbles on Form A, I flipped to Booklet Two—HTML and Web Page Design—and nearly groaned out loud.

Every single question treated 1996 browser wars and long-deprecated HTML 3.2 markup as if they were the pinnacle of modern software engineering. Instead of cascading style sheets or semantic markup, the test demanded to know the exact body-tag attribute to tile a repeating sixteen-color GIF image across a webpage background, how to format paragraph text using deprecated inline font-face tags, how to slice a 640-by-480-pixel VGA screen into three separate scrolling framesets, and which proprietary tag made a line of warning text blink on and off or scroll horizontally across the screen like a stock ticker. Beside me, I could hear the crisp, irritated scratch of Katie's No. 2 pencil writing a brisk margin note on her written-response sheet before she bubbled in the obsolete answer the grading key wanted.

And yet, for the next three hours, Mr. Miller sat four feet away leaning forward on his elbows, staring unblinkingly from my eyes to Katie's eyes, while Ms. Hollis paced back and forth behind our rear push-handles every four minutes waiting to pounce on a sideways glance that never came.

She caught nothing—because there was nothing to catch.

Neither Katie nor I spoke a syllable, tilted a page, or lifted our eyes from our own paperwork. When ten o'clock arrived and Mr. Miller cleared his throat to announce the fifteen-minute morning restroom and water-fountain break, both of us simply shook our heads without looking up and kept right on working. On my yellow legal pad on the left side of the table, I worked front-to-back through Form A's two's-complement binary arithmetic, hexadecimal memory conversions, Boolean truth tables, and quadratic word problems in neat, vertical block columns; on the right side of the table, Katie tore through Form B's scrambled logic gates, algebraic proofs, third-normal-form relational database diagrams, and hand-written SQL join queries using her own distinct step-by-step factoring notation—ending with a final section that absurdly asked us to diagram how an HTML form passed query strings to a 1990s Perl CGI-BIN script over a dial-up intranet.

At eleven-forty-five—fifteen minutes before the lunch bell, having completed the entire two-day, twelve-hour testing battery in three hours and fifteen minutes flat—Katie and I closed the back covers of Booklet Five within ten seconds of each other, set our No. 2 pencils down on the laminate, and rolled side by side up to Ms. Hollis's desk.

"We're finished," I said, sliding Form A, my bubble sheet, my written SQL and HTML packet, and my scratch pad across her blotter as Katie laid Form B right beside mine.

Ms. Hollis stared down at the two completed stacks, her eyebrows shooting up in open skepticism. Convinced that we had either Christmas-treed the bubble sheets at random just to get out before lunch or found some clever way to copy off each other right under Mr. Miller's nose, she pulled the laminated Form A and Form B plastic grading overlays out of her locked desk drawer and lined them up over our answer sheets right in front of us while Mr. Miller stepped over to check our written database normalization and scratch pads against the instructor key.

As Ms. Hollis's forefinger moved down the columns of green bubbles on Form A and then Form B, the smug expectation drained right out of her face.

"Ninety-nine percent on Form A," Ms. Hollis murmured under her breath, blinking twice before recounting the holes on Katie's overlay, "and ninety-eight percent on Form B."

Even more damning to her cheating theory, the single multiple-choice question I had missed on Booklet One wasn't even the same question Katie had missed on Booklet Three. Suspiciously, Ms. Hollis snatched up our two yellow scratch pads and held them side by side, hunting for identical handwriting, copied formulas, or matching erasures.

"Their scratch work is completely different from page one to the end, Ms. Hollis," Mr. Miller admitted quietly, pointing to our written SQL and HTML packets. "He solved all the hexadecimal conversions by factoring through base-two, and she solved hers by direct base-sixteen division. And on the written HTML and database section, they both got one hundred percent—in fact, on Question Forty-Two, Katie wrote a three-sentence citation in the margin explaining that the test key's 1996 HTML 3.2 syntax was officially deprecated by the World Wide Web Consortium in 1999, and she wrote out both the obsolete 1996 tag from our answer key and the modern CSS2 rule so we couldn't mark it wrong."

Katie folded her hands neatly on her lap tray and looked straight across the desk at Ms. Hollis.

"And while we're on the subject of Question Forty-Two, Ms. Hollis, that entire exam battery is an embarrassment," Katie said bluntly. "Whoever wrote those five booklets hasn't updated a single page since my brother and I were in seventh grade in 1997. Half the hardware questions were asking about 28.8k dial-up modems, 1.44-megabyte floppy disks, and ISA jumper pins, and the web design section tested HTML 3.2 font and blink tags that nobody in the real world has used in almost ten years."

"If we had handed in HTML 3.2 framesets and font tags like that at the Community College of Hampton Roads," I added dryly, "our professors would have failed us on the spot. At least once we're out of Vocational Evaluation and inside Mr. Alderman's Web Applications Developer lab on Monday morning, we'll be working in an actual 2007 computer classroom instead of filling out 1990s paper packets."

Pressing her lips into a thin, bloodless line, Ms. Hollis couldn't say a word in rebuttal. With two near-perfect answer sheets, forty pages of independent scratch work, and our Monday ultimatum hanging over her desk, she uncapped her pen, stiffly signed both of our one-week Vocational Evaluation completion forms, and cleared us to start the Web Applications Developer class on Monday morning.

## Saturday, March 10, 2007, 1:00 PM — Still Stuck on Level 2 in Barton Hall & Hating the Yellow Hall Pass Rule

"Look at this yellow slip of paper, Nate," Katie whispered in disgust on Saturday afternoon as we sat parked wheel-to-wheel in the Barton Hall lobby holding two signed yellow paper Level 2 Hall Passes while we waited by the glass doors for the Center recreation van. "We rushed the entire two-week Voc Eval in one single week so we could start our Web Applications Developer class on Monday morning, and we're still stuck right here in Barton Hall on Level 2!"

"Because moving over to Cantor Hall is part of earning Level 3, and nobody who's only been on campus for six days has been here long enough to earn Level 3, let alone Level 4," I muttered, looking down at the counselor's signature on my yellow paper pass. "Back home in Norfolk or Virginia Beach, if we want to go buy snacks or browse computer books at Chapters & Co., we just roll out our front door to the nearest CVTA bus stop, pay our fare, and go wherever we please like normal adults. Up here in Barton Hall, a Level 2 client can't even roll past the front gates on Roosevelt Avenue to catch the local VATS shuttle bus between Staunton, Fishersville, and Waynesboro without begging the dorm office for a signed yellow Hall Pass stating where we're going and what minute we'll be back."

"And if you don't want to wait two hours on the shoulder of Route 250 for the VATS bus on a Saturday," Katie added, gesturing toward the recreation clipboard hanging outside the lobby office where students had to sign up for chaperoned van trips to The Spot recreation hangout or the Waynesboro ValueMart, "you have to sign up for a chaperoned Center van ride! We will be twenty-two years old in four weeks, Nate. We have associate degrees, we run our own regional transit website, and we had to get a signed Hall Pass from a dorm counselor just to ride four miles down the road to buy laundry detergent and ginger ale at ValueMart—the exact store Ms. Hollis thought I should stand at the door and greet shoppers for!"

"And tonight makes our seventh night in a row being forced down separate hallways for the eleven-o'clock bed check," I whispered back, lacing my fingers tightly through hers across our armrests as the white Center van pulled up to the curb outside. "Let's see what Mr. Alderman's PHP class looks like on Monday morning, Katie—because I am already sick to death of this place."
