## Thursday, October 28, 2010, 8:15 AM — Five Days Before the Election: The Incumbent Attacks Katie's Wheelchair on WTGA 790 AM

"Turn the desktop speakers up a notch, Nate," twenty-five-year-old Katie said from her SwiftChair beside mine on the Thursday morning before the election, pausing her typing on our morning canvassing walk-sheets as the live web stream of conservative talk radio WTGA 790 AM—Tidewater Talk 790—crackled across our home office on Lord Nelson Drive. "The host just said Harold Dietz called in unscheduled."

I reached over and turned up the volume knob beside my monitor while taking a sip of cold chocolate milk.

For the past thirteen days since Greg Mercer's front-page Metro story in *The Tidewater Pilot* and the forty-minute switchboard takeover on WHRC 89.5 FM, Councilman Harold Dietz had vanished from public view—canceling two neighborhood civic-league appearances while our volunteer canvassing teams knocked on thousands of doors across Kempsville, Bellamy Manor, Brandon, and Larkspur. Now, with five days left before Tuesday's election, the ten-year incumbent had evidently decided to go live on Hampton Roads' loudest morning talk-radio microphone to try to stop the bleeding.

*"We're joined on the studio line right now by Virginia Beach City Councilman Harold Dietz, representing the Kempsville District,"* the WTGA morning host announced. *"Councilman, folks all over the city have been talking about that empty podium over at WHRC and the front-page story in the Tidewater Pilot about your twenty-five-year-old opponent catching three buses home at eight-twenty at night. Looks like you've got a real horse race on your hands out in Kempsville."*

When Harold Dietz's voice came through the broadcast compressor, he didn't sound like a calm ten-year incumbent. He sounded tight, defensive, and rattled.

*"Well, Mike, let's call that newspaper story what it was—a staged piece of political theater,"* Dietz snapped over the airwaves. *"The Tidewater Pilot loves a melodramatic human-interest angle, so they print a picture of my opponent rolling onto a bus at night as if riding a bus qualifies somebody to manage a one-point-seven-billion-dollar municipal corporation. Let's talk about the real world at City Hall that nobody at the newspaper wants to mention."*

Katie and I looked at each other across our wheelchair armrests, our fingers stilling on our keyboards.

*"First of all,"* Dietz ploughed ahead, his voice rising with irritation as he went completely off script, *"if my opponent were actually elected on Tuesday, do the taxpayers of Virginia Beach have any idea what City Hall facilities would have to do to the historic mahogany City Council chamber in Building One? They would have to tear out the woodwork on the side of the raised Council dais and spend tens of thousands of taxpayer dollars building a custom structural wheelchair ramp just to get one person up to a desk! And second, let's be honest with the voters about the demands of this job. Serving on the Virginia Beach City Council isn't a hobby you do from a laptop. We sit in six-hour afternoon budget workshops followed by six-hour evening sessions that run past midnight, plus regional commission meetings all over Tidewater. Are we really supposed to pretend that a twenty-five-year-old girl in a wheelchair—who has to have her twin brother rolling right beside her everywhere she goes just to get through the day—has the physical stamina to handle twelve-hour days on the City Council?"*

Inside our quiet home office in Kempsville, the silence lasted three full seconds.

Even over the radio broadcast, you could hear the WTGA morning host draw in a sharp, audible breath inside the Norfolk studio as he realized the incumbent councilman had just committed political suicide on live air in the largest military and veteran community on the East Coast.

*"Uh—Councilman Dietz,"* the WTGA host cut in quickly, his tone dropping an octave as the red warning lights on his studio call screener's board began flashing like a Christmas tree, *"hang on a second there, Harold. We have eighty thousand disabled veterans and wounded warriors living right here in Virginia Beach and Norfolk who use wheelchairs every day, and federal law under the Americans with Disabilities Act requires public buildings to be accessible anyway—"*

*"I'm just saying the voters deserve to know the practical reality and the cost, Mike!"* Dietz insisted defensively before the host hastily took the program to a commercial break—and when the show came back from the break three minutes later, Councilman Dietz was no longer on the line.

What followed over the next forty-five minutes on WTGA 790 AM was unlike anything we had ever heard on conservative talk radio.

The host didn't take a single call defending Harold Dietz—because there weren't any. Instead, caller after caller was a furious Navy chief, a retired Marine colonel, a wounded Iraq or Afghanistan veteran from Little Creek or Naval Air Station Oceana, or a lifelong conservative Kempsville homeowner calling in to blister the incumbent for attacking a candidate's wheelchair and mocking the bond between twin siblings who had lived with a spinal-cord condition since high school.

*"Mike, this is Senior Chief Petty Officer Miller calling from Bellamy Manor,"* a booming voice growled on Line One. *"I served twenty-four years in the Navy and I've voted Republican in every election since Ronald Reagan. My nephew lost the use of his legs in Anbar Province in 2007 and uses a power wheelchair just like Katie and Nate Prairie. For a sitting Virginia Beach City Councilman to go on the radio and complain about the cost of putting a wheelchair ramp on the City Council dais—and to insult a young woman's stamina because she uses a wheelchair and works alongside her twin brother—is the most disgraceful thing I've ever heard in this city. I walked out to my front yard five minutes ago, pulled Harold Dietz's sign out of my grass, and threw it in the trash can!"*

By noon on Friday, the fallout had turned into an avalanche. Three of Virginia Beach's largest business associations issued public statements revoking their endorsements of Councilman Dietz and clarifying that they stood for equal access for all citizens. When *The Tidewater Pilot* called our campaign phone asking for Katie's response to Dietz's radio remarks, Katie gave Greg Mercer a single, quiet two-sentence statement:

*"My brother Nate and I have used power wheelchairs since our legs gave out during our junior year at Northwood High School, and we have never once asked anyone in Virginia Beach for pity. All we have ever asked for is a ramp to the table and a bus that runs late enough for working people to get home."*

On Saturday morning, October 30—seventy-two hours before the polls opened—*The Tidewater Pilot* devoted the entire top half of its Editorial & Opinion page to a special full-width spread of reader letters under the banner headline:

*LETTERS TO THE EDITOR: DIETZ'S ATTACKS ON BUSES, WHEELCHAIR RAMPS CROSS THE LINE*

Sitting at our kitchen table with Dad over Saturday morning pancakes and orange juice, I read three of the printed letters aloud while Katie listened beside me:

> A Ramp to the People's Dais Is Not a "Special Favor"<br>
> As a retired Navy Master Chief who lost both legs above the knee to an IED outside Fallujah in 2006, I nearly drove my hand-control truck off Kempsville Road on Thursday morning listening to Councilman Harold Dietz complain on WTGA 790 AM about the cost of building a wheelchair ramp up to the City Council dais for Katie Prairie. When I came home from Bethesda Naval Hospital in a wheelchair, nobody told me I lacked the "stamina" to contribute to my community. If the mahogany dais in Building 1 doesn't have a wheelchair ramp after twenty years of the Americans with Disabilities Act, that isn't an indictment of Katie Prairie—it is an indictment of Harold Dietz and every politician who never thought a citizen in a wheelchair belonged up there.<br>
> — Master Chief Robert H. Callahan, USN (Ret.), Kempsville, Virginia Beach

> The 8:24 PM Bus Is Real Life, Not "Political Theater"<br>
> Councilman Dietz claims Katie and Nate Prairie catching the 8:24 PM Route 2 bus home from the WHRC debate was a "publicity stunt." Tell that to those of us who close the retail stores at Pembroke Mall and Glenrock Mall every night and pray our connecting Route 20 or Route 15 bus isn't five minutes late so we aren't stranded in the dark. Katie and Nate Prairie have ridden the bus with us in the rain and cold for five years. On Tuesday, the bus riders of Virginia Beach are going to send one of our own to City Hall.<br>
> — Denise T. Holloway, College Park, Virginia Beach

> We Remember the Day Their Legs Gave Out at Northwood High<br>
> Hearing Councilman Dietz mock Katie Prairie for having her twin brother Nate rolling beside her broke my heart and made my blood boil. I taught English at Northwood High School in September 2001 on the Friday afternoon when sixteen-year-old Katie and Nate's legs gave out within three hours of each other. Every teacher in that building watched those two teenagers refuse to give up, finish high school from hospital beds and wheelchairs, earn their degrees at CCHR and Fishersville, build their own software business, and buy their own home in Kempsville. Their bond isn't a weakness—it is the finest example of family courage in Virginia Beach.<br>
> — Margaret L. Sterling, Retired Teacher, Bayside, Virginia Beach

When I finished reading Mrs. Sterling's letter, Dad had to take off his glasses and wipe his eyes with his napkin, while Katie held my hand tightly across our armrests.

## Tuesday, November 2, 2010, 7:19 PM — The 94.1% to 5.9% Landslide & The Four-Station Election Night Post-Mortem

![Inside their Kempsville home on Election Night, November 2, 2010, 25-year-old twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs pressing their foreheads together and clasping hands in joyful tears beneath the television displaying Katie's 94.1% victory projection, embraced from behind by their proud father David.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/nov_2010_kempsville_election_night_94_percent_landslide_katie_nate_david.jpg)

At seven o'clock on the evening of Tuesday, November 2, 2010, the polls closed across the City of Virginia Beach.

We hadn't rented a hotel ballroom at the Oceanfront. Instead, Katie, Dad, and I were gathered inside our living room on Lord Nelson Drive along with sixteen of our volunteer sidewalk canvassers, CCHR students, and fellow CVTA bus riders, with platters of warm pizza and two-liter bottles of ginger ale, orange soda, and flavored water set out on the dining table and a fresh pot of black coffee brewing in the kitchen for Dad. Across the living room wall, I had connected four television monitors and my dual laptop screens to our custom PHP precinct-return engine, which scraped the live precinct-by-precinct XML data feed straight from the Virginia State Board of Elections and the Virginia Beach Voter Registrar every fifteen seconds.

At 7:08 PM, the first four Kempsville precincts reported their optical-scan tabulator tapes.

Dad leaned over my shoulder, staring at my PHP dashboard screen through his glasses. "Nate... check your parser code on Kempsville Precinct Three and Bellamy Precinct One. Is a decimal point shifted?"

"The parser checksums match the state XML feed to the single vote, Dad," I breathed, my heart hammering against my ribs as five more precincts popped onto the screen in bright green. "Look at the raw totals."

At 7:19 PM—just nineteen minutes after the polls closed—all four local television network affiliates in Hampton Roads (WCVB-TV 10 Coastal News 10, WTID-TV 3 NewsChannel 3, WVHR-TV 13 13News, and WHRC TV-15) flashed simultaneous breaking-news graphics across the bottom of their screens with a loud electronic chime:

*PROJECTION — VIRGINIA BEACH CITY COUNCIL (KEMPSVILLE DISTRICT):*
*KATIE PRAIRIE (WINNER) — 94.1%*
*HAROLD DIETZ (INCUMBENT) — 5.9%*

Inside our living room on Lord Nelson Drive, a roar went up that shook the framed blue butterfly print on the hallway wall. Dad wrapped both of his arms around Katie's shoulders and mine at the same time, his voice choked with pride, while Katie pressed her forehead against mine across our armrests with tears of pure joy shining in her blue eyes.

As the final precincts locked in over the next twenty minutes confirming the staggering 94.1% to 5.9% margin—the largest landslide defeat of a sitting incumbent in the modern history of Hampton Roads municipal politics—we unmuted the four television feeds and the radio stream to hear the stunned political analysts dissect what had just happened.

On WCVB-TV 10, Coastal News 10 anchor Don Roberts turned to Virginia Dominion University political science professor Dr. Quentin Marsh standing in front of the electronic precinct map.

*"Ninety-four point one percent to five point nine percent, Dr. Marsh,"* the anchor said, shaking his head at the big board. *"We have never seen numbers like this in a contested Virginia Beach City Council race. How does a ten-year incumbent get wiped out by eighty-eight points?"*

*"Don, what we just witnessed tonight in Kempsville is the textbook anatomy of a catastrophic one-two political knockout,"* Dr. Marsh explained, tapping the touchscreen map of Virginia Beach. *"Three weeks ago, before the WHRC debate, Harold Dietz was sitting on a comfortable eight-point incumbent lead mostly because voters didn't know who Katie Prairie was. Then came Punch Number One on October fourteenth: Dietz didn't even bother to RSVP to WHRC TV-15, leaving his podium empty on live television for forty-five minutes while Katie Prairie demonstrated complete command of the municipal budget—followed ten minutes later by Greg Mercer's front-page story in the Tidewater Pilot showing Katie and her twin brother Nate sprinting across six lanes of Hampton Boulevard in their wheelchairs to catch the 8:24 PM Route 2 bus so they wouldn't miss the last three-bus transfer chain home to Virginia Beach. We now know from campaign insiders that by the Monday after that story ran, Dietz's internal tracking poll had crashed from an eight-point lead down to a dead heat—48.2 percent to 47.8 percent, a razor-thin four-tenths of one percent margin right inside Virginia's automatic recount threshold—and his major Oceanfront donors threatened to freeze his final forty-thousand-dollar TV buy."*

Across the room on WVHR-TV 13, a live 13News remote camera panned across a cavernous, completely deserted ballroom at an Oceanfront resort hotel where untouched trays of shrimp cocktail sat under heat lamps beside a silent podium banner reading *RE-ELECT COUNCILMAN DIETZ*.

*"And that panic inside the Dietz camp led directly to Punch Number Two last Thursday morning on WTGA 790 AM,"* the WVHR-TV 13 political reporter told the studio anchors from the empty ballroom. *"Trying to look tough for his panicked donors, Councilman Dietz went on live talk radio, mocked the Prairies' three-bus ride home, complained about the taxpayer cost of building a wheelchair ramp up to the City Council dais in Building 1, and questioned whether a woman in a wheelchair rolling alongside her twin brother had the stamina to serve on Council. In a city filled with military families, disabled veterans, and working bus commuters, that radio call turned a 0.4 percent race into a 94 percent wipeout. The Councilman called Ms. Prairie three minutes ago to concede and left the hotel through a side kitchen door."*

Meanwhile, on WTID-TV 3 NewsChannel 3, the tech-desk correspondent pulled up a live graphic highlighting our grassroots ground game:

*"Don't overlook the engineering behind this victory, either,"* the WTID-TV 3 analyst noted. *"Katie and Nathaniel Prairie didn't spend a dime on outside political consultants. Nathaniel Prairie wrote their custom PHP voter-turnout and precinct-mapping software himself, and look at the precinct turnout bars along the CVTA Route 12, Route 15, Route 20, and Route 36 corridors—precincts that normally see 18 percent turnout in an off-year municipal election surged by 42 percent tonight because bus riders and working families came out in droves to vote for one of their own."*

Even over on conservative talk radio WTGA 790 AM, the evening election host sighed into his microphone as the 94.1% total scrolled across his screen: *"Folks, Harold Dietz dug his own grave right here on our morning airwaves last Thursday. When a twenty-five-year-old woman and her twin brother out-work you, out-debate you, ride three city buses home in the dark, and build their own software from scratch, attacking their wheelchairs in a Navy town is a guaranteed way to get sent into retirement."*

## January to May 2011 — The Building 1 Dais Ramp, Locking $810,000 of CVTA Perks in Escrow, & Taking Seat on the CVTA Commission

![At the top of the newly constructed red-carpeted mahogany wheelchair ramp on the Virginia Beach City Council dais in Building 1 on January 4, 2011, 25-year-old Councilwoman-Elect Katie Prairie sits in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair taking the secular constitutional affirmation of office on the Constitution of Virginia held by her twin brother Nate, with their father David standing proudly behind them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/jan_2011_vb_city_council_dais_ramp_secular_affirmation_katie_nate_david.jpg)

During the eight weeks between Election Night in November 2010 and Reorganization Night in January 2011, municipal carpenters and accessibility contractors went to work inside the City Council Chamber of Building 1 at the Virginia Beach Municipal Center.

Removing the steep wooden steps on the right wing of the curved mahogany City Council dais, the city constructed a permanent, gently sloped, red-carpeted wheelchair ramp bordered by polished mahogany handrails leading straight up to the Kempsville District desk, where the heavy leather swivel chair was rolled away so twenty-five-year-old Councilwoman-Elect Katie Prairie could pull her compact black SwiftChair directly up to the voting console.

On Tuesday evening, January 4, 2011, with the chamber gallery packed wall-to-wall with Kempsville neighbors, CCHR students, bus operators, and retired Master Chief Robert Callahan sitting in the front row in his own wheelchair, Katie rolled up the new mahogany dais ramp to her Kempsville District seat, inviting Dad and me onto the dais beside her solely for the ceremonial swearing-in while I held open our bound leather copy of the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia.

Neither Katie nor I believed in any religion or any god—and before the ceremony, Katie had politely notified the Clerk of the Circuit Court that under Article VI, Clause 3 of the United States Constitution, Article II, Section 7 of the Constitution of Virginia, and Section 49-9 of the Code of Virginia, she would be taking the secular constitutional affirmation on the Virginia Constitution rather than a religious oath on a Bible, omitting the optional parenthetical reference to a deity at the end.

Placing her left hand flat on the Virginia Constitution and raising her right hand, Katie repeated the constitutional affirmation of office as the newly elected Kempsville District Councilwoman in a clear, ringing voice that echoed to the back of the chamber:

*"I do solemnly affirm that I will support the Constitution of the United States, and the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia, and that I will faithfully and impartially discharge all the duties incumbent upon me as a member of the Council of the City of Virginia Beach, according to the best of my ability."*

As soon as the applause thundered across the chamber, Dad and I descended the ramp back to the front row of the public gallery so Councilwoman Katie Prairie could take her official seat alone at the Kempsville console on the dais.

She didn't wait six months to keep the promise she had made to Greg Mercer at the 8:24 PM bus stop on Hampton Boulevard.

Just two weeks later, on Tuesday evening, January 18, 2011, Item 9B came up on the formal Virginia Beach City Council agenda: a request from the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority for a $1.42 million mid-year supplemental municipal appropriation to cover administrative cost overruns at CVTA's Norfolk headquarters.

Stepping up to the low citizen and staff microphone podium on the chamber floor below the raised mahogany dais were two men in tailored charcoal suits whom Katie and I knew very well: CVTA President and Chief Executive Officer Leonard Sterling, and Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead—the exact two executives who, fourteen months earlier at 509 East Eighteenth Street, had cut off my public-comment microphone at the hundred-and-eighty-second buzzer, sneered in the hallway that we were *"statistically insignificant hobbyist bloggers,"* refused to release raw GTFS schedule files, and secretly blackballed me from the Transit Riders Advisory Committee.

Now, the roles were reversed. Sterling and Halstead were standing down at the floor microphone looking up at the raised mahogany dais—where twenty-five-year-old Councilwoman Katie Prairie sat in the elected seat of power looking down at them with her finger resting beside her microphone button, while I sat as a private citizen down in the front accessible bay of the chamber floor right beside the dais ramp with the PHP line-item audit I had prepared for her open on my MagnaBook Pro.

When CEO Sterling finished his five-minute presentation asking the City Council to approve the $1.42 million check with no strings attached, the Mayor turned toward the right wing of the dais.

"Councilwoman Prairie," the Mayor said, "you have the floor."

Katie pressed her white microphone button. The green indicator ring lit up—and this time, Richard Halstead didn't have a cutoff switch.

"Thank you, Mr. Mayor," Katie said, her voice calm, polite, and surgical as every camera in Building 1 zoomed in on her desk. "Mr. Sterling, Mr. Halstead, before the voters of Kempsville elected me to this Council by a margin of ninety-four point one percent, the two of you cut off my brother Nate's microphone at Eighteenth Street and told us in the hallway that CVTA doesn't take operational direction from 'hobbyist bloggers.' Now that you are asking the taxpayers of Virginia Beach for an extra one point four two million dollars, my brother and I have gone through every single line of your general ledger."

Down at the podium, Richard Halstead swallowed hard, a bead of sweat glistening at his temple under the television lights.

"Out of the one point four two million dollars you are requesting tonight," Katie continued, reading directly from the PHP audit sheet on her desk, "six hundred and ten thousand dollars goes toward fuel and frontline bus operator wages, which I fully support. The remaining eight hundred and ten thousand dollars, however, is earmarked for executive retention bonuses, out-of-state conference travel, fleet take-home sedans for senior staff, and proprietary scheduling consultant contracts. Meanwhile, here in Virginia Beach, the last Route 12 bus out of the CCHR hub still leaves at 5:48 PM, locking citizens out of our 6:00 PM Council meetings, and your agency still refuses to publish its raw General Transit Feed Specification database tables for the public."

Katie held up a two-page printed budget amendment she had drafted with the City Attorney that morning from our audit notes.

"Therefore, Mr. Mayor," Katie announced, "I move an amendment to Item 9B: the City of Virginia Beach will release the six hundred and ten thousand dollars for bus operators and fuel tonight, and we will lock the remaining eight hundred and ten thousand dollars of executive overhead in a restricted municipal escrow account. Not one penny of that eight hundred and ten thousand dollars will be released by the City Treasurer until CVTA meets two mandatory conditions: first, publishing its complete, unredacted GTFS schedule database online under an open public license within thirty days; and second, implementing the Evening Civic Access Transit Pulse—extending Route 33 out of the Virginia Beach Municipal Center until 10:30 PM on Council nights and operating a synchronized 9:00 PM and 10:00 PM evening connecting pulse for Routes 12, 25, 29, and 36 at the CCHR Virginia Beach Campus hub."

"Second!" called out At-Large Councilman Ronald Villa from the other side of the dais with a broad grin.

When the electronic voting board on the chamber wall chimed ten seconds later, all eleven lights glowed green: 11–0.

And the reckoning wasn't quite finished. Immediately after the budget vote, the Mayor called up Item 9C—the resolution appointing Virginia Beach's new municipal representatives to the governing board of the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority, the Transportation District Commission, to fill the seat vacated by former Councilman Harold Dietz.

By a second unanimous 11–0 vote, the Virginia Beach City Council appointed Councilwoman Katie Prairie as a voting Commissioner of the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority.

Nine days later, on the fourth Thursday of January 2011, while every other city council commissioner from across Hampton Roads drove into Norfolk and parked in the reserved VIP lot behind 509 East Eighteenth Street, CVTA's newest governing Commissioner arrived the exact same way she and I had always traveled: by riding a three-bus chain on CVTA—boarding Route 15 northbound on Military Highway, transferring to Route 20 westbound at Glenrock Mall into Downtown Norfolk, and riding Route 1 up Granby Street to Eighteenth Street while paying our regular fare.

When Katie and I rolled our SwiftChairs through the double doors of the Eighteenth Street boardroom, I parked at the front accessible citizen-advisor table while Commissioner Katie Prairie rolled straight up onto the raised Commissioners' horseshoe dais—right past Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead's chair. Under New Business, the very first official action Commissioner Katie Prairie took from the dais was exercising her unilateral prerogative as a Virginia Beach Commissioner to make a citizen appointment to the Transit Riders Advisory Committee.

"Pursuant to Section 4.2 of the Commission bylaws," Katie said clearly into her Commissioner's microphone while looking straight across the table at Richard Halstead, "as a voting Commissioner for the City of Virginia Beach, I hereby appoint Nathaniel Prairie to the Virginia Beach citizen seat on the Transit Riders Advisory Committee, effective immediately."

By May 2011, CVTA had published its complete open GTFS schedule feed and launched the permanent Evening Civic Access Transit Pulse across Virginia Beach. For the first time in the history of our city, any citizen who rode a public bus or used a wheelchair could attend a six o'clock City Council meeting, speak at the public microphone, and ride a CVTA bus safely home afterward.

Inside Building 1 at the Municipal Center, Katie also established a daily office habit that baffled the veteran city hall staffers down the corridor: whenever she was sitting at her desk and the main constituent telephone line rang, she didn't wait for a city clerk or a shared administrative receptionist to screen the call and take a paper message. In Katie's literal autistic mind, her name was on the door, the voters of Kempsville had hired her to do the job, and sitting three feet away from a ringing telephone without picking it up would be rude and dishonest. So whenever she could reach the receiver, she pressed the button herself and answered, "Councilwoman Katie Prairie's office, this is Katie speaking," so the caller knew she was actually at her desk working for them—regularly leaving Kempsville homeowners and retired shipyard workers speechless for two full seconds when they realized their own City Councilwoman had picked up the phone on the first ring.

And on every single Tuesday night when the Virginia Beach City Council adjourned at nine-thirty, Councilwoman Katie Prairie rolled down the mahogany dais ramp in Building 1 to meet me at the front of the gallery, and the two of us rolled out to the Municipal Center bus shelter alongside the citizens who had come to watch the meeting, swiped our thirty-day CVTA passes, and rode the 9:45 PM Route 33 and Route 12 pulse right back home to Kempsville.
