## Sunday, July 8, 2007, 11:15 AM — Why Dormitories Were Never Built for Us

![Inside the 1960s red-brick and terrazzo lobby of Barton Hall at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center on a lush summer morning on Sunday, July 8, 2007, twenty-two-year-old fraternal twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit in their collapsible manual wheelchairs outfitted with armrest joysticks, spoked push-rim wheels, and rear rubber push-handles holding hands and looking reassuringly up at twenty-year-old Wesley Sorenson—who holds his royal-blue silicone ring with tears on his cheeks while Heather Collins comforts him from behind—as their father David Prairie stands warmly beside a brass luggage cart and stacks of yellow Hall Passes rest on the wooden intake counter.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jul_2007_rrc_barton_hall_checkin_wesley_heather_david_katie_nate.jpg)

"Good timing—we beat the noon heat up Afton Mountain by ten minutes," twenty-two-year-old Heather Collins smiled as she clicked the power side-ramp of our silver accessible van back into place along the curb outside Barton Hall at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville, right behind where Dad had just backed his Dark Midnight Blue and Ultra Silver 1991 Crestline Garrison 350 pickup into an adjacent visitor space.

Since Dad lived up on Sunset Drive in Alexandria during the week for his systems-engineering job in Tysons Corner, Heather had driven Katie, twenty-year-old Wesley Sorenson, and me up from Virginia Beach that morning—stopping five minutes east down Jefferson Highway near the Waynesboro ValueMart first to pick up four room keycards for the ground-floor two-queen extended-stay kitchenette suite she had booked on her credit card for the next two weeks—while Dad drove down Interstate 66 and Interstate 81 from Northern Virginia so all five of us could get Katie and me settled in together.

"Welcome to Barton Hall, folks—clients check in on Sundays all the time, so come right on inside," a male residential staffer said, propping the aluminum-framed double glass vestibule doors open against the warm July mountain breeze. Katie and I guided our wheelchairs side by side across the threshold with our laptop backpacks slung over our canvas seatbacks, Heather and Wesley walked right beside my left elbow with Wesley turning his smooth royal-blue silicone ring calmly in his palm, and Dad rolled a brass luggage cart stacked with our suitcases behind us across the scuffed green-and-white terrazzo floor.

Walking down the wide cinder-block corridor beneath buzzing fluorescent ceiling troffers—four summers after the five-minute visit over in Bates-Harding when we had stopped short outside Redmond Hall and turned around on the spot—I felt Wesley step half an inch closer to Heather's sleeve as we approached the wooden intake counter.

"Here are their updated medical clearances and both emergency contacts," Dad told the Sunday residential coordinator, a middle-aged woman whose plastic badge read *Linda*, setting a manila folder onto her blotter. "First is me: my primary residence is on Sunset Drive near King Street in Alexandria, and my second number is our house on Arvin Road in Virginia Beach. Right underneath my name, please list Heather Collins here as their local emergency contact in the Shenandoah Valley—she and her cousin Wesley are staying five minutes down Route 250 toward Waynesboro for the next two weeks."

"Got Mr. Prairie and Miss Collins both entered on the top emergency sheet," Linda said, clipping the page into two green intake folders before glancing over the counter at our wheelchairs—the backup folding manual cross-brace frames with spoked push-rim wheels and rear rubber push-handles that Dr. Carter's seating clinic had outfitted with right-armrest joystick power-drive kits after Hurricane Isabel. "Your Tidewater counselor's note says you two left your full-size power chairs back in Virginia Beach?"

"Dr. Carter had these backup folding frames rigged with joystick drives for us," I explained, resting my palm on my controller. "Neither of us has the wrist or upper-arm strength to push manual hand-rims across a campus, so we still drive everywhere with the joysticks, and whenever we ride in a regular car, you just unclip the battery box underneath and fold the frame into a trunk."

"Which makes getting back and forth down Jefferson Highway super easy," Heather added warmly, resting a reassuring hand on my shoulder and smiling across at Katie. "I already put all four of our names—Heather, Wesley, Nathaniel, and Katie—on our two-queen extended-stay suite right on the Shenandoah Valley Transit Route 250 Connector line, and we packed two whole rolls of quarters in your desk bags since the bus is fifty cents a ride. It runs Monday through Saturday and doesn't run on Sundays or late at night—so I want both of you to promise me right now that if you don't feel comfortable in these dorms for any reason at all, and the Route 250 Connector isn't running, you'll call my cell phone anytime day or night so Wes and I can drive right over, pick you up, and let you sleep at the hotel."

Behind the wooden counter, Linda stopped writing and tapped her pen sharply against a thick four-inch stack of canary-yellow cardstock pads sitting beside two brass room keys.

"Hold on right there, Miss Collins—that is not how residential enrollment works here," Linda said firmly. "All newly arriving clients in Barton Hall start at Level 2. Level 2 clients are not permitted to simply walk off campus onto the Route 250 Connector bus or get into someone's car whenever they feel like it, and they certainly are not allowed to sleep off campus on weeknights. Anytime Katie or Nathaniel leaves these grounds, they must first obtain a signed yellow Level 2 Off-Campus Hall Pass from residential staff stating exactly where they are going, and they must be signed back inside Barton Hall before eight o'clock in the evening ahead of our mandatory eleven-o'clock flashlight bed checks."

Katie, Heather, Dad, and I all stared across the counter at the stack of yellow paper slips in disbelief—and before any of us could even process having to beg for a signed yellow permission slip at twenty-two years old, Linda slid the two tagged brass room keys into two separate piles on the counter.

"And here are your dormitory room assignments," Linda continued briskly. "Nathaniel, your bed is down the hall on the men's wing, and Katie, you are in Room 157 over on the women's wing. Under Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center residential policy, there is strictly no sharing rooms or visiting dorm rooms of the opposite gender."

Katie and I looked at each other across our armrests as that second announcement hit us like a bucket of ice water. Back home in Virginia Beach, when we had looked at the glossy brochure from the DARS office showing a two-bed college-style dorm room in Barton Hall, we had figured that since regular colleges only separate men and women so boyfriends and girlfriends don't have messy breakups—and since we were twin brother and sister who had been at each other's side since we were little and had never spent a single night under separate roofs in twenty-two years—they would let us share a room, put up with two Twin XL beds on paper, and curl up in our sleeping bag inside the dorm closet at bedtime.

Keeping my voice completely polite, I leaned forward in my chair toward the counter.

"Excuse me, ma'am—I think there might be a misunderstanding," I said politely. "Katie and I aren't a boyfriend and girlfriend who might break up; we're fraternal twins—brother and sister. We thought since we're family, we'd be able to share one of the two-bed rooms. We've been at each other's side since we were little, we've lived together every single day of our lives—all through earning our associate degrees at the Community College of Hampton Roads—and whenever one of us gets overwhelmed at night, the other one is right there."

"I understand you're brother and sister, Nathaniel, and that you're used to sharing a home," Linda replied in a practiced, unyielding voice, tapping the highlighted paragraph on the housing sheet, "but this is a state residential facility, not an off-campus house. We keep men and women on separate wings because we can't have male and female clients in the same dorm rooms hooking up behind closed doors."

Katie and I blinked at her, both of our literal autistic brains stalling on the slang phrase.

"Hooking up?" Katie asked politely, tilting her head. "Like hooking up a computer monitor? What does 'hooking up' mean?"

Linda paused, her cheeks coloring slightly as she looked across the counter at the two of us and lowered her voice a notch. "It means having sex, Katie."

Katie and I looked at each other and then looked straight back at Linda in complete earnestness. Ever since sitting side by side in Family Life Education classes back in Virginia Beach public schools, both of us had known as a strict, memorized rule that brothers and sisters are not supposed to have sex with each other—a rule we obeyed one hundred percent literally even though neither of us had ever really understood why strangers assumed a dark closet sleeping bag had anything to do with sex.

"Oh!" Katie said matter-of-factly. "Well, we're brother and sister—we know brothers and sisters aren't supposed to have sex with each other, so of course we won't have sex!"

"Yeah, of course we won't," I agreed earnestly, nodding right alongside my twin sister.

Linda stared at the two of us for a second, momentarily flustered by how plainly we had just said that across the intake counter, before clearing her throat and falling right back on the rulebook.

"Be that as it may, nobody shares a room across the gender line here, and opposite-gender room visitation between six and eleven in the evening is an earned privilege reserved exclusively for Level 4 clients over in Cantor Hall," Linda said firmly. "You two are welcome to spend time together in the dorm lobby, the outdoor courtyard, the recreation hall, or the dining hall—just not inside the dorm rooms proper. Once your suitcases are unpacked, you will each sleep on your own wing."

Right beside my wheelchair, a sudden, ragged hitch of breath cut through the quiet lobby.

I turned my head—and my chest squeezed tight.

Standing under those buzzing fluorescent cinder-block lights five feet from the yellow pass pads, twenty-year-old Wesley had tears spilling straight down both cheeks. Wesley didn't need a single policy binder explained to him to understand what he was hearing: he knew in his bones that Katie and I stuck together twenty-four hours a day *just like he and Heather stuck together*, and being back inside Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center had brought the awful memory of Redmond Hall four Julys ago—when another clipboard stranger had talked about locking him behind heavy doors away from his Safe Person—rushing straight back over him on our behalf. Gripping his blue silicone ring tightly in his right hand, Wesley reached his left hand down onto my shoulder, his chin trembling as he cried openly for Katie and me.

"Hey, Wes—shh, breathe with me, buddy, I've got you," Heather murmured immediately, stepping right in behind Wesley to wrap both arms warmly around his shoulders and rub a slow, steady circle over his chest until his breathing stopped hitching. "Nobody is locking anyone behind Redmond Hall doors today, Wes, I promise. Remember? You and I are staying together down the road, and Katie and Nate have their phones right in their pockets."

Katie and I immediately pivoted our wheelchairs inward toward Wesley, Katie reaching across to hold my hand while I reached up to clasp Wesley's wrist gently beside my shoulder.

"Look at us, Wes—we're going to be okay, buddy, we promise," I told him gently, keeping my voice calm and steady just for him even though my own stomach was knotted tight.

"We promise we'll be okay, Wesley," Katie echoed softly, giving him the warmest, most reassuring smile she could muster. "Nate and I still get to stay side by side in the lobby, at meals, and all day in class, and if anything goes wrong, we've got Heather's number on speed dial."

Seeing both of us looking calmly up at him and promising we would be all right, Wesley let out a long, shaky breath against Heather's forearm, wiped his cheek on the back of his sleeve, and gave a single earnest nod while Dad rested a quiet, grateful hand on Wesley's shoulder.

As Linda stepped around the intake counter to walk us down the corridors, however, the male residential staffer who had propped the front door lingered by the office threshold, looked down at the rubber push-handles sticking out the back of Katie's folding frame, and reached both hands straight for her grips without asking as if he were about to wheel her down the hallway himself.

Katie clicked her joystick controller off, slammed both manual wheel-locks forward with a sharp metal *clack*, whipped her head around, and went straight after him with an aggressive edge in her voice that she almost never used back home.

"Get your hands off my wheelchair right now!" Katie barked across the lobby. "Do not touch my push-handles! Do I look like a piece of luggage to you?! Our chairs have joysticks on the armrests for a reason—my brother and I drive ourselves, and if you grab my chair without my permission again, you and I are going to have a serious problem!"

"Hey—watch your tone with residential staff, young lady," the staffer bristled, jerking his hands back from her rubber grips while his face turned red.

"Watch your hands around my sister's wheelchair!" I shot right back, guiding my own chair flush alongside Katie's while Dad stepped half a pace forward beside the luggage cart with a hard, warning look in his eyes.

Linda held up a hand between the counter and the doorway, frowning sharply at the two of us. "Katie, Nathaniel, I'm going to let that slide with a verbal warning since you two just came through the door and I know check-in day is stressful, but aggressive outbursts toward staff are a write-up offense here at Roosevelt."

Neither Katie nor I apologized. Instead, both of us fixed the staffer by the doorway with identical, disgusted looks—staring him straight in the eye with an unblinking glare that amounted to a flat-out dare: *go ahead and write us up right now if you want to.*

The staffer swallowed, looked away from our twin glares, and shoved his hands into his pockets without touching his clipboard.

With Linda walking beside our group down the men's corridor first so we could unload our bags under her one-time check-in escort, Katie, Heather, Wesley, and Dad helped me stow my clothes and laptop bag onto my side of the narrow cinder-block two-bed room—where my assigned roommate was already lounging on the opposite mattress watching us in silence—before we rolled across the building to Room 157 on the women's wing to help Katie unpack alongside her new roommate, who greeted all five of us warmly.

As soon as the last suitcase was stowed under Katie's bed and Katie and I rolled back out to a quiet corner of the Barton Hall lobby ahead of the others, Katie steered her wheelchair flush against mine and leaned close to my ear.

"I noticed your roommate was staring at my tits the entire time I was helping you unpack," Katie said quietly, her jaw tight.

"I noticed it too, and I wasn't thrilled about it for a second," I answered, my blood simmering as I put my arm around my sister's shoulders. "Your roommate back in Room 157 seemed like a genuinely nice person, whereas I think I got stuck with a complete jerk of a roommate."

"No offense, Nate, but your roommate gave off a really bad first impression," Katie sighed, before turning in her seat and pulling me into a tight, reassuring hug so I knew she didn't blame me for who the housing office had put in my room.

A moment later, Dad, Heather, and Wesley walked over from Linda's counter—Dad shaking his head wryly as he held up two freshly signed canary-yellow paper slips just so his twenty-two-year-old son and daughter were "allowed" to go eat Sunday lunch with their own family.

"Got your official yellow permission slips right here so nobody calls out the National Guard while I buy my kids lunch," Dad said dryly, wrapping an arm around Heather's and Wesley's shoulders as we rolled out into the warm July sunshine. "Come on—let's go sit down together in Waynesboro for a real Sunday meal before I have to head north toward Alexandria."

## 3:00 PM — House Guard 2 in the Dorm Lobby, Calling Heather & Wesley, Cafeteria Rations, & The 11:00 PM Curfew Check

"I wish they had an actual lap pool here instead of just the small therapeutic pool, but oh well," Katie sighed at three o'clock that afternoon after Heather and Wesley had dropped us back off at Barton Hall and returned our signed lunch passes to the desk, peering through the interior observation glass of the campus recreation hall at the quiet, steamy natatorium before glancing down at her wristwatch as her stomach gave an audible rumble. "I'm already hungry again and it's only three o'clock, even after that big lunch Dad bought all five of us in Waynesboro."

"Look at the board games over on that table—half the boxes are missing their lids, and the TV in the lounge is turned up way too loud," I said, turning my chair beside hers. "Want to head back across the walkway to the Barton Hall lobby?"

"Please," Katie nodded, rolling out the automatic doors beside me into the warm summer air.

We parked wheel-to-wheel on the far side of the Barton Hall lounge chairs, well out of earshot of the front desk, and sat quietly for a minute holding hands across our armrests.

"If it weren't for that silly opposite-gender dorm rule, I'd say let's just head back to one of our rooms and close the door," I muttered, looking toward the two separate wing doors at opposite ends of the lobby. "What do you think, Katie? Heather said she and Wes can drive back over right now if we want to skip the dorms."

Katie looked down at our joined hands, thinking of twenty-year-old Wesley wiping the tears off his cheeks in this exact lobby four hours earlier after we had looked him in the eye and promised him we would be okay—and of why we had come up the mountain after June's failed interview in the first place.

"Let's at least give the dorms an honest try first, Nate," Katie said quietly. "We came all this way for the Web Applications class, and we promised Wesley we'd be okay. Let's call Heather right now so she and Wes aren't sitting by the phone worrying about us."

Pulling out my flip phone right there in the corner of the lounge, I dialed Heather's cell. She answered on the first ring from their extended-stay suite down Route 250 and put us right on speakerphone so Wesley could hear both of our voices at the same time.

"Hey, Heather—hey, Wes," I said warmly as Katie leaned in beside my shoulder. "Everything's quiet over here, and Katie and I talked it over: we're going to at least give the dorms a fair try tonight and get through Monday morning's start."

"We're sitting right side by side in the Barton Hall lounge about to watch a movie on Nate's laptop, Wes!" Katie added cheerfully into the phone, and over the speaker we heard Wesley let out a relieved, happy two-note hum while Heather thanked us warmly for calling and reminded us her ringer was staying turned all the way up on the nightstand just in case.

Once we pocketed my phone, I unzipped my backpack, pulled out a two-way stereo headphone splitter, and plugged both of our headsets into the audio jack of my laptop. Sliding a DVD into the drive, we queued up the brownstone booby-trap sequence of one of our favorite childhood Christmas comedies, *House Guard 2: Lost in Manhattan*—right where the kid stands on the rooftop throwing bricks down at the two bumbling burglars.

Within two minutes, both Katie and I were shaking with laughter across our armrests—laughing so hard that half a dozen other students sitting around the dorm lobby looked over and asked what on earth was so funny. Grinning, Katie hit the rewind button back to the scene where the two bandits were robbing the toy-store cash register, unplugged the headphone splitter so the sound played through my laptop speakers, and angled the screen toward the lounge so the other students could crowd around and laugh right along with us.

When the sequence wrapped up at quarter to five, we shut down the DVD player and zipped our laptops back into our bags.

"How about we roll inside the courtyard together for some fresh air," Katie suggested, lacing her fingers through mine, "and then we'll split up for two minutes just to drop our backpacks off in our rooms, wait for each other right outside the wing doors, and head to dinner together?"

"Deal," I said, giving her a quick hug before we parted at the hallway junction.

When I unlocked my door on the men's wing and rolled inside to set my backpack on my desk chair, my roommate looked up from his pillow with a sleazy smirk. "Hey, man—who was the hot blonde babe in here with you earlier?"

I didn't dignify him with a single word. Locking my desk drawer, I spun my wheelchair around in the narrow aisle, rolled straight back out to the courtyard walkway where Katie was waiting for me, and told her verbatim what he had just said.

"Gross," Katie said, curling her lip in disgust as we turned toward the covered breezeway at five-thirty with the stream of residential students heading to the main cafeteria. "If he walks past us tonight, hold my hand so he gets it through his thick skull not to talk to me."

Inside the noisy dining hall, we picked up two plastic trays, balanced them across our lap trays along the stainless-steel rail, and steered over to a small table for two near the wall.

"Looks like burgers and french fries," Katie said, picking up a limp, lukewarm fry from her plate. She took one bite and made a face. "That tastes nasty!"

At the next table over, a veteran student in a faded hoodie snorted around his plastic fork. "You two must be new here. Get used to your rations."

"Well," I sighed, poking dubiously at my gray hamburger patty, "the food definitely isn't the greatest, but what choice do we have?"

As soon as we dumped our trays at the dish window and rolled back out into the warm summer dusk toward Barton Hall, Katie reached across our armrests and took my hand, giggling softly under her breath. "Just in case your creepy roommate is watching from the sidewalk—I want it to look like we're dating."

"Where to now?" I asked as the front doors of Barton Hall slid open. "Dorm lobby?"

"Yeah," Katie said softly, her smile fading a little as she looked toward the separate hallway doors. "Until it's time for bed. I miss living together back home."

We parked in our quiet corner of the lobby, where I wrapped my arm around her shoulders and Katie snuggled against my chest with a long yawn. "We'll have breakfast together first thing in the morning," I promised, resting my cheek against her hair, "and then we're staying right at each other's side through every minute of evaluation."

By nine o'clock, both of us were nodding off in our chairs. Tearfully, we hugged each other goodbye in the middle of the lobby before reluctantly turning down our separate wings for the night.

When I rolled into my room on the men's wing, the bed across from mine was empty. Relieved to have the room to myself, I changed into my pajamas, locked my wheelchair brakes beside the frame, and transferred under the stiff institutional blanket—lying awake in the dark waiting for the eleven-o'clock curfew check.

Five minutes past eleven, the hallway door clicked open and a night staffer shone his flashlight inside, pausing when the beam hit the empty mattress across from mine.

"Where's your roommate?" the staffer asked.

"I haven't seen him at all tonight," I said from my pillow. "And considering the sexually suggestive things he was saying about my sister earlier today, I really don't want to see him right now."

The staffer stepped inside and lowered his clipboard. "What did he say?"

"Right after I finished unpacking, he asked me, 'Who's the hot blonde babe?'" I replied. "And earlier, while my sister was helping me unpack, she noticed him staring at her breasts."

"What's your sister's name?" the staffer asked, uncapping his pen.

"Katie. Katie Prairie. Room 157," I replied.

When my wristwatch alarm beeped at quarter to seven on Monday morning, I looked across the narrow room and saw my roommate passed out face-down on top of his bedspread in his street clothes, reeking of stale beer and marijuana. Keeping silent so I wouldn't wake him, I got dressed, transferred into my wheelchair, and rolled straight out to the Barton Hall lobby, where Katie came through the women's wing doors less than a minute later and wrapped her arms around my neck.

Before we could head out the front doors toward the cafeteria, the weekday morning dorm supervisor—a tall, no-nonsense woman with steel-gray hair—stepped out of the residential office and beckoned to the two of us.

"Nathaniel, Katie, step into my office for thirty seconds," she said, closing the door behind our wheelchairs. "I wanted both of you to know before you head to breakfast that Nathaniel's roommate is being expelled and escorted off the property by Campus Police this morning. At eleven-fifteen last night, night staff caught him trying to sneak past the fire doors into the women's dormitory wing to pressure another female client into sleeping with him, and just like you, Katie, that young woman wanted nothing to do with him and turned him straight in because he wouldn't stop harassing her. Between that, his prior write-ups, and staggering back onto campus intoxicated after curfew, he is done here. You have that room to yourself, Nathaniel."

Katie let out a long breath beside me, her shoulders dropping two inches in pure relief as we thanked the supervisor and rolled back out into the morning hallway together.
