## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 6:00 PM — The Evening News in Richmond and Norfolk

"Hand me the pepper shaker, Nate," Katie said at six o'clock that Thursday evening, setting two warm plates of baked chicken and rice down on the dining table of Apartment 104 at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts on West Broad Street after we had ridden the 5:35 PM westbound JRTC Route 6 bus home from Ninth and Broad. "And turn on the television so we can see the weather forecast for the weekend."

We had spent the entire afternoon from two until five-thirty back in Katie's fifth-floor office in Room 518 of the General Assembly Building, meeting with special-education parents from Virginia Beach and reviewing coastal flooding amendments with our legislative aide, with both of our laptops closed during the meetings.

Picking up the remote control from the corner of the table, I clicked on the wall-mounted television—tuned first to the Richmond local six-o'clock news broadcast, with my laptop open beside my glass of ginger ale streaming our hometown six-o'clock broadcast from Norfolk so we could keep up with Hampton Roads news while we were in Richmond for the winter session.

I barely had time to set the remote down before both my twin sister and I froze with our forks halfway to our mouths.

On the television screen on the wall—and simultaneously on the Norfolk local news stream playing on my laptop—was the exact ninety-five-second video clip I had captured from my wheelchair bay on the House Floor at lunchtime.

*"—and our top story from the State Capitol tonight,"* the Richmond evening anchor announced over the footage of Katie sitting upright in her compact SwiftChair on the House Floor holding up the two printed pages of House Bill 663, *"a fiery floor exchange in the House of Delegates this afternoon over House Bill 663, the controversial bathroom bill restricting public restrooms by 'anatomical sex,' after Virginia Beach Delegate Katie Prairie brought the House Chamber to a standstill with a single question for the bill's patron."*

Before our eyes, both the Richmond station and the Norfolk station aired the floor clip in full—every word of Katie asking Delegate Martin Cobb why she, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove she had female parts just to use the ladies' room, followed by the four seconds of Delegate Cobb standing frozen and beet-red behind his desk microphone before the Majority Leader hurriedly passed the bill by for the day.

Katie stared at the television screen, her blue eyes wide. "Nate... they're playing the whole floor clip on the six-o'clock news. In Richmond and back home in Norfolk."

I reached over and woke my laptop browser window, refreshing the server dashboard for Katie's legislative archive and The Grid.

My jaw dropped.

"Katie, look at the traffic logs," I said, turning the laptop screen toward her wheelchair. "The Capitol beat reporters from the Richmond and Norfolk papers embedded your captioned clip on their blogs at two-thirty this afternoon while we were in constituent meetings, and late this afternoon the national politics desks in Washington and New York picked it up. Because the open captions were burned right into the video frame, millions of people are watching it on their phones and sharing it coast to coast!"

## Friday, February 5, 2016, 9:15 AM — The Speakerphone Call on Line One, Two Twin Meltdowns, & The Majority Leader's Nephew

The black multi-line telephone on Katie's desk in our fifth-floor General Assembly Building office rang once on Line One at nine-fifteen on Friday morning, and Katie reached over from her SwiftChair to press the speakerphone button, fully expecting it to be a retired shipyard worker from Kempsville calling to report a missed CVTA bus connection on Princess Anne Road.

"Delegate Katie Prairie's office, this is Katie speaking," she said, keeping one hand resting on her open municipal transit binder while I sat right beside her left wheel with my silver MagnaBook Pro laptop open on the side table.

There was a brief, startled pause on the other end of the speakerphone. "Oh! Good morning, Delegate Prairie. I honestly didn't expect you to pick up your own main line. My name is Arthur Vance, and I'm the Senior Booking Producer for Meet the Nation at GBS News up here in Washington. I hope I'm not catching you at a bad time?"

Katie blinked, glancing sideways at me in surprise. Even though Katie was thirty years old and had just begun her second two-year term representing the Eighty-Fifth District after two quiet winters as a backbench minority member in Room 518, neither of us had ever dealt with the national political press in our lives. To us, Thursday afternoon's ninety-five-second exchange with the gentleman from Spotsylvania over House Bill 663 had been a Virginia statehouse floor fight over bad statutory drafting. Neither of us had the faintest clue how Washington network booking wars worked, or why a national Sunday show producer would be calling a junior state delegate from Virginia Beach.

"Not at all, Mr. Vance," Katie said politely. "My brother Nate and I were just looking over some bus shelter amendments. How can I help you?"

"Well, Delegate, David Vance and our entire editorial team here in Washington watched your exchange on the House floor yesterday afternoon," Mr. Vance said warmly, his voice remarkably courteous and unhurried. "Your floor video just crossed ten million views nationwide, and the constitutional questions you raised about how a law like House Bill 663 could ever be enforced in public buildings without violating the Fourth Amendment are leading the national conversation this morning. David would be honored if you would join him this Sunday morning at nine o'clock for a quiet, one-on-one conversation in our opening A-Block to walk through the text of the bill—if you'd be willing to make it a Sunday morning exclusive with Meet the Nation."

Katie and I looked at each other across the two-inch gap between our armrests.

Neither of us knew what Washington television producers meant when they asked for an exclusive, or that politicians in Washington spent years lobbying to get onto the opening A-Block of Meet the Nation. To Katie and me, Meet the Nation was simply the quiet Sunday morning news program our dad David used to put on the living room television in Virginia Beach while the three of us ate scrambled eggs and toast together.

Even better, to Katie's autistic mind, the word exclusive sounded like a wonderful relief: it meant she would only have to put on a stiff blazer, sit under bright studio lights, state the literal facts of House Bill 663 once, and then go straight back to our apartment in Richmond without having to repeat the exact same sentences to anyone else.

"Do you mean if I talk to Mr. Vance at nine o'clock on Sunday morning, I only have to explain the bill one time and nobody else will ask me to repeat it all afternoon?" Katie asked literally.

On the speakerphone, Arthur Vance let out a soft, delighted laugh, though neither of us understood why. "That is the exact agreement, Delegate Prairie. Just you and David Vance one-on-one at nine o'clock sharp, zero panel cross-talk, and you're completely done by nine-twenty. We would be happy to send a network limousine down to your address in Richmond at five-thirty Sunday morning to drive you up Interstate 95—"

"Oh, you don't need to waste money on a limousine, Mr. Vance," Katie said right away. "My brother Nate and I always ride public transit and pay our own regular fare, and a car would just get stuck in traffic on the 14th Street Bridge anyway. The suburban Staples Mill station doesn't have Sunday bus service yet, so we'll take the early JRTC bus down Broad Street to Richmond Main Street Station, ride the six-oh-five ARTS Northeast Regional train up to Alexandria, and use our blue SmartPass cards from our family trips with our dad to take the Capital Transit Yellow Line across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge into Washington. We'll be at your studio desk by eight-thirty—and then we can catch the eleven-twelve train out of Alexandria with the connecting ARTS Thruway shuttle back to Main Street Station so I can get back to our apartment and out of my dress blazer by two o'clock."

"You two are taking a city bus, an intercity train, and the Yellow Line subway across the Potomac?" Mr. Vance asked, sounding genuinely charmed. "Well, whatever makes you most comfortable, Delegate. We'll have two security passes waiting for you and your brother at the Washington bureau front desk at eight-thirty Sunday morning. Thank you so much for giving us your word."

"Thank you for being so polite, Mr. Vance," Katie said, and pressed the button to end the call.

Only as the speakerphone clicked off at nine-twenty-two did Katie and I look down at the plastic telephone console on her desk.

While we had been talking cleanly on Line One for the last four minutes, the little red hold lights on Line Two, Line Three, Line Four, and Line Five had all started blinking furiously at the exact same time, though the ringer had been automatically muted while the speakerphone was active.

Before I could even ask Katie why the whole console was lit up like a Christmas tree, our office door flew open. Katie's twenty-four-year-old legislative aide stumbled in carrying a thick stack of pink paper message slips, followed two seconds later by Greg, the Communications Director for the Virginia House Democratic Caucus, who was holding two vibrating cell phones in his hands and breathing like he had just sprinted all the way up the stairs from the third floor.

"Katie! Do not touch that phone!" Greg gasped, leaning over the front edge of her desk. "Every national network booking desk in New York and Washington just saw your floor clip pass ten million views! Capitol Sunday is holding on Line Two, This Sunday is on Line Three, Inside Politics is on Line Four, and the national cable networks are blowing up my cell phone! Every Sunday morning show in America is fighting over you right now!"

Katie stared up at him from her SwiftChair, her eyebrows knitting together in complete confusion as she looked from the blinking red lights on her telephone over to me.

"Why are they fighting over me?" Katie asked, genuinely baffled. "I'm a state delegate from Virginia Beach, and House Bill 663 is a Virginia bill. Why would four television shows in Washington want me?"

"Because you're the biggest national political story in the country this morning!" Greg said, waving both cell phones in the air. "Do you have any idea what a gold mine this is?! Most members of Congress wait ten years just to get invited onto one of the big Sunday morning network shows! We can set up a full Sunday morning sweep right now—all four networks back-to-back from eight in the morning until noon—"

"Oh, no thank you," Katie said calmly, shaking her head. "I would hate sitting in a stiff blazer repeating the exact same thing four times in a row. Besides, I already promised Mr. Vance on Line One that I would only talk to Meet the Nation."

The room went dead silent.

Greg stared down at Katie, then over at my wheelchair, his mouth opening and closing without a sound.

"You... you answered your own phone and gave an exclusive to Meet the Nation?" he whispered, looking as if Katie had just casually announced she had traded a gold bar for a pack of gum. "Katie, do you have any idea how prestigious Meet the Nation is?! Or what kind of leverage we could have used with the networks?!"

I looked up from my laptop keyboard, just as confused by his theatrical panic as Katie was. "Why would we need leverage? Mr. Vance called while we were looking at CVTA bus stops. He was the first one to ask, he was very polite to Katie, we used to watch that show with our dad over breakfast when we were kids, and he told Katie that if she goes on at nine o'clock, she only has to explain the bill once and then we can catch the eleven-twelve train out of Alexandria and come home."

"Yeah," Katie agreed simply. "Why would anyone want to go on four television shows on a Sunday morning when you can explain the statute once at nine o'clock and be back in your apartment in sweatpants by two in the afternoon?"

Greg slowly lowered both of his ringing cell phones to his sides, staring at the two of us in sheer, stunned disbelief. "I... I have to go tell the other three networks you're off the board," he muttered, backing out of our office doorway into the outer reception room.

As he pulled the heavy oak door behind him, the latch didn't quite catch, leaving a two-inch gap between the frame and the jamb.

Through that crack, Katie and I heard every single word as Greg snatched up the blinking phones at the legislative aide's desk in the outer room and started barking into the handsets back-to-back.

"Look, Sarah, I'm telling you, I can't put her on Capitol Sunday!" we heard him shout into Line Two, his voice loud and exasperated. "She's a backbench delegate from Virginia Beach who has never dealt with national press in her life! She picked up her own main office line five minutes ago before my staff could even get up the stairs and promised an exclusive to Meet the Nation! She had no idea what she was doing!"

Beside me, Katie's hands froze on the edges of her transit binder.

Out in the reception room, Greg slammed down Line Two, punched Line Three, and started all over again. "No, I'm not freezing This Sunday out on purpose! Delegate Prairie doesn't know how Washington booking rules work—she literally answered her own phone and gave away a Sunday morning exclusive without clearing it with Caucus Communications or the Minority Leader's office! Now I have to go down to the third floor and explain to leadership and the entire Democratic Caucus what just happened!"

The outer hall door banged shut as he hurried down the corridor toward the stairs.

Inside Room 518, the silence felt suffocating.

I looked over at my twin sister, and my stomach dropped right through the floor. Katie's fingers were trembling violently against the yellow highlighter in her lap, her breathing was coming in shallow, rapid gasps, and every trace of color had drained out of her face.

For two autistic twins who had spent our whole lives relying on literal written rules to survive, there was no phrase on earth more terrifying than unwritten rules. Whenever someone in authority started shouting about protocols that weren't printed in any handbook—about failing to clear things with leadership or breaking rules nobody had ever told us existed—it sent every alarm bell in our nervous systems screaming at once.

"Nate..." Katie whispered, her voice shaking so badly she could barely get my name out as her chest began to heave. "Did I just break a House rule? Was I not allowed to talk to Mr. Vance on the telephone without asking the Caucus first?"

"I—I don't know, sis," I stammered, and the raw panic in my own voice immediately set off a feedback loop between the two of us. My own hands were shaking so hard on the keyboard of my laptop that I could barely click open the PDF of the Rules of the Virginia House of Delegates, my eyes darting frantically down the table of contents for anything about national television interviews or caucus clearance while Katie's breathing grew faster and louder beside me. "It's not in the written standing rules on my laptop, Katie, I swear it isn't, but the way Greg just ran downstairs to report you to the Minority Leader..."

Katie burst into tears, a full-blown autistic meltdown overtaking her as she buried her face in both hands and began sobbing and shaking from head to toe.

"What if they kick me out of the Caucus, Nate?!" Katie cried, gasping for air between sobs as her whole body trembled in her wheelchair. "What if I broke an ethics rule and they expel me from the House of Delegates?! All the voters in Virginia Beach trusted me, and Dad was so proud of us, and I ruined everything just because I answered the telephone!"

Seeing my twin sister in a full autistic meltdown—and spiraling straight into a sympathetic meltdown of my own as the terrifying thought seized my chest that Katie was really about to be kicked out of the House of Delegates because neither of us understood some secret Richmond rule—I released the electromagnetic brakes of my SwiftChair with a trembling hand, wheeled myself flush against the side of her chair, and wrapped both of my arms around her shoulders, hugging her as tight as I could across our armrests.

Katie pressed her wet face into the shoulder of my charcoal suit jacket, sobbing uncontrollably against my lapel while my own tears spilled hot down my cheeks. I held onto my sister with everything I had, resting my chin against the top of her blonde hair while my chest shook right alongside hers and my heart hammered at a hundred and thirty beats a minute, staring in terror at the office doorway and waiting for the Sergeant-at-Arms or the Minority Leader to walk in and tell us her career was over.

Three minutes later, brisk footsteps echoed down the fifth-floor hallway.

The office door swung open, and in walked the House Minority Leader himself, with Greg trailing half a step behind him.

As I clutched Katie tighter against my chest, trembling from head to toe and bracing for the blow, the Minority Leader took one look at the two of us huddled together in our power wheelchairs—Katie sobbing into my shoulder in full meltdown, and me crying and shaking right along with her like I was trying to shield my twin sister from an oncoming train—and stopped dead in his tracks.

"Whoa, whoa, hold on a second," the Minority Leader said softly, the color draining from his own face as he realized what had happened. He spun on his heel and shot a furious glare at the communications director—"Step outside in the hall, Greg. Right now"—before walking quietly into our office and pulling the wooden guest chair over in front of our footrests so he was sitting at eye level with us.

Katie lifted her tear-stained face an inch off my shoulder, gasping for breath and wiping furiously at her red eyes with the back of her trembling wrist as she tried to sit up in her chair.

"Mr. Leader, I am so sorry," Katie choked out through her tears, her whole frame still shaking against my arms. "I didn't know there was a rule that a delegate wasn't allowed to answer Line One or agree to talk to a television show without asking you first! When Mr. Vance asked for an exclusive, I only said yes because I thought it meant I could explain House Bill 663 one time and come home on the eleven-twelve train! Please don't kick me out of the House of Delegates—I'll call him back right now and cancel it!"

"Please don't expel her, sir," I pleaded, my own voice cracking as my hands shook on Katie's shoulders. "I searched the whole House Manual on my laptop and we didn't know—"

The Minority Leader stared at Katie and me, and his face softened with deep, unmistakable remorse.

"Expel her? Kicked out of the House?" he repeated gently, holding up both hands palm-out in a calm, steady gesture. "Katie, Nate—look at me. Take a slow breath, both of you. You are not getting kicked out of the House of Delegates, you are not getting kicked out of the Democratic Caucus, and you did not break a single rule. Not a written rule, and not an unwritten rule."

My grip on Katie's shoulders loosened by half an inch as I blinked through my tears at him. "We... we didn't? Then why was Greg yelling in the outer office that Katie had no idea what she was doing and that he had to report what she did to you and the whole Caucus?"

"Because Greg is a hyperactive press staffer who treats every five minutes like a four-alarm fire, and he had no business talking like that outside your door," the Minority Leader said firmly. He leaned forward, resting his elbows on his knees as he looked straight into Katie's wet blue eyes. "Katie, you are the constitutional officer elected by the voters of Virginia Beach. Not Greg, and not me. Nobody in this building owns your voice, and you never need permission from a single soul in Richmond to answer your own telephone or accept an interview."

Katie swallowed hard, letting out a long, shuddering breath as her hand still clutched my jacket lapel. "Then... why did he say I didn't realize what I did by giving an exclusive to Meet the Nation?"

A warm, reassuring smile broke across the Minority Leader's face. "Because what you did without even trying, Katie, is the single biggest triumph our Caucus has had in ten years. Meet the Nation is the number-one Sunday morning public affairs broadcast in the United States. Every President, every U.S. Senator, every Governor, and every corporate CEO in America watches that show at nine o'clock on Sunday morning. Usually, when a state lawmaker goes viral on a Thursday, the networks try to stick them in a noisy three-minute cable panel at the back of the hour. Because you picked up your own phone and politely agreed to give Arthur Vance your only interview of the morning, he gave a Virginia Beach delegate the solo nine o'clock A-Block lead at the very top of the broadcast."

I blinked, looking from the Minority Leader over to Katie, whose violent trembling was finally starting to slow against my arm.

"Wait," I whispered, the crushing vise around my ribs finally beginning to loosen. "So when Greg told the other delegates down on the third floor what Katie did..."

"We were cheering, Nate!" the Minority Leader laughed gently, reaching into his breast pocket and handing Katie a clean, folded white handkerchief. "The only people upset this morning are the booking producers at the other three networks who called three minutes too late—and that's their problem, not yours. Give me sixty seconds to deal with Greg in the hallway."

He stood up, stepped out into the fifth-floor corridor, and pulled our heavy oak door firmly shut behind him—though through the solid wood, the marble acoustics of the hallway still carried the low, blistering lash of his voice as he backed the communications director up against the wall by the stairwell.

"Have you lost your absolute mind, Greg?!" we heard the Minority Leader snap in the corridor. "Do you have any idea what you just did in there? Both of those twins are on the autism spectrum, they take every word you say completely literally, and when you stood in her outer office yelling into the phone that she broke booking rules and failed to clear an interview with leadership, you threw both of them into a full panic thinking she was about to be expelled from the House of Delegates!"

"Sir, I wasn't trying to scare them, I was just trying to manage the Sunday network requests—" Greg stammered weakly in the hall.

"You work for the members of this Caucus, Greg—the members do not work for you!" the Minority Leader barked. "Delegate Prairie's name was on the ballot in Virginia Beach, not yours. She just single-handedly handed us our biggest floor victory of the session yesterday afternoon, and she doesn't need your permission to pick up her own telephone! Before noon today, you are going to walk back in here, look both Delegate Prairie and Nate in the eye, and apologize for acting like a lunatic outside her door. Do I make myself clear?"

"Yes, sir," Greg muttered, his footsteps retreating fast down the marble stairs.

Before the Minority Leader could turn back toward our door, the brass elevator doors down the hall chimed open. Measured footsteps approached down the corridor carpet, and a second, deeper voice spoke up—one with a calm Southside Virginia drawl that both Katie and I recognized from the front desk of the Republican aisle on the House Floor.

It was the House Majority Leader.

"Everything alright up here on the fifth floor, Tom?" the Republican Majority Leader asked quietly outside our door. "My clerk came down from the committee room a minute ago and said your press director was raising a ruckus outside Delegate Prairie's office and had those two kids in tears."

"I just handled Greg," the Minority Leader sighed in the hallway. "Katie answered her own main line at nine-fifteen and gave Meet the Nation a Sunday morning exclusive, and Greg went into a staffer meltdown in her reception room shouting about unwritten rules and reporting her to leadership. Sent both Katie and Nate into an absolute tailspin thinking she was about to be kicked out of the House."

Out in the corridor, the Republican Majority Leader let out a low, disgusted breath.

Ten seconds later, a polite two-knuckle knock sounded on our office door. When I called out softly to come in, both the Democratic Minority Leader and the Republican Majority Leader stepped inside Room 518 together.

Katie and I were still parked flush side by side, my right wheel touching her left wheel, my left arm still wrapped around her shoulders, and Katie holding the white handkerchief in her lap. Seeing the two most powerful floor leaders from both parties standing in our office at the same time made both of us sit up straight in our SwiftChairs.

"Morning, Delegate Prairie. Morning, Nate," the Republican Majority Leader said gently, taking off his reading glasses and tucking them into his breast pocket so there was nothing formal or adversarial about his posture. "I hope you two don't mind me stepping in for a minute with Tom."

"Good morning, Mr. Majority Leader," Katie said quietly, her voice steady again even though her eyes and mine were still red and damp around the edges.

"Tom just filled me in on the commotion out in the hallway," the Majority Leader said, looking warmly at both of us. "Now, you and I sit on opposite sides of that green carpet downstairs, Delegate, and lord knows you gave my side of the aisle a rough afternoon yesterday on House Bill 663. We're going to disagree on plenty of votes while you're in Richmond. But my thirteen-year-old nephew is on the autism spectrum—he's actually in the eighth grade in middle school and serving right downstairs on the House Floor this session in his navy blazer as a House Page, learning about state government for the very first time—and I know firsthand how overwhelming it is when folks in a building like this start shouting about unwritten rules that aren't in the handbook."

Katie blinked in surprise, looking from the Majority Leader over to me as the last of the tension finally drained out of our shoulders. We had seen the thirteen- and fourteen-year-old middle-school House Pages hurrying across the green chamber carpet in their navy blazers every afternoon carrying bill copies and messages for Delegates, and knowing that one of those middle-school Pages was the Majority Leader's own autistic nephew changed the entire feeling in the room.

"When my nephew started his first week as a Page in January, I gave him one piece of advice, and it's the only rule you and Nate ever need to know in this building," the Majority Leader continued, pointing a finger toward the floor beneath our wheels. "Every single unwritten rule you hear a staffer or a lobbyist talk about in the hallways of Richmond is completely made up. The only rules that govern a member of the Virginia House of Delegates are the Constitution of Virginia and the printed Manual of the House on your desk—and I know for a fact you two have already read every page of both."

"Twice," I admitted quietly, a small, relieved smile finally tugging at the corner of my mouth.

"I figured as much," the Majority Leader chuckled softly. "Which means whenever some overcaffeinated press staffer starts hollering because you answered your own phone instead of playing Washington media games, you two didn't do a doggone thing wrong—he did. In fact, between you, me, and Tom, half my own caucus wishes the gentleman from Spotsylvania had asked you two to proofread his bill before he embarrassed the whole Commonwealth on television yesterday."

Across from him, the Democratic Minority Leader laughed out loud, and for the first time since nine-twenty that morning, Katie let out a genuine, relieved laugh too, wiping the last trace of tears from her cheek.

"And just so we're clear on the bipartisan record," the Majority Leader added with a kindly wink as he turned toward the door, "if you two are taking the six-oh-five train out of Main Street Station on Sunday morning to go talk to David Vance in Washington, don't let anyone in this building bother you all weekend. Safe travels up the tracks, Delegate—and I'll see both of you at noon on Monday when we strike House Bill 663 off the calendar for good."

As the two floor leaders stepped back out into the corridor together, Katie looked over at me across our touching armrests, the color finally returning to her cheeks as she reached out and laced her fingers tightly through mine.

"Okay," Katie breathed softly, resting her forehead against my shoulder for one quiet second before picking up her yellow highlighter again. "No unwritten rules. Just the Constitution of Virginia, the House Manual, and the six-oh-five train out of Main Street Station."

"And two Business Class wheelchair bays with our names on them," I said, pulling our bank card out of my wallet and clicking the confirmation button on my laptop screen to reserve the only two integrated wheeled-mobility spaces on Sunday morning's ARTS Northeast Regional Train 164 from Richmond Main Street Station to Alexandria, along with the eleven-twelve southbound return on Train 143 and its connecting ARTS Thruway 8443 shuttle—paying full regular fare out of our own pockets just like we always did.

## Saturday, February 6, 2016, 11:15 PM — The Studio 8H Tip-Off & The "Anatomical Verification Checkpoint" Cold Open

Because our Sunday morning alarm was set for four-thirty so we could catch the early JRTC Route 6 bus down Broad Street to Main Street Station, Katie and I planned to turn in early on Saturday night.

By eleven-fifteen on Saturday evening inside Apartment 104 at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts, our royal-blue and charcoal blazers and pressed slacks were already laid out for the morning, our reloadable blue plastic SmartPass cards for Capital Transit were tucked into our wallets, and our two SwiftChairs were parked side by side near the bedroom wall outlet. I was just reaching for the remote control on the table to shut off the living room television when Katie's cell phone buzzed once, then twice, then five times in rapid succession.

Katie picked it up from her lap tray and blinked at the stack of incoming text messages from three different Capitol beat reporters and the Minority Leader's chief of staff.

"What's going on?" I asked, pausing with my thumb over the power button on the remote. "Did Delegate Cobb pull the bill already?"

"No," Katie said slowly, reading the messages off the glowing screen. "A producer in New York just tipped off the Richmond press corps about the eleven-thirty dress rehearsal in Studio 8H. On the exact same GBS television network where I'm appearing tomorrow morning, their late-night comedy broadcast just scrapped its planned Washington cold open. Everyone is telling us to leave the television on right now because House Bill 663 is the star of the eleven-thirty cold open."

I stared at my sister for two seconds, then burst out laughing and backed my SwiftChair right up beside hers in front of the television. "Well, four hours of sleep and a ninety-minute train ride to Alexandria is plenty. Let's watch!"

Five minutes later, at eleven-thirty sharp, the network chime rang out and the broadcast faded up on a painted replica of a Virginia State Capitol hallway in New York.

Right beside a mahogany door marked *LADIES ROOM* stood a cast member wearing a khaki state trooper uniform with an oversized foam campaign hat, a clipboard, and a yellow carpenter's tape measure clipped to his belt, sitting behind a folding card table with a hand-painted poster board sign reading:

> COMMONWEALTH OF VIRGINIA<br>
> ANATOMICAL VERIFICATION CHECKPOINT<br>
> HAVE YOUR BIRTH CERTIFICATE OR FIFTY DOLLARS READY

Beside the folding table stood a second cast member wearing a gray hairpiece and a rumpled navy suit with an oversized name badge reading *DELEGATE FROM SPOTSYLVANIA*.

"Good evening, fellow Virginians!" the actor playing the Spotsylvania delegate declared earnestly into the camera, dabbing sweat off his forehead with a pocket handkerchief while the New York studio audience immediately started laughing. "Now, I know a lot of folks in the news media got the wrong impression this week when Delegate Katie Prairie asked me on the House Floor how my new bathroom bill, House Bill 663, is actually going to work. People are saying, 'Wait a minute, is the Commonwealth of Virginia really going to make women pull their pants down for a government official just to use the ladies' room at Virginia Dominion University or the Community College of Hampton Roads?' And I am here tonight to assure every tourist, college student, and business owner in America that we have worked out a totally normal, non-creepy system!"

He gestured proudly toward the actor in the state trooper uniform, who blew a silver referee whistle as a female cast member carrying a shopping bag walked up to the restroom door.

"Hold it right there, ma'am!" the fake trooper barked, flipping open his clipboard. "Before you enter this state-owned facility to wash your hands, pursuant to House Bill 663, I need to verify your physical anatomy for the Virginia Literary Fund! Did you bring your original hospital birth certificate with the raised county seal?"

"To go to the bathroom at the DMV?" the woman asked, blinking in disbelief. "No! Why would I carry my birth certificate in my purse?"

The fake trooper gasped and looked over at the Spotsylvania delegate. "Uh-oh, Delegate! No birth certificate in the hallway! What's Step Two in Line 18 of your bill?"

The actor playing the Spotsylvania delegate froze, his eyes going wide under the studio lights as he pulled a twenty-foot-long accordion scroll of paper out of his suit pocket and squinted at it in panic.

"Well—uh—hang on, let me check Line 18!" he stammered, his face turning beet-red as the audience howled. "We don't—nobody is suggesting we look! Wait—Officer, just ask her a trick question only a biological woman from Virginia would know! Ask her what shade of autumn cardigan she's wearing!"

"Actually, Delegate," the woman said dryly, pulling a crisp fifty-dollar bill out of her wallet and slapping it onto the card table, "if the options in Spotsylvania County are handing a state trooper my birth certificate, answering questions about my pants, or paying fifty bucks to buy schoolbooks for the Virginia Literary Fund, here's fifty dollars—because clearly Spotsylvania County needs all the schoolbooks it can get!"

The studio audience erupted into cheers and applause as the actor playing the delegate stared helplessly into the camera lens for four silent seconds—mimicking Delegate Martin Cobb's exact four-second freeze-frame from Thursday afternoon's House Floor broadcast—before dropping into the folding chair behind the card table and muttering, "I pass the bill by for the day, Mr. Speaker—and Live from New York, it's Saturday Night!"

In our living room on West Broad Street, I was laughing so hard my shoulders shook, while Katie sat staring at the opening saxophone montage with both hands pressed over her warm cheeks.

"And in nine hours," I grinned, clicking off the television so we could climb into our shared sleeping bag sanctuary for four hours of sleep before the alarm, "you get to take the train up to Washington and finish that bill off for good."

## Sunday, February 7, 2016, 6:05 AM to 2:05 PM — Train 164 from Main Street Station, The Charles R. Fenwick Bridge, & Live on Meet the Nation

![Inside the GBS News Washington bureau studio on Sunday morning, February 7, 2016, 30-year-old Delegate Katie Prairie sits in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair in a wide camera frame in front of an illuminated Virginia State Capitol backdrop for a live national interview on Meet the Nation, while twin brother Nate monitors the broadcast from his SwiftChair beside the studio confidence monitor.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/feb_2016_gbs_richmond_studio_meet_the_nation_live_interview_katie_nate.jpg)

At five-fifteen on Sunday morning, while the rest of Richmond was still fast asleep in the February dark, Katie and I rolled out onto West Broad Street in our winter coats over our broadcast suits, caught the early Sunday morning eastbound JRTC Route 6 bus down the hill into Shockoe Bottom, and took the station elevator up to the historic train shed at Richmond Main Street Station.

At six-oh-five on the dot, the bright headlamp of ARTS Northeast Regional Train 164 swung around the curve from the Peninsula Subdivision and glided alongside the high-level platform. We rolled side by side into the warm Business Class car at the rear of the consist, engaged the electromagnetic brakes of our SwiftChairs inside the two integrated mobility bays we had reserved on Friday morning, and traded the February chill for the quiet hum of the floorboard heaters. Ten minutes north of the station, I rolled up to the café counter at the end of the car and came back carrying two cold cans of ginger ale—included complimentary with our Business Class tickets—and set one in Katie's cupholder.

For the next hour and forty minutes, as the winter sun rose pale pink and gold over the Rappahannock River at Fredericksburg and the Potomac creeks along Quantico, nobody asked Katie about House Bill 663, nobody shoved a microphone in her face, and nobody recognized us. We were just Katie and Nate again, riding the rails together the way we loved best.

At seven-fifty sharp, Train 164 pulled to a stop at Alexandria Union Station beneath the white stone spire of the George Washington Masonic Memorial.

Rather than staying onboard for the slow twenty-eight-minute crawl through the freight switches into Washington Union Station, we detrained onto the brick platform at Alexandria with the station attendant's portable lift, followed the flat brick walkway a hundred and fifty feet straight into the King Street–Old Town Capital Transit station, and pulled our blue plastic SmartPass cards out of our wallets—the exact same reloadable farecards we had carried on weekend family trips to Washington with Dad when we were growing up in Virginia Beach.

We tapped our SmartPass cards against the circular target pads on the wide accessible faregate, rode the elevator up to the island platform, and rolled straight onto a waiting northbound Yellow Line train.

Twelve minutes later, just past the Pentagon tunnel portal, our silver railcar burst out into the bright morning sunlight high above the Potomac River on the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge.

Directly to our right across the shimmering gray water, the northbound highway lanes of Interstate 395 on the 14th Street Bridge were backed up for half a mile behind an early-morning fender bender and a highway maintenance truck—the exact traffic jam a network limousine would have been stuck idling in right then. Out here on the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge, our Yellow Line train flew across the river at fifty miles an hour with a clear view of the white marble dome of the Jefferson Memorial and the Washington Monument rising against the crisp Sunday sky.

"Told you the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge beats the 14th Street Bridge every single time," I said, grinning across the aisle of the railcar as we glided smoothly into the L'Enfant Plaza portal in downtown Washington.

"Every single time," Katie agreed, tapping her SmartPass card out at the transfer station as we headed up to the GBS News Washington bureau with twenty-five minutes to spare.

When we rolled through the security glass of the Washington broadcast bureau at eight-thirty-five, Arthur Vance nearly dropped his coffee mug when he realized Katie had actually come all the way from Richmond on a city bus, a Business Class intercity train, and a Yellow Line subway car across the Potomac without a single staffer or chauffeur in tow.

Inside the main studio, the floor crew had cleared away the standard swivel guest chair and projected a high-resolution panorama of Thomas Jefferson's white-columned Virginia State Capitol across the curved LED wall directly behind Katie's tape mark, while the large flat-screen studio monitor to the side glowed blue with the program banner reading *MEET THE NATION — DEL. KATIE PRAIRIE*. When a well-meaning stage manager asked if Camera One should crop the frame tight around Katie's shoulders so viewers wouldn't focus on her wheelchair, Katie shook her head politely and rested her hand on her joystick armrest.

"Keep the frame wide enough to show my chair, please," Katie told him warmly. "A lot of people watching across the country this morning have never seen a legislator in a power wheelchair on a Sunday morning show, and I don't hide who I am."

Five minutes before air, the floor director clipped the miniature broadcast microphone to the lapel of Katie's royal-blue blazer and fitted the clear plastic IFB earpiece into her right ear, then guided me to my spot right beside the studio confidence monitor just outside the wide camera lens—completely out of the broadcast frame so there was zero confusion about who held the constitutional office, yet close enough that Katie had a clear, unobstructed sightline to me the entire time.

Underneath the glowing *MEET THE NATION — DEL. KATIE PRAIRIE* studio monitor, I engaged the electromagnetic brakes of my SwiftChair and opened my silver MagnaBook Pro across my lap tray. On my screen sat the exact statutory text of House Bill 663 alongside a list of the fourteen Fortune 500 corporate headquarters located in the Commonwealth of Virginia.

"Thirty seconds to live, Delegate," the stage manager called out softly as the iconic brass orchestra fanfare of Meet the Nation echoed through the studio monitors.

Katie rested her right hand calmly near her joystick controller and her left hand on her armrest, looking briefly over at me. I gave my twin sister a steady nod from behind my laptop screen.

The red tally light atop Camera One flared bright red.

"If it's Sunday, it's Meet the Nation," moderator David Vance's voice resonated across the studio and into millions of living rooms from coast to coast. "Joining us live in our Washington studio this morning—after riding the early morning train up from Richmond Main Street Station and the Yellow Line across the Potomac—is Virginia Delegate Katie Prairie of Virginia Beach, whose blistering ninety-five-second exchange on the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates over House Bill 663 has been viewed more than twelve million times since Thursday afternoon, and was even immortalized last night in the cold open on late-night television. Delegate Prairie, welcome to Meet the Nation."

"Good morning, David," Katie said evenly, keeping her eyes focused straight into the glass lens of Camera One. "Thank you for having me—and the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge was wide open all the way across the river."

"Delegate, let's start with that moment on the House Floor on Thursday," Vance said, as the broadcast aired the ninety-five-second floor clip side by side with our live studio feed. "You held up the printed text of House Bill 663 and asked the bill's patron why you, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer just to use the ladies' room at a community college, a public university, or the State Capitol. Critics on the other side of the aisle claim you were being deliberately theatrical to embarrass the majority. Did you plan that line to go viral?"

"Not at all, David," Katie replied, her voice calm, clear, and completely literal. "Anyone who knows my twin brother Nate and me back home in Virginia Beach and Norfolk knows I don't do political theater. We write computer software for a living, and when a bill is placed on our desks in the House of Delegates, my job as a constitutional officer is to read every single word literally—because the courts and the police have to enforce the literal words we put into the Code of Virginia, not whatever talking point a politician gives at a press conference afterward."

Out of the corner of her eye, Katie glanced toward my chair as I tapped the touchpad of my laptop beside the studio monitor.

"And when you read Line 18 on Page 1 and Line 54 on Page 2 of House Bill 663 side by side," Katie continued smoothly, "the bill applies to every public building, public school, and public college campus owned or leased by the Commonwealth. It defines compliance not by an identification card or how someone lives their daily life, but strictly by what it calls 'a person's anatomy,' and it authorizes any law enforcement officer to issue a fifty-dollar civil summons for the Literary Fund. As someone who has used a power wheelchair full-time since I was sixteen years old—and who had to pull out a steel tape measure before my freshman session just to get an ADA-compliant restroom stall wide enough to close the door in privacy inside our own State Capitol complex—I asked the gentleman from Spotsylvania that question on Thursday because I wanted a literal, legal answer to a basic Fourth Amendment problem: since police officers standing in the hallway of the State Capitol, Virginia Dominion University, or the Community College of Hampton Roads do not carry a filing cabinet of citizens' birth certificates, how can an officer prove a citizen's physical anatomy in General District Court without conducting an invasive physical search?"

"And as millions of Americans saw on Thursday—and again in parody last night in New York—the patron couldn't answer you," Vance noted. "Instead, the Majority Leader had to pass the bill by for the day. What are you hearing from Virginia's business community and House leadership ahead of tomorrow's noon session?"

"What I can tell you is that employers, universities, and families across Hampton Roads, Richmond, and Northern Virginia are deeply alarmed," Katie said clearly. "Virginia is home to the largest naval base in the world at Norfolk, major shipbuilding and port facilities, world-class public universities like Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute and Virginia Dominion University, and fourteen Fortune 500 corporate headquarters. Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and none of those employers—and none of the working families I represent in Virginia Beach—sent us to Richmond to waste taxpayer money turning police officers into fifty-dollar bathroom inspectors or turning our Commonwealth into a national punchline. We have coastal flooding to mitigate, public transit systems to expand, disability Medicaid waitlists to clear, and classrooms to fund."

"One final question before we let you go, Delegate Prairie," Vance said, his tone warming with genuine respect. "A lot of viewers across the country are meeting you for the very first time this weekend. You entered public life after fighting to fix public transit schedules in Hampton Roads, and now you've single-handedly brought one of the most controversial bills in the country to a dead halt. Do you have your sights set on higher office down the road—perhaps the State Senate or even here in Washington?"

Just off-camera beneath the studio monitor, I smiled faintly above my laptop keyboard.

"Right now, David, my only focus is noon tomorrow in the building right behind me," Katie said, gesturing slightly toward the glowing image of the Virginia State Capitol behind her SwiftChair, "and catching the eleven-twelve ARTS train out of Alexandria with my brother so we can get back to Richmond this afternoon. Once we make sure House Bill 663 is permanently laid on the table where it belongs, I have transportation and accessibility amendments to pass for the people of Virginia Beach."

"Delegate Katie Prairie of Virginia Beach, thank you for joining us this morning," Vance said.

By nine-eighteen, the red tally light atop Camera One clicked off and the floor director unclipped the miniature microphone from the lapel of Katie's royal-blue blazer.

Arthur Vance walked out onto the studio floor holding his cell phone with wide eyes alongside two fresh mugs from the green room. "Delegate Prairie, our control room phones are already lighting up from Capitol Hill to Richmond, and the Associated Wire just put out an urgent alert from the Virginia State Capitol—House leadership just announced they are sending House Bill 663 back to the General Laws subcommittee tomorrow morning to strike it from the docket before the noon floor gavel even falls! You're welcome to relax in the hospitality suite as long as you like, and whenever you're ready to head back to Virginia, our driver can bring the town car around."

"Thank you, Mr. Vance, but we really do have that eleven-twelve train to catch at Alexandria," Katie said politely, handing her clear plastic earpiece back to the audio technician.

She had been sitting upright in that stiff royal-blue broadcast blazer, button-down collar, and tailored dress slacks since four-forty-five in the morning on four hours of sleep, and under the hot studio lights her sensory limit was nearly maxed out. Waiting around until three-thirty-eight in the afternoon for the direct one-seat Peninsula train back to Main Street Station would have meant spending four more hours trapped in formal interview clothes.

At nine-twenty-five sharp, I closed my silver laptop, tucked it into my bag, and rolled out the front doors of the Washington bureau right beside my sister.

We rolled straight back to the Capital Transit station, tapped our blue SmartPass cards at the wide accessible gate, and rode a southbound Yellow Line train right back out into the bright morning sun across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge. By ten-fifteen, we rolled off the King Street–Old Town platform, crossed the brick plaza to the bakery opposite Alexandria Station to grab two hot egg-and-cheese croissants for the road, and boarded the Business Class car of southbound ARTS Northeast Regional Train 143 at eleven-twelve on the dot.

As the train glided south past Quantico and Fredericksburg, Katie's cell phone buzzed nonstop inside her bag with texts from delegates, reporters, and national political producers. She didn't answer a single one. She leaned her head back against her headrest, sipped her cold ginger ale from the café car, and watched the winter pine trees blur past the window while I held her hand across the aisle.

At one-oh-two in the afternoon, Train 143 pulled to a stop at the suburban Staples Mill station in Henrico County.

Waiting right outside the station doors in the reserved charter lane was the white lift-equipped ARTS Thruway 8443 connecting motorcoach with *RICHMOND MAIN STREET* glowing on its amber destination sign. Because our Business Class tickets were booked through to Main Street, the driver already had the hydraulic wheelchair lift unfolded and waiting on the curb before we even rolled through the sliding glass doors.

In less than ten minutes, the driver secured both of our SwiftChairs into the floor tracks of the coach, raised the lift, and pulled straight out onto Interstate 64 East toward downtown. By one-thirty-five, the Thruway shuttle dropped us right beneath the historic clock tower at Richmond Main Street Station, where we caught the Sunday afternoon westbound JRTC Route 6 bus straight up Broad Street to Boulevard.

At five minutes past two—while the political panels in Washington and the Governor's staff in Richmond were still replaying her nine o'clock interview on every screen—I unlocked the front door of Apartment 104 at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts.

Katie rolled straight into the bedroom, unbuttoned that stiff royal-blue television blazer, hung it up in the closet, and traded her starched collar and dress slacks for her softest gray cotton sweatshirt and fleece sweatpants. Five minutes later, after I had ditched my charcoal suit and tie for an old Virginia Beach t-shirt and athletic pants, we parked our SwiftChairs side by side near the living room window overlooking West Broad Street, kicked up our footrests, and enjoyed the quietest Sunday afternoon in Richmond.

The next morning, Monday, February 8, just before the noon floor session convened at the Capitol, the House majority recommitted House Bill 663 back to the General Laws subcommittee room downstairs to dispose of it without forcing their members to cast a recorded roll-call vote on the Floor.

With television cameras crammed shoulder-to-shoulder along the side walls, the subcommittee chairman called House Bill 663 up and didn't even allow debate.

"Is there a motion to lay House Bill 663 on the table?" the chairman asked briskly.

"So moved," a majority member muttered without looking up from his desk.

"All in favor of laying the bill on the table say aye—all opposed say no—the ayes have it, and House Bill 663 is laid on the table!" the chairman announced in a single breath, banging his gavel to kill his own party's bathroom bill on an unrecorded voice vote before anyone could demand a roll call.

Standing by the lectern as his bill died in nine seconds flat—alongside an equally furious Delegate Robert Marsh, who was already muttering under his breath that he would bring his own "Physical Privacy Act" back next session—Delegate Martin Cobb glared down the dais at his own party's members, gathered up his folders, and snapped into the open air as he stormed out the door:

"I'm going to pray that you all get courage!"

At the accessible end of the room, Katie watched the bill's patron stomp out into the hallway, turned her head toward my wheelchair, and gave me the quietest, proudest twin smile in the world as we reached across our armrests and squeezed each other's hand.
