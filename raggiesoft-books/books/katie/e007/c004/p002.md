## Saturday, August 18, 2007, 1:30 PM — Turning My Old Bedroom Closet Into Our Networking Closet

"All right, Chief Technology Officer—lower intake louver is screwed flush, upper exhaust cutout is clean, and every speck of drywall dust is in the shop-vac," Dad announced cheerfully at half past one on Saturday afternoon, setting his cordless drill onto a folded towel beside the open closet doorway of our twelve-by-twelve home office on Arvin Road before wiping his forehead with his sleeve. "I'm going to step out to the garage and blow the sawdust off the jigsaw blade before your sister catches a single flake near her keyboard. Don't wire the hot leg on that relay until you double-check the pinout diagram."

"Already pulling up the schematic, Dad," I grinned from my compact black SwiftChair at the right half of our twelve-foot span of light-oak desks, turning a small gray digital thermostat controller and a solid-state relay module over on my desk mat beside my precision screwdriver.

Three feet to my left, where neat Velcro bundles of blue Category-6 patch cord lay coiled across her lap tray, Katie clicked her label maker twice, peeled a crisp white strip reading *ETH-04 — STAGING LINUX*, and wrapped it cleanly around the boot of a patch cable.

"Listen to the difference in here already just having the server tower tucked behind the closet jamb, Nate," Katie said happily, pausing for two seconds to tilt her head toward the quiet room. "For five months we've had four case fans humming right beside our ankles eight hours a day, and neither of us realized how tired our ears were until today."

Looking over at the open three-by-six alcove three feet to my right made my whole chest swell with quiet, geeky satisfaction.

Because Katie and I slept every night across the hallway inside the carpeted three-by-six closet of Katie's old bedroom—leaving our navy-and-slate-blue queen bed out in the main room purely for afternoon reading and folding warm laundry—the matching three-by-six closet right here inside my old high-school bedroom had been sitting empty ever since we turned this room into our office back in March. As soon as our first three client checks for Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC cleared the bank the week before, Katie and I had drawn up a real small-business hardware upgrade on graph paper and waited for Dad's weekend drive down from Alexandria to cut the vents.

Mounted inside the open office closet on sturdy black wire shelving sat our Linux staging and database server, our cable modem, a twenty-four-port managed gigabit ethernet switch with neat rows of blinking green link lights, and a heavy black uninterruptible power supply resting on the floor slab so a summer thunderstorm over Kempsville couldn't drop an open SQL transaction before we saved our work.

The one piece of engineering neither Katie nor I was willing to cut corners on was heat. Shutting a running server and a twenty-four-port switch behind a hollow-core closet door in August in Tidewater Virginia without active ventilation would cook every hard drive on the shelf by suppertime. To solve it properly, we had ordered a quiet hundred-and-twenty-millimeter exhaust fan for the upper header alongside an industrial twelve-volt digital thermostat controller and external relay—planning to wire the relay in a strict fail-safe configuration so that if the temperature sensor wire ever got nicked, loosened, or unplugged, the relay contacts would default to spinning the exhaust fan continuously rather than letting the closet overheat in silence.

I unfolded the tiny tissue-thin instruction leaflet that had come inside the cardboard thermostat box, squinted at a blurry, postage-stamp diagram translated half into broken English where Terminals Three and Four weren't even labeled normally open or normally closed, and let out an amused snort.

"Hold off plugging in the twelve-volt wall adapter for two minutes, sis," I said, sliding my fingers onto my keyboard while Katie slipped one side of her cushioned headphones over her left ear to check vibration-damping fan mounts on her own screen. "Whoever printed this datasheet made the normally closed symbol three pixels wide. Let me see if somebody on StreamTube already bench-tested this exact controller with a multimeter."

Typing the eight-character alphanumeric model code stamped on the side of the gray thermostat housing into the StreamTube search bar brought up four hits right away. Clicking the top clip—ninety seconds of an older electronics hobbyist touching continuity probes across the green screw block on a workbench—gave me the exact answer I needed in under a minute: Terminal Three was common, and Terminal Four stayed closed whenever power or probe resistance dropped to zero, giving us a true fail-safe fan circuit.

Satisfied, I reached for my mouse to close the browser tab and pick up my wire strippers.

Half an inch before my cursor reached the corner *X*, my eye caught the second rectangular thumbnail generated automatically down StreamTube's right-hand *Related Videos* sidebar.

## Saturday, August 18, 2007, 2:10 PM — Gloved Hands, A Minnesota Voiceover, & Thinking of Cousin Chelsea

![Inside Katie and Nathaniel Prairie's sunlit dual-desk home office on Arvin Road in August 2007 beneath their framed Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC certificate—with their open three-by-six office closet beside them newly converted into a ventilated networking closet holding a Linux server tower, blinking 24-port gigabit switch, blue patch cables, UPS battery backup, exhaust fan, and digital thermostat—twenty-two-year-old fraternal twins Katie and Nate sit side by side in matching black SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding inner hands as they smile warmly over their shoulders at an angled StreamTube monitor showing black-nitrile-gloved hands arranging basking stones for a painted turtle.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/aug_2007_arvin_road_networking_closet_thermostat_streamtube_turtle_video.jpg)

Nothing on earth except StreamTube's literal 2007 keyword matcher—pairing the exact eight-character thermostat part number in the electronics title to another upload carrying that same part number in its description—would ever have put those two videos on the same page. Sitting sandwiched between a home-brewing fridge build and an attic gable-fan clip was a standard-definition four-by-three thumbnail with barely three hundred views from a tiny channel called *North Lion Scales & Tails*:

*Indoor Painted and Box Turtle Habitat — Fail-Safe Thermostat Relay, Basking Dock, and Quiet Filtration*.

I froze with my hand on the mouse, staring at the little brightly patterned painted turtle resting on a flat gray river stone inside that tiny preview frame.

In half a heartbeat, before I even thought about electronics again, my chest gave a sharp, wistful squeeze—and all I could think of was our cousin Chelsea.

Nearly three years had passed since that freezing December night on Tonomy Hill up in Newport, Rhode Island—Chelsea's very first Christmas without Aunt Liz—when Tiffany Sloan had screamed in the hallway, terrified twenty-two-year-old Chelsea out of the shower, and driven Chelsea to pull both Katie's wheelchair and mine straight into her bedroom with her beloved green plush sea turtle *Shelly Turtle* tucked tight under her chin while she locked mean Tiffany out in the living room for three straight nights to keep us safe. After we finally escaped Tiffany's apartment in November of 2005 and came home to Arvin Road, Dad had tried twice through Rhode Island's state disability office to find out where the court-appointed guardian who took over after Aunt Liz's passing had moved Chelsea once the Tonomy Hill apartment closed; because Dad was an uncle by marriage rather than legal next of kin, the agency clerks wouldn't even confirm what town Chelsea lived in. Not a single month went by without Katie and me thinking of her and hoping somebody up north was being gentle with her and Shelly Turtle.

Smiling softly to myself just picturing how wide Chelsea's eyes would get watching a real live painted turtle climb onto its own sunlit rock indoors, I clicked the thumbnail instead of closing the window.

When the four-by-three video buffered open on my angled LCD monitor, there wasn't a single person's face anywhere on screen—no shaky webcam intro, no host mugging into a lens. Instead, a rock-steady tripod camera looked straight down onto a spotless basement workbench beside a crystal-clear forty-gallon glass tank lined with smooth river slate, natural cork bark, and live aquatic plants.

Into the frame reached a pair of calm, steady hands wearing black nitrile work gloves, holding the exact same gray digital thermostat controller and external relay sitting six inches from my keyboard.

"Hey there, folks—welcome back to the basement," a warm, cheerful man's voiceover narrated off-camera with an unmistakable, rounded Upper-Midwest Minnesota accent, speaking cleanly into a desk microphone while his gloved hands crimped insulated red and black spade terminals onto Terminal Three and Terminal Four. "Today we're wiring up the basking and submersible-heater safety loop on our rescued painted turtle tank. Now, anytime you put a heating element around water or basking reptiles, you never want to trust a single cheap internal bimetallic switch by itself, because if that contact ever welds shut while you're out running errands, that water gets way too warm in a hurry. Wiring this external relay fail-safe means if a probe ever fails or pops out of the suction clip, power to the heater drops straight to zero before a single turtle shell gets uncomfortable..."

I leaned forward two inches in my wheelchair, genuinely impressed despite myself. Not only was the guy's wiring cleaner than the electronics clip I had just watched—every solder sleeve heat-shrunk and strain-relieved along the back rim of the tank—he talked about caring for animals with the exact same calm, common-sense precision Dad used when working on wheelchair chargers.

Three feet to my left, Katie suddenly let out a quiet, delighted laugh of her own, slipped her headphone earcup back off her ear, and released her brake to angle her chair toward mine.

"Nate, wait until you see what I just stumbled across by sheer luck while searching quiet silicone grommets for our closet fan," Katie said, pointing toward her own monitor across our armrests. "Some reptile guy up in Minnesota posted a whole tutorial on isolating aquarium canister-filter vibration so the cabinet doesn't hum at night—and look at the player bar, somebody actually typed real, properly punctuated closed captions for every single sentence!"

I blinked at Katie's angled screen, looked back at mine, and burst out laughing out loud.

"Katie—read the channel name right under your video title!"

Katie squinted half a second at her browser tab, then looked across my shoulder at the exact same dark-green banner reading *North Lion Scales & Tails*—with nothing in the sparse *About* box except *Educational Reptile & Amphibian Care — P.O. Box, Maplewood, MN*—while the exact same pair of black-nitrile-gloved hands tightened a brass hose barb on her screen and lowered a smooth river-stone basking ramp into the water on mine.

"No way!" Katie gasped, laughing right along with me as she muted her tab, rolled her SwiftChair flush against my left side, and laced her right hand warmly through my left hand across our inner armrests so we could watch mine together with the closed-caption toggle switched on. "What are the odds that you look up a twelve-volt thermostat relay, I look up rubber fan grommets five minutes later, and StreamTube drops both of us onto the exact same three-hundred-view turtle basement in Minnesota?"

"Look at the little guy go," I said softly as the camera cut to the finished habitat.

Under the warm golden glow of the newly wired basking lamp, a bright olive-and-red painted turtle paddled effortlessly through sparkling, ripple-free water, hauled its wet shell up the gentle cork-bark ramp without slipping once, and stretched its striped neck out contentedly toward a small square of clean wax paper on the feeding ledge—where neat, uniform, bite-sized squares of crisp green romaine lettuce sat arranged in tidy rows.

Beside me, Katie's laughter softened into the exact same quiet, affectionate smile I had felt three minutes earlier. Resting her cheek lightly against my left shoulder, she watched the little turtle nibble a square of lettuce while clean white closed captions tracked smoothly along the bottom edge of the frame.

"Can you imagine Chelsea's face if she had a tank like that right in the living room beside Shelly Turtle?" Katie murmured softly. "She'd pull a chair right up to the glass and introduce every turtle by name before breakfast."

"First thing I thought of the second the thumbnail popped up," I admitted quietly, squeezing my twin sister's hand before rolling my cursor up to click the yellow *Subscribe* button beside *North Lion Scales & Tails*—watching Katie reach across to her own mouse three seconds later to click *Subscribe* on her browser too, right as Dad pushed open the hallway door from the garage with clean hands to help us finish buttoning up our brand-new networking closet.
