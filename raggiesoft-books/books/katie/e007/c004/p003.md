## Saturday, April 12, 2008, 2:00 PM — Zero Clergy, Level Brick Paths, & "Heather Prairie" Under the Rose Pergola

"Check the ring cushion one more time, Wes—you've got both bands clipped tight on the satin ribbon?" twenty-three-year-old Katie smiled warmly in her royal-blue silk dress, easing her polished black SwiftChair hub to hub beside my left armrest along the wide, zero-step herringbone brick promenade of the Rose Pavilion at Tidewater Botanical Gardens in Norfolk.

Standing proudly in a sharp charcoal suit right beside my right wheel with a crisp white rosebud pinned to his lapel, twenty-one-year-old Wesley held his smooth blue silicone ring in his left palm, tapped the small velvet ring box twice with his right forefinger, and gave a beaming, rock-steady two-note hum.

Picking a venue for Heather's and my wedding after Katie and I officially bought our Kempsville house on Arvin Road fifty-fifty in March—right as Heather wrapped up her very first full school year teaching High School Special Education Adapted Curriculum—had come down to two non-negotiable rules that all four of us agreed on from the first minute.

First, every single inch of the ceremony and reception had to be effortless on four wheels and gentle on Wesley's ears: wide, level brick garden walkways under shaded cedar pergolas overlooking the calm lake, acoustic string guitars instead of booming DJ subwoofers, and a sunlit glass-and-timber garden conservatory ten minutes from CVTA Route 15.

And second, this was a one-hundred-percent secular civil wedding—no church sanctuary, no clergy, no prayers, no scripture readings, and no religious invocations of any kind. Neither Katie, Dad, nor I believed in any religion or any god, and Heather wanted our marriage anchored strictly in honest human promises we wrote ourselves. Standing beneath the blooming April azaleas and early climbing roses at the flat garden arch was Judge Arthur Penn, a retired Virginia Circuit Court civil marriage celebrant authorized under the Code of Virginia to solemnize civil marriages without a single religious word.

In the front row of white garden chairs—with a wide accessible bay left open right beside them—sat our fifty-year-old dad in his best navy suit alongside Aunt Karen and Uncle Steve, right next to Aunt Linda, Heather's parents, and Heather's grandparents who had flown in from Saint Paul the afternoon before.

When the two acoustic guitarists began a warm, gentle instrumental arrangement of The Stardust Engine's *Synchronous*, twenty-three-year-old Heather stepped onto the sunlit brick path in an ivory satin tea-length gown, carrying a simple bouquet of white gardenias and pale-pink roses—not looking down at my wheelchair frame, looking straight into my eyes with the steadiest, happiest smile in Virginia.

Reaching the pergola, Heather paused first to hug Katie warmly across her shoulders, bumped Wesley's knuckles gently with her free hand so Wesley grinned from ear to ear, and then took both of my hands at seated eye level.

"We gather in the spring sunshine among the family who built this circle," Judge Penn spoke clearly and warmly across the quiet garden, "to witness Nathaniel David Prairie and Heather Marie Collins join their lives in civil marriage—honoring the twin bond and the family guardianship that brought all four of you to this day."

Katie held my bouquet microphone steady while Wesley stepped forward on cue and unclipped the two brushed-platinum bands from the ribbon without dropping a thing.

"Five Junes ago under the crape-myrtles outside the Pavilion, you promised you would always listen first, keep calm air in our home, and never treat Katie or Wesley like an afterthought," I said quietly, slipping the band onto Heather's finger without my voice shaking once. "Every single day since then—on ten college campuses, on Monticello Avenue in the dark, down Route 250 in the mountains, and every morning in our kitchen on Arvin Road—you kept your word. Wherever I go, Katie goes; wherever you go, Wesley goes; and for the rest of our lives, all four of us go together."

"You and Katie taught me what keeping a promise actually looks like, Nate," Heather answered, her hazel eyes bright and clear as she slipped my ring onto my right fourth finger while my left hand stayed resting beside Katie's armrest. "I choose you, I cherish our whole household just the way we are—including your and Katie's quiet closet sleeping bag down the hall and my door staying open right next to Wes's room—and I am so proud to stand beside you as your wife."

"By the civil authority vested in me by the Commonwealth of Virginia," Judge Penn smiled, closing his folder without a single religious syllable, "I pronounce you husband and wife!"

Before I had even finished kissing Heather—Mrs. Heather Prairie—in the warm April breeze, twenty-one-year-old Wesley threw his right fist straight up toward the rose trellis and let out the exact same booming, triumphant three-note cheer he had let loose outside the Virginia Beach Pavilion five years earlier, making every single Prairie, Fletcher, and Collins in the garden burst out laughing and applauding together.
