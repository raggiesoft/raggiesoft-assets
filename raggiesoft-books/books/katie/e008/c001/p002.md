## Tuesday, September 16, 2008, 2:00 PM — Four Buses Across Two Cities & The Expired Two-Hour Transfer

![Inside a moving CVTA Route 15 bus crossing the Elizabeth River bridge into Virginia Beach on September 16, 2008, 23-year-old Katie Prairie sits in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair holding up printed CVTA bus timetables for stunned mayoral candidate Scott Tanner as he inspects his expired two-hour paper transfer slip, while twin brother Nate films the interview with a digital camcorder mounted on his SwiftChair lap tray.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/sep_2008_cvta_route_15_bus_mayoral_loop_interview_scott_tanner.jpg)

"Right on time," twenty-three-year-old Katie said, nodding toward the concrete island at the Community College of Hampton Roads Virginia Beach Campus transit hub as mayoral candidate Scott Tanner locked his sedan in the student visitor lot and walked over to our two compact SwiftChairs with a notebook tucked under his arm.

"Katie, Nate—good to meet you both in person," Scott said, shaking Katie's hand and then mine while I checked the microphone levels on the compact digital camcorder mounted to my wheelchair lap tray. "I brought crisp single-dollar bills and quarters just like your email said. Where does our loop start?"

"Right here on Route 29 northbound to Hilltop," Katie said, pointing up at the approaching forty-foot low-floor bus pulling into the CCHR bus loop. "We're going to ride a single continuous four-bus circuit across Virginia Beach and Norfolk and bring you right back to your car at this exact bus stop: Route 29 from CCHR up to Hilltop, Route 20 westbound down Virginia Beach Boulevard into Norfolk to Glenrock Mall, Route 15 southbound down Military Highway back across the city line into Virginia Beach, and Route 12 back here to CCHR."

When the front ramp of the Route 29 bus folded down onto the concrete pad, Katie and I rolled aboard into the dual front wheelchair securement bays and swiped our thirty-day CVTA magnetic passes through the farebox. Right behind us, Scott stepped up into the vestibule, fed his base fare plus the paper transfer fee into the bill validator, and took the timestamped paper transfer slip the driver tore off the cutter bar.

"Keep an eye on the punched time at the top of that paper transfer," I told Scott as I framed the timestamped slip in my camcorder viewfinder while the bus pulled out of CCHR onto Princess Anne Road. "It's good for exactly two hours."

Because Virginia Beach's local routes ran on forty-five- to sixty-minute headways without synchronized transfer pulses, the math of the two-hour paper transfer trap unfolded right in front of my camera lens in real time.

Our first leg on Route 29 carried us northeast up First Colonial Road to the Hilltop transfer stop, where we waited twenty-five minutes on an unsheltered concrete sidewalk pad in the September sun before boarding a westbound Route 20 bus down Virginia Beach Boulevard.

Halfway down the Boulevard past Little Neck and Newtown Road, an older Hispanic gentleman carrying a canvas tool bag boarded the bus and asked the operator a confused question in Spanish about which stop connected to the Military Highway corridor. Before the operator could shake his head, Scott leaned across the aisle and answered the rider fluently in Spanish—explaining the exact stop at Glenrock Mall where they could both transfer to Route 15.

"You speak Spanish?" Katie asked with genuine respect when the rider thanked Scott warmly and sat down two rows behind our wheelchairs.

"Picked it up during my Navy deployments to South America," Scott said simply. "Half the folks riding Route 20 work in the hospitality kitchens at the Oceanfront or the construction yards along the Boulevard. A mayor ought to be able to talk to the people who keep the city running."

When Route 20 dropped us off at the Glenrock Mall transit hub in Norfolk, we waited another twenty-eight minutes for our third bus—a southbound Route 15 heading down Military Highway toward Virginia Beach.

By the time the Route 15 bus rolled south along Military Highway and crossed the low bridge over the Eastern Branch of the Elizabeth River from Norfolk back into Virginia Beach, it was four-fifteen in the afternoon. Holding my camcorder steady on my lap tray with the river glinting through the bus window behind Katie and Scott, I recorded the conversation that became the centerpiece of our video.

"We're crossing back into Virginia Beach right now, Scott," Katie said on camera, holding up the official printed CVTA timetables for Route 33 and Route 12. "Let's talk about what happens if a taxpaying citizen of Virginia Beach who relies on the bus—like Nate and me, or a working parent, or a senior citizen—wants to attend a formal evening meeting of the Virginia Beach City Council down at the Municipal Center in Courthouse."

"Formal City Council meetings gavel in at six o'clock in the evening," Scott said, looking down at the printed schedule columns in Katie's hand.

"Six o'clock in the evening," Katie repeated clearly. "Now look at the Route 33 schedule serving the Virginia Beach Municipal Center. You can ride Route 33 down to City Hall in the afternoon just fine. If you live at the north end of the Oceanfront, the last northbound Route 33 all the way to Sixty-Eighth Street leaves the Municipal Center at 6:01 PM—one minute after the council meeting starts—and the 7:01 PM trip terminates early at Nineteenth Street. And if you live anywhere else in Virginia Beach and need to ride Route 33 from the Municipal Center back to the CCHR hub to catch your connecting bus home—like Route 12, Route 25, or Route 29—look at the departure times."

Katie tapped her finger against the bottom row of the Route 33 and Route 12 timetables so my camera lens could capture the exact numbers.

"The very last Route 33 out of the Municipal Center to CCHR leaves at 6:33 PM, and by the time it reaches CCHR, every single connecting route except Route 36 has already shut down for the night," Katie said. "Because Nate and I live in Kempsville and ride Route 12 home from CCHR toward Military Highway and Indian River Road, look at when the last Route 12 bus of the entire day departs CCHR: 5:48 PM. To catch that 5:48 PM Route 12 home from CCHR, we have to leave City Hall on the 5:33 PM Route 33 bus—twenty-seven minutes before the six o'clock City Council meeting even starts! Our last bus home literally pulls out of CCHR twelve minutes before the Mayor bangs the gavel."

Scott stared at the two printed timetables in disbelief, looking up into my camera lens. "So if you ride the bus to a six o'clock Virginia Beach City Council meeting to speak during public comment, you're stranded at City Hall with no way home."

"Every single Tuesday night," I said from behind the camera. "You cannot tell citizens that local government is open to the public when City Hall's own bus schedule locks bus riders and wheelchair users out thirty minutes before the meeting begins."

Twenty minutes later, when we rolled down the front ramp off Route 15 at Indian River Road and boarded our fourth and final bus—eastbound Route 12 back to CCHR to complete our circle—the final piece of CVTA's broken math played out right on cue.

Katie and I rolled up the ramp and swiped our thirty-day passes. Behind us, Scott handed his paper transfer slip to the Route 12 operator.

The operator glanced at the punched holes along the top edge of the paper slip and shook his head.

"Sorry, sir—this transfer expired nineteen minutes ago," the driver said, tapping the two-hour rule printed on the farebox. "That'll be another full dollar-fifty fare to ride back to the college."

Right on camera, Scott had to reach into his pocket and feed a second full fare into the box just to finish a single afternoon round-trip back to his parked car at CCHR—solely because waiting for hourly buses had eaten up more than two hours.

## Thursday, November 19, 2009, 1:35 PM — Cut Off at the Eighteenth Street Microphone & Blackballed From TRAC

![Inside the Coastal Virginia Transit Authority boardroom on East 18th Street in Norfolk on November 19, 2009, 24-year-old twins Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs at the public-comment table as CVTA Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead leans over the dais to cut off their microphone.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/nov_2009_cvta_18th_street_mic_cutoff_and_jan_2011_commissioner_reckoning.jpg)

That night in September 2008, I edited our four-bus ride down into a crisp twelve-minute documentary video with full burned-in open captions and published it at the top of Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit.

Within a week, the video had been watched more than forty thousand times across Hampton Roads and picked up by *The Tidewater Pilot* and every television station in Norfolk and Virginia Beach. In the November 2008 election, twenty-year incumbent Mayor Myra Overton was defeated, and although the wealthy banking-executive frontrunner edged out Scott Tanner in the four-way race, the public uproar over our video forced CVTA and City Hall to promise a "comprehensive review" of bus transfers and Virginia Beach evening service hours.

Fourteen months later, however, on a gray Thursday afternoon in November 2009 inside the CVTA boardroom on East Eighteenth Street in Norfolk, we learned exactly what CVTA's executive suite thought of that promise.

Katie and I had ridden three buses into Norfolk carrying a twenty-four-page PHP audit we had compiled over the autumn, documenting more than nine hundred missed transfer connections at Glenrock Mall and CCHR alongside a formal request for CVTA's raw General Transit Feed Specification schedule tables so riders could build real-time trip-planning tools. Just two weeks earlier, I had also submitted my formal application to fill one of Virginia Beach's vacant citizen seats on CVTA's Transit Riders Advisory Committee—known around the agency as TRAC.

Parked side by side in our SwiftChairs at the low public-comment table in the center of the boardroom, I was two minutes and forty seconds into reading our findings into the record while CVTA President and Chief Executive Officer Leonard Sterling and Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead sat slouched in their high-backed leather chairs along the raised executive tier.

"—and because the outbound Route 20 and southbound Route 15 schedules at Glenrock Mall are offset by four minutes in the master dispatch table without a hold-for-transfer rule," I said clearly into the gooseneck microphone, holding up our printed PHP chart, "evening commuters transferring back into Virginia Beach are missing the final southbound bus seven nights a week—"

*BZZZZT.*

Before I could finish the sentence, Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead reached forward and jabbed the master cutoff switch on the clerk's console. The red ring at the neck of my microphone went dead in the middle of my word.

"Your three minutes are expired, Mr. Prairie," CEO Leonard Sterling said flatly without looking up from his leather portfolio. "Next speaker."

"Mr. President, if staff would simply release the raw GTFS schedule files we requested under the Freedom of Information Act—" Katie spoke up from her SwiftChair beside me, her voice carrying across the quiet room even without the microphone.

"Public comment is not a question-and-answer session, Ms. Prairie," Sterling interrupted, waving a hand toward the rear doors. "The commission has a full agenda."

Fifteen minutes later, when the board recessed briefly before its closed executive session, Katie and I rolled our wheelchairs out into the tiled corridor near the drinking fountain to speak with Chief Operating Officer Richard Halstead and the agency's government-affairs director as they walked toward the coffee credenza.

"Mr. Halstead," I said politely, holding out the bound copy of our twenty-four-page schedule audit and a thumb drive containing our PHP transfer-timing script, "we brought a clean copy of the Glenrock Mall and CCHR connection data for your scheduling department, and I wanted to check on the status of my application for the Virginia Beach citizen seat on the Transit Riders Advisory Committee."

Halstead stopped, looked down his nose at the thumb drive in my hand, and didn't even reach out to take it. A thin, patronizing smirk curled the corner of his mouth.

"Look, Nate, Katie," Halstead said, lowering his voice just enough that the reporters inside the boardroom couldn't hear him, "let's save everybody some time. My scheduling engineers don't take operational direction from statistically insignificant hobbyist bloggers, and we aren't handing over internal GTFS database files so the two of you can play armchair transit director from your living room."

Katie's blue eyes narrowed. "We ride your buses every single day of the week in power wheelchairs, Mr. Halstead. There is nothing 'hobbyist' about needing a bus that actually connects before dark."

"And as for the Transit Riders Advisory Committee," Halstead added smoothly, adjusting his silk tie as he turned back toward the executive suite door, "staff already forwarded our recommendations for the Virginia Beach TRAC vacancies this morning. Your name wasn't on the list, Nate. TRAC is reserved for constructive community stakeholders—not blog agitators with a laptop."

The heavy oak door of the executive suite clicked shut behind him.

For ten seconds, Katie and I sat side by side in the quiet hallway on East Eighteenth Street, staring at that closed oak door. They had cut off my microphone at a hundred and eighty seconds, laughed at our engineering work, locked up public schedule data paid for by the taxpayers, and secretly blackballed me from a volunteer citizen advisory committee because we had dared to prove on camera that their schedules were broken.

And the final insult came four months later, in the spring of 2010, when the eleven-member Virginia Beach City Council met down at the Municipal Center to vote on the city's annual transit contribution. Rather than holding CVTA accountable or funding the evening connecting pulse we had proposed, ten-year Kempsville District incumbent Councilman Harold Dietz led the budget motion to strip out every dime of proposed late-evening bus funding—muttering into his dais microphone at the Tuesday afternoon work session that *"almost nobody rides the bus out of Courthouse after dark anyway."*

Sitting side by side at our dual coding desks in our house off Military Highway and Indian River Road in Kempsville that evening watching the archived City Hall webcast over cold glasses of ginger ale, Katie reached forward and clicked Pause on the video player.

"Of course nobody rides the bus out of Courthouse after dark, Councilman Dietz," Katie said to the monitor, her voice calm and razor-sharp. "There isn't a bus out of Courthouse after 6:33 PM! That's like taking the ramp off a building and claiming nobody in a wheelchair ever comes inside."

Katie turned her SwiftChair toward me, her blue eyes steady and bright.

"Leonard Sterling and Richard Halstead think they can cut off your microphone at Eighteenth Street and blackball you from TRAC because we're just two twenty-five-year-old bloggers in wheelchairs with no vote on their budget," Katie said. "And Harold Dietz thinks he owns the Kempsville seat on the Virginia Beach City Council for life. Well, Harold Dietz's seat is on the ballot this November, Nate. City Council elections in Virginia are non-partisan, and we live right here in our house in Kempsville. What do you say we stop asking Richard Halstead and Councilman Dietz for permission—and go take his seat on the City Council?"

"Let's do it," I said immediately, already opening a fresh PHP project directory on my MagnaBook Pro. "If they won't let us sit at the public-comment table for three minutes, we'll put you up on the dais where they have to answer to you."
