## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 11:35 AM — The Capitol Tunnel & Splitting Up at the Chamber Doors

![Rolling side by side through the subterranean marble concourse tunnel connecting the General Assembly Building to the Virginia State Capitol in Richmond on February 4, 2016, 30-year-old Delegate Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate hold hands across the armrests of their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs moments before Katie's historic floor speech on House Bill 663.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/feb_2016_capitol_tunnel_rolling_to_house_floor_katie_nate.jpg)

"Look at Line 18 on Page 1 again, Nate," my twin sister Katie said, tapping her yellow highlighter against the two-page bill printout resting on her lap tray as our compact SwiftChairs rolled side by side down the long underground concourse tunnel connecting the General Assembly Building to the historic Virginia State Capitol. "'Anatomical sex means the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy.' Not a birth certificate, not a driver's license, and not how someone lives their daily life—strictly their physical anatomy. And then look at Line 54 on Page 2: 'Any person who willfully and knowingly violates this section shall be liable for a civil penalty of not more than fifty dollars,' enforced by a law enforcement officer's summons."

"I read it three times while I was setting up the daily calendar script this morning—and even Sam, Mandy, and Sarah spotted the flaw in ten seconds flat when they dropped off the morning floor packet in Room 518," I replied, steering my wheelchair smoothly beside hers past the subterranean security desk as the fifteen-minute warning bell for the noon floor session chimed from the bronze wall speakers overhead. "Delegate Cobb and Delegate Marsh put it straight onto today's Second Reading floor calendar out of the General Laws Committee. Because it didn't come through any of your three committees, they think everybody on the Floor is going to treat it like a routine committee bill and wave it through before lunch."

It was thirty-year-old Katie's second two-year term representing the Eighty-Fifth House District back home in Kempsville and Virginia Beach, and she sat as a rank-and-file member of the thirty-four-seat Democratic minority facing a sixty-six-seat Republican supermajority. In the hierarchy of the Virginia House of Delegates, Katie held no leadership office—not Speaker, not Majority or Minority Leader, not Whip, and not a committee gavel. The chamber was presided over from the high marble rostrum by the Republican Speaker of the House, William J. Harwell of Stafford, who referred all legislation to committees and appointed every standing committee roster, while the sixty-six-member Republican majority on the floor was managed from the front row of the center aisle by House Majority Leader Richard Callaway of Danville, flanked by Deputy Majority Leader Todd Gentry, Majority Whip Jackson Milligan, and Republican Caucus Chair Timothy Hughes, across from our Democratic Minority Leader, Tom Toscano, along with Democratic Whip Eileen Fillmore and Democratic Caucus Chair Kenneth Plumley.

As a second-term opposition delegate who used a power wheelchair full-time, medically could not drive, and rode public buses and passenger trains everywhere she went, Katie served on three standing committees where she could do the most practical good for working families: the House Committee on Transportation, the House Committee on Health, Welfare and Institutions, and the House Committee on Education. On the Transportation Committee, whenever a colleague from a suburban district casually remarked in a hearing that "people can always drive," Katie would look calmly across the committee table from her SwiftChair and reply, "No, they can't. I took the bus here."

House Bill 663 had not come through Transportation, Health, Welfare and Institutions, or Education. It had been reported out of someone else's committee—the House Committee on General Laws, chaired by Delegate Gilbert Peace—where the majority had advanced it to the House Floor without anyone asking what the statutory words actually required a human being to do.

Every day the General Assembly was in session during January and February, the two of us operated by the exact same synchronized routine we had followed since her freshman session in 2014: we woke up in our accessible one-bedroom winter-session apartment at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts on West Broad Street, rode the eastbound JRTC Route 6 bus straight down Broad Street to Ninth and Broad, and went over every bill on the daily House calendar line by statutory line the exact same way we audited PHP code or Coastal Virginia Transit Authority bus schedules. Twenty minutes earlier upstairs in Room 518, when our three favorite thirteen-year-old middle-school House Pages—eighth-grade triplets Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins from Southside Virginia, who followed Katie around the Capitol whenever they had a free courier run—had delivered the printed Second Reading calendar packet to Katie's desk, Katie had handed each of them a copy of House Bill 663.

Thirteen-year-old Sarah had reached the operative language on Line 18 first. She wasn't trying to make a political or constitutional argument; as a literal, rule-following thirteen-year-old autistic girl, she had simply applied the proposed statutory rule to herself—*I am a girl, and this bill says I am allowed into the girls' room because of my anatomy*—read the definition twice, and looked up at Katie with genuine fear in her eyes.

"Delegate Prairie?" Sarah had asked softly, pointing a finger at Line 18 on the page.

"Yeah, Sarah?" Katie had answered.

"Wait," Sarah had said, her voice tight. "Does somebody have to see my parts to prove I'm a girl?"

Katie had stopped reading completely.

Right beside her twin sister, Mandy had immediately looked over Sarah's shoulder at the page. "What?"

"It says which bathroom I can use depends on my anatomy," Sarah had said.

"So how do they know?" their brother Sam had asked, looking up from his copy.

Katie had gently taken the printout back from Sarah's hand. For three seconds, the office had gone dead quiet as my sister read Line 18 and Line 54 again.

"That's a very good question," Katie had said softly.

"Maybe your birth certificate?" Mandy had suggested, squinting at her copy of the bill.

"The bill doesn't say that," Katie had told her.

"A doctor?" Sam had asked.

"Doesn't say that either," Katie had replied.

"But they don't know," Sarah had said literally.

"Right," Katie had agreed.

"Everybody knows Sarah's a girl," Mandy had said reassuringly, taking her sister's hand.

"But how?" Sarah had asked.

"The bill says anatomy," Sam had pointed out. "Not your Page badge, and not how you're dressed."

"Exactly," Katie had said, as the software-developer quality-assurance part of our brains clicked straight into gear. If you stress-tested House Bill 663 the way we stress-tested a software routine before production—input: an accusation that someone used the wrong restroom; required statutory determination: anatomical sex; evidence specified by statute: none; verification procedure: none; authorized investigator: unspecified; standard of proof: unspecified; privacy protections for minors: unspecified—the entire statute collapsed into a terrifying Fourth Amendment hole.

Looking straight into thirteen-year-old Sarah's eyes first so the frightened eighth-grader would know she was safe, Katie had said gently, "Sarah, nobody should ever have to see your private parts for you to use a bathroom."

Sarah had let out a long, relieved breath. "Okay."

Then Katie had looked back down at the yellow-highlighted text of House Bill 663, her blue eyes hardening into flint. "Which means the Gentleman from Spotsylvania has some explaining to do."

We rode the subterranean elevator up into the historic Capitol and rolled out onto the first-floor marble corridor just outside the tall mahogany double doors of the House Chamber. Lobbyists, school groups, and legislative aides were hurrying past the white marble columns as the ten-minute bell sounded.

At the velvet brass rope flanking the Chamber entrance—where the Sergeant-at-Arms staff checked member pins—Katie braked her SwiftChair and turned toward me.

Ever since our very first visit to the State Capitol back in February 2011—when Capitol Police had stopped us outside the third-floor public viewing gallery because the small historic elevator locked out during fire alarms and there was no safe way to evacuate a wheelchair down three flights of eighteenth-century stairs—House Leadership and the Sergeant-at-Arms had maintained a standing ADA floor pass for me (and any visiting constituent in a wheelchair plus one companion) to watch the daily session from a level wheelchair bay inside the first-floor House Chamber itself, right along the outer right aisle beside our fellow Virginia Beach lawmaker, Delegate Ronald Villa of the Twenty-First District.

"Are you set to plug into the floor feed over by Ron's desk?" Katie asked, reaching across our armrests to give my right hand our familiar twin squeeze.

"All set," I said, patting the padded laptop bag strapped across my lap tray where my MagnaBook Pro and my digital stream-capture rig were tucked alongside our daily legislative binder. "I'll have the chamber stream hooked into my laptop before the Speaker bangs the gavel so I can record and caption your remarks for the constituent archive."

"Wait until Delegate Cobb finishes his floor pitch on House Bill 663," Katie said, her blue eyes steady and focused as she tucked the highlighted two-page printout under her left wrist on the lap tray. "I'm going to ask the Speaker to let me put one direct operational question to him on the record."

"Give him hell, Delegate Prairie," I whispered with a small smile.

"See you at the center doors when we recess," Katie said warmly.

Releasing my hand, my twin sister eased her joystick forward, nodded to the doorkeeper holding open the left-hand mahogany door, and rolled her compact black power wheelchair across the green carpet toward her modified accessible desk at the end of the far left minority aisle.

I flashed my gold-stamped ADA floor pass to the Sergeant-at-Arms assistant at the right-hand doorway and rolled quietly inside to the designated wheelchair bay along the outer right aisle, twenty feet from the ground-level emergency exit.

Right beside my bay, seated at his mahogany desk bearing the brass nameplate *MR. VILLA — VIRGINIA BEACH (21ST DISTRICT)*, Delegate Ronald Villa looked up from his leather binder and gave me a warm, familiar nod.

"Morning, Nate," Delegate Villa whispered, reaching over to shake my hand just as he had done the very first day the Sergeant-at-Arms had brought Katie and me onto the Floor back in 2011. "How's the Route 6 bus running down Broad Street this morning?"

"Right on the ten-minute headway, Delegate Villa," I whispered back with a smile as I flipped down my lap tray, opened my laptop, plugged my headphones into the assistive-listening audio jack at the side rail, and launched my digital capture software hooked into the chamber's fixed legislative webcast camera.

Looking across the wide expanse of green carpet under the tall cream-and-gold coffered ceiling and crystal chandeliers of the House Chamber, I had a clear, floor-level view of the Speaker's high marble rostrum at the front of the room, the Clerk's well, the red-and-green electronic voting boards flanking the front wall, and Katie sitting upright in her compact SwiftChair at her accessible desk across the chamber on the minority aisle, her yellow-highlighted copy of House Bill 663 resting squarely in front of her desk microphone.

## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 12:18 PM — Watching Across the House Floor as Katie Challenges the Patron

![On the Floor of the Virginia House of Delegates on February 4, 2016, 30-year-old Delegate Katie Prairie sits at her accessible mahogany desk in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair holding up the highlighted pages of House Bill 663 to challenge its 'anatomical sex' restroom inspection mandate while twin brother Nate records the floor feed from his SwiftChair.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/feb_2016_virginia_house_floor_hb663_speech_katie_prairie.jpg)

At twelve o'clock noon on the dot, the Speaker of the House rapped his wooden gavel against the rostrum block.

Up in the third-floor public gallery overhanging the rear wall—where two dozen college students from Virginia Dominion University and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute, a handful of off-duty teachers, and several parents sat along the wooden benches—and down across the hundred mahogany desks on the Floor, everyone fell silent for the opening prayer, the Pledge of Allegiance, and the routine morning leaves of absence.

By 12:18 PM, the House had moved into the Second Reading calendar of bills reported out of committee. Half the Delegates on the floor were leaning back in their leather swivel chairs chatting with deskmates or flipping through binders while the Clerk read bill titles in a rapid, monotone cadence.

"House Bill 663, a bill to amend the Code of Virginia relating to single-gender public restrooms and facilities in buildings owned or leased by the Commonwealth, public institutions of higher education, and local school divisions; civil penalty. Patron, Delegate Cobb."

From my wheelchair bay beside the outer right aisle, I clicked the red Record button on my laptop capture window and checked my audio level meters. Right in front of me, I saw Delegate Ronald Villa—a pragmatic coastal Virginia Beach Republican who cared about bus shelters, military families, and Oceanfront tourism, not culture-war stunts—let out a quiet sigh and rub his forehead as the bill was called.

Three rows ahead on the majority side of the House Floor, Delegate Martin Cobb—with his chief co-patron, Delegate Robert Marsh, seated two desks over—stood up behind his mahogany desk, buttoned his navy suit jacket, and leaned into his silver gooseneck microphone.

"Mr. Speaker and members of the House," Delegate Cobb's voice echoed across the chamber and came cleanly through my floor headphones, "House Bill 663 is a simple, common-sense measure to protect personal privacy and public safety across the Commonwealth of Virginia. This legislation directs the Department of General Services, every public institution of higher education, and every local school board to ensure that every single-gender public restroom, locker room, and shower facility in any public building or school is used exclusively by individuals whose anatomical sex matches the designated gender on the door—backed by a fifty-dollar civil penalty enforceable by law enforcement summons. I hope it will be the pleasure of the House to engross the bill and advance it to its third reading."

Across the chamber at the end of the minority aisle, I saw Katie press the white request-to-speak button on her desk console and raise her right hand into the air.

Up on the high marble rostrum, the Speaker glanced down toward the left aisle.

"For what purpose does the gentlewoman from Virginia Beach, Delegate Prairie, rise?" the Speaker asked into his microphone.

Across the green carpet, Katie flipped the toggle switch at the base of her desk microphone so the green indicator ring illuminated, and lifted the two printed pages of House Bill 663 in her right hand.

"Mr. Speaker, to see if the gentleman from Spotsylvania will yield for a question on the bill," Katie's voice rang out crisp and clear across the chamber.

"Does the gentleman from Spotsylvania yield to the gentlewoman from Virginia Beach?" the Speaker asked.

Standing at his desk across the center aisle, Delegate Cobb gave a brief, stiff nod. "I yield for a question, Mr. Speaker."

"The gentleman yields," the Speaker said.

From my wheelchair on the far side of the floor, I watched my twin sister look straight across the House Chamber at the bill's patron.

"Thank you, Mr. Speaker," Katie said. "Look with me for a moment at the exact text printed in House Bill 663, Delegate Cobb. My brother and I write computer software for a living, and when you write code—or when you write a state law—definitions determine how an instruction actually executes in the real world. Unlike bills that reference a birth certificate or a state identification card, your Line 18 on Page 1 explicitly defines anatomical sex as 'the physical condition of being male or female, which is determined by a person's anatomy.' Not their driver's license. Not how they are dressed. Their physical anatomy. And then on Page 2, Line 54, you mandate that a law enforcement officer issue a fifty-dollar civil summons at the restroom door based on that physical anatomy."

Across the floor around me, the background murmur of side conversations suddenly died down. Delegates who had been thumbing through their afternoon calendars looked up from their desks.

"The language of the bill speaks for itself, Mr. Speaker," Delegate Cobb replied stiffly into his desk microphone. "Restrooms in state buildings, public colleges, and public schools will be restricted strictly by anatomical sex, and law enforcement officers are authorized to issue a fifty-dollar civil summons to anyone who enters a restroom that does not match their anatomical sex."

Up at the front row of the center aisle, I saw Deputy Majority Leader Todd Gentry lean sharply across the mahogany divider toward House Majority Leader Richard Callaway's desk. Even from my wheelchair bay along the right rail, I could read the urgent tension in Gentry's posture as he whispered, "Richard, Prairie's gutting the bill. Are you going to do something?"

Majority Leader Callaway didn't even look up from the two-page printout of House Bill 663 resting on his leather blotter. "Like what?"

"Stop her!" Gentry hissed under his breath. "Look what she's doing to Cobb."

"She's asking him how his bill works," the Majority Leader murmured dryly, turning Page 1 over to Page 2 with two fingers. "She's reading the bill. And Cobb seems to be doing an adequate job of gutting it himself."

Over at her accessible desk on the far left aisle, Katie released her joystick brake half an inch, rolling her compact SwiftChair flush against the mahogany desk edge as she held the two printed pages of House Bill 663 high in the air. Across the chamber, I could hear the raw, unmistakable fire rising in my sister's voice.

"Mr. Speaker, when the people of Kempsville and Virginia Beach first elected me to serve in this House of Delegates two years ago," Katie declared, her voice echoing off the marble walls of the Capitol chamber, "I had to fight the facilities bureaucracy in this very Capitol complex just to get an ADA-compliant restroom stall wide enough for my power wheelchair to turn around in and close the door in privacy! Every single day of my life since I was sixteen years old, I have known what it feels like to have strangers stare at my body and treat a basic human necessity like using a public restroom as a public spectacle. And now the gentleman comes onto the Floor of this House with House Bill 663 and tells the women and girls of Virginia that the state government is going to police 'anatomical sex' at the door of every ladies' room from Virginia Beach to Blacksburg for a fifty-dollar fine!"

Up in the third-floor public gallery above us, the college students and parents leaned forward over the brass balcony rail, holding their breath. Down on the House Floor, you could have heard a pin drop.

"Then if the gentleman says there will be no physical inspection, how does the Commonwealth establish that someone's anatomical sex does not match the designation of the facility?" Katie demanded, shaking the printed pages of House Bill 663 across the aisle. "How, exactly, is a police officer or a state building inspector standing outside a restroom door at the Community College of Hampton Roads, or Virginia Dominion University, or right outside in this State Capitol corridor supposed to verify a citizen's 'physical anatomy' before writing your fifty-dollar ticket? If I am accused of being in the wrong women's restroom, what am I supposed to show somebody to prove that I'm female? I return to the exact question a thirteen-year-old girl asked me twenty minutes ago when she read your bill: why on earth should I—or any girl or woman in the Commonwealth of Virginia—have to pull my pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove I have female parts just to use the ladies' room?!"

An audible gasp swept across the chamber, followed half a second later by a sudden burst of applause from the visitors up in the third-floor gallery benches and several sharp "Hear, hear!" calls from the minority desks down on the Floor. Right beside my wheelchair on the Republican side of the floor, Delegate Ronald Villa winced, shook his head at Cobb's bill, and exchanged an unmistakable *she's got him dead to rights* look with his seatmate.

And up at the front row of the center aisle, at the words *a thirteen-year-old girl asked me twenty minutes ago when she read your bill*, I saw House Majority Leader Richard Callaway freeze for half a second and turn his head to look straight back at thirteen-year-old Sarah Hawkins standing at attention in her navy blue House Page blazer behind his desk.

Sarah looked right back at the Majority Leader and gave a single, solemn nod.

Even from across the green carpet, you could practically see the *oh, for heaven's sake—one of my thirteen-year-old Pages found the hole in this bill* realization cross the Majority Leader's face.

*Bam! Bam!*

"The House will come to order! The House will be in order!" Speaker William J. Harwell barked from the marble rostrum, pounding his wooden gavel twice as he looked up toward the third-floor balcony. "The guests in the public gallery are reminded that demonstrations of approval or disapproval are strictly prohibited by the rules of the House!"

Down on the Republican benches two seats over from Delegate Cobb, the bill's chief co-patron, Delegate Robert Marsh, shot to his feet with his face flushed crimson, stabbing a finger toward his microphone button. "Mr. Speaker! Point of order! The gentlewoman from Virginia Beach is using indecorous language on the floor of this House!"

Before the Speaker could rule—and before Democratic Minority Leader Tom Toscano could even rise from the front row of the left aisle—the Republican Majority Leader himself stood up behind his center-aisle desk.

"Mr. Speaker," Majority Leader Callaway called out calmly.

Up on the rostrum, Speaker Harwell paused with his gavel in hand. "The Majority Leader."

Richard Callaway looked once at Delegate Marsh and Delegate Cobb, and then straight up at the rostrum. "Mr. Speaker, the Gentlewoman's phrasing may be uncomfortable, but the Gentlewoman didn't introduce anatomy into this legislation. The bill did. The legislation before us expressly makes anatomical sex determinative. I believe she is entitled to ask the patron how the Commonwealth proposes to determine that fact."

A stunned hush fell over all one hundred desks in the chamber.

Across the green carpet, Katie looked at the Republican Majority Leader in quiet amazement—remembering a sentence she would never forget for the rest of her life—before turning her eyes back to the bill's patron.

Out on the majority aisle of the House Floor, Delegate Martin Cobb stood completely frozen behind his desk microphone for four full seconds, his face turning beet-red under the chamber chandeliers as he looked from Katie's power wheelchair to the uneasy, downward stares of his own party colleagues around him.

"I—Mr. Speaker, the bill does not—nobody is suggesting—" Delegate Cobb stammered into his microphone, shuffling his folder on his desk without answering the question.

"Mr. Speaker, that is literally what the words printed on Page 1 and Page 2 of House Bill 663 require!" Katie replied from her SwiftChair, her voice steady, clear, and unflinching across the silent chamber. "Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and every citizen in this Commonwealth deserves basic human dignity and privacy without politicians stationing bathroom police at the door to inspect people's anatomy. No woman or girl in Virginia should ever have to pull her pants down to prove she has female parts just to use a public restroom!"

Along the Republican rows from Hampton Roads to the Northern Virginia suburbs, I saw half a dozen moderate delegates—starting with Delegate Ronald Villa right beside my wheelchair and Appropriations Chairman S. Christopher Jennings two rows ahead—emphatically shaking their heads at Majority Whip Jackson Milligan. Milligan hurried up the center carpet and leaned down beside the Majority Leader's ear.

"Leader, we're bleeding votes," the Majority Whip murmured urgently.

"How badly?" Richard asked under his breath.

"Badly."

Majority Leader Richard closed his leather binder with a quiet snap, stood up at his front-row desk, and flipped on his microphone. "Pass it by."

"Mr. Speaker," the Majority Leader announced briskly across the chamber, "I move that House Bill 663 be passed by for the day."

"Without objection, House Bill 663 is passed by for the day," Speaker Harwell announced immediately, rapping his gavel once and hurrying straight ahead. "The Clerk will read the next bill on the calendar."

## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 12:35 PM — Archiving the 95-Second Clip, "Your Bill Killed Your Bill," & Rolling Back Through the Tunnel

In my wheelchair bay beside Delegate Villa's desk, I clicked the Stop button on my capture window and exhaled a quiet breath.

Delegate Villa leaned slightly toward my armrest and whispered out of the corner of his mouth, "Your sister just drove a freight truck straight through the middle of that bill, Nate."

I gave him a quiet grin and looked down at the timeline bar on my laptop screen while the Clerk moved on to a routine highway-naming bill. Checking the timestamp on the video file I had just recorded from the chamber feed, I saw that the entire floor exchange between Katie and Delegate Cobb ran ninety-five seconds.

Just like I did whenever Katie spoke on a bill on the House Floor, I opened Quantum Code right there on my lap tray, typed out a verbatim, frame-accurate WebVTT closed-caption file for the ninety-five-second video, burned high-contrast open captions along the bottom of the frame so constituents watching on mute could read every word, and uploaded the clip to Katie's legislative website archive, StreamTube, and The Grid at 12:35 PM with a standard archival heading:

> Delegate Katie Prairie (D-Virginia Beach) Questions HB 663 Patron on the House Floor Regarding "Anatomical Sex" Restroom Enforcement (Feb. 4, 2016)

Twenty-five minutes later, at one o'clock, the House adjourned its Thursday floor session so members could grab lunch before afternoon committee work.

I unplugged my audio cable from the side rail, packed my laptop and capture rig back into my bag, shook Delegate Villa's hand, and eased my joystick forward to roll toward the right-hand chamber doorway.

Right near the front of the aisle by the Clerk's well—where Speaker William J. Harwell had just stepped down from the marble rostrum to confer with House Majority Leader Richard Callaway while thirteen-year-old House Page triplets Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins stood dutifully at attention behind the Majority Leader's desk holding stacks of committee folders—a red-faced Delegate Martin Cobb marched up to the Majority Leader with his copy of House Bill 663 crumpled in his fist.

As I slowed my SwiftChair behind a cluster of departing delegates waiting for the doorway to clear, I heard every word.

"Leader, Prairie ambushed me out there!" Delegate Cobb complained bitterly. "Prairie killed my bill!"

Majority Leader Callaway stopped buttoning his suit coat and looked at the Gentleman from Spotsylvania over the top of his reading glasses.

"No," Richard said flatly.

"Leader, you heard what she did on the floor!"

"I certainly did," the Majority Leader replied in his calm, unhurried Danville baritone. "Katie asked how your bill worked."

For two full seconds, nobody around the Clerk's well made a sound.

"Your bill killed your bill, Martin," Richard told him evenly. "And for your information, Sarah found this."

Delegate Cobb blinked past the Majority Leader's shoulder at thirteen-year-old Sarah Hawkins standing in her navy blue House Page blazer. "Leader..."

"Sarah is thirteen," Richard said flatly.

"I understand that."

"Apparently she understood the bill," the Majority Leader replied dryly. "You brought a statute onto the House calendar whose central operative concept collapses under the very first implementation question. Supporters will always tell you why your bill is wonderful; opponents will tell you where it breaks. You should've put this bill in front of Katie before it ever left committee."

Delegate Cobb stared at him as if the Majority Leader had lost his mind. "Prairie would've opposed it!"

"Of course she would've opposed it," Richard replied. "That's why you should've put it in front of her. Give your bill to the smartest person you know who's determined to kill it. If it survives her, you might have something."

Richard glanced half an inch over his shoulder toward the three eighth-grade Pages standing behind his desk.

"Better yet," the Majority Leader added dryly, "you should've given it to Katie and those three Pages who follow her around."

Cobb blinked at the three teenagers in their navy blue Page blazers. "Sam, Mandy, and Sarah?"

"Yes."

"They're thirteen."

"And apparently none of the four of them were going to let you get away with an undefined enforcement mechanism," Richard said.

Beside the Clerk's desk, Speaker Harwell looked from the crumpled printout of House Bill 663 to the three eighth-graders in quiet disbelief. "Three thirteen-year-olds caught this?"

Standing between her brother and her twin sister, thirteen-year-old Sarah nodded solemnly. "We caught it really fast, Mr. Speaker."

Right behind the Majority Leader's back, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah clamped their lips tightly together, their shoulders twitching in unison as all three of them tried desperately not to laugh out loud on the House Floor.

Without even turning his head around, the Majority Leader caught them in the reflection of the brass rail.

"And don't you three start," Richard muttered—though I could see the corner of his mouth twitching as a thoroughly chastened Delegate Cobb turned on his heel and stalked out the side door.

Speaker Harwell let out a long breath, shook his head, and murmured to his floor leader, "Next time somebody brings us a bill about people's anatomy, Richard, have Prairie read it first."

"Actually, Mr. Speaker," Majority Leader Callaway sighed, rubbing the bridge of his nose as he tucked his binder under his arm, "never bring a bill to this floor that failed Prairie—and have those three read it too."

Biting the inside of my cheek to keep from grinning from ear to ear, I rolled out the right-hand chamber doorway into the first-floor marble corridor just as Katie came rolling out the left-hand doorway in her SwiftChair.

"Hey," I said, rolling right up beside her and reaching out to take her left hand. "The ninety-five-second floor clip is captioned and archived on your constituent page—and wait until I tell you what the Majority Leader and the Speaker just told Delegate Cobb by the Clerk's well. First, when you mentioned on the floor that a thirteen-year-old girl asked you that question twenty minutes ago, the Majority Leader turned right around and looked at Sarah standing behind his desk, and Sarah nodded right back at him! Then when Marsh tried to call you out of order, the Majority Leader stood up and told the whole House that you didn't introduce anatomy into the legislation, the bill did. And when Cobb complained after the gavel that you killed his bill, the Majority Leader looked him dead in the eye and told him, 'No, Katie asked how your bill worked—your bill killed your bill, and Sarah found this,' while Sarah told the Speaker, 'We caught it really fast!' The Majority Leader said next time Cobb should have you and Sam, Mandy, and Sarah proofread his legislation before bringing it to the floor!"

Katie's blue eyes lit up with a mixture of surprise, warmth, and quiet respect as she squeezed my fingers and we turned our wheelchairs side by side toward the elevator down to the underground Capitol tunnel. "Looks like Sarah, Mandy, Sam, and I just became the unofficial House Quality Assurance Department. Even if we sit on opposite sides of the aisle on almost everything else, at least the Majority Leader actually reads the bills. Come on—let's go grab a sandwich in the basement cafeteria before our two o'clock Transit Legislative Day meetings."
