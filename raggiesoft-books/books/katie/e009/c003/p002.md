## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 2:15 PM — Virginia Transit Legislative Day in Room 518 & A Visit from Michael and Rachel Ragsdale

"Permission to roll into the bus delegate's office?"

Katie and I looked up from the Hampton Roads transit allocation table on her mahogany desk in Room 518—right as the last three Newport News bus operators thanked us in the hallway—and broke into wide grins at the exact same second.

Out of all sixty days of the winter session in Richmond, Virginia Transit Legislative Day was the one afternoon when the marble corridors of the General Assembly Building traded corporate lobbyists in thousand-dollar tailored suits for everyday bus and rail riders wearing bright blue *RIDE TRANSIT VIRGINIA* lapel stickers. Rolling through our open fifth-floor doorway in a folded-frame, blue-upholstered manual wheelchair was our twenty-eight-year-old childhood friend and [former across-the-hall neighbor Michael Ragsdale](https://books.raggiesoft.com/katie/seventeen-senior-year-greenway-court/greenway-court-apartment-a6-the-square-closet-sanctuary/part-1-apartment-a6-on-greenway-court-dad-s-microwave-cart-across-the-hall-neighbors), with his twenty-six-year-old sister Rachel walking right beside him in her glasses and royal-blue Virginia Dominion University jacket, keeping one hand resting lightly on his left rear push-handle and her other hand laced tightly through his fingers. Both of them had blue *RIDE TRANSIT VIRGINIA* stickers pressed over their hearts and carried folders of Coastal Virginia Transit Authority route maps.

"Michael! Rachel!" Katie beamed, nudging her joystick and rolling her SwiftChair out from behind her mahogany desk as I wheeled right up beside her so the four of us could exchange warm hugs across our armrests. "What are you two doing up in Richmond? And Michael, what happened to your ankles?"

"Elective bilateral Achilles heel cord lengthening two weeks ago," Michael smiled, patting the frame of his blue manual wheelchair while never letting go of Rachel's hand. "My heel cords have been too tight to pivot safely between driving pedals my whole life, and Rachel stubbornly refused to get her Learner's Permit until we could walk into the DMV and get our permits together once my surgeon clears me. I can stand and walk short flat distances, but for a full day of marble Capitol hallways, my orthopedic specialist ordered me to use the manual chair and let my Bug guard the push-handles."

"And we came up on the Hampton Roads transit advocacy bus this morning for Transit Legislative Day," Rachel added proudly, adjusting her glasses with a grin. "Technically, our family home on Moosewood Drive is just across the district line in the Eighty-First District, and our campus apartment at VDU is in Norfolk's Eighty-Ninth, so you aren't officially our Delegate on our voter registration cards—but when there's a Virginia Beach Delegate in the General Assembly who actually rides CVTA buses every day and knows the Route 25 and Route 2 timetables by heart, she's our Delegate too! After we finished lobbying our assigned offices for evening bus frequency and Sunday service hours, there was no way we were leaving Richmond without coming up to the fifth floor to see our old Delaney Street and Greenway Court neighbors."

"Wait—were the two of you up in the third-floor House gallery at noon today?" I asked, spotting the green visitor gallery passes tucked into their lanyards.

"Front row center, right behind the brass rail!" Michael laughed, his eyes lighting up. "Katie, watching you hold up the text of House Bill 663 and ask Delegate Cobb why any woman in Virginia should have to pull her pants down for a government official to prove she has female parts just to use the ladies' room was the greatest thing I have ever seen in a government building. Nate, the second you posted the captioned video link at twelve-thirty-five, Rachel and I sent it to every student group chat at VDU and CCHR."

"And tell them what you promised yourself in the hallway afterward, Bear," Rachel said, nudging his shoulder with a laugh.

Michael grinned sheepishly from his blue manual wheelchair. "When we took the elevator downstairs after the floor session let out for lunch, I was still so fired up about that ridiculous bill that I made a solemn promise to myself and Rachel: if I saw Delegate Cobb—or Delegate Robert Marsh, who keeps talking to reporters about bringing his own bathroom bill—heading toward the men's room anywhere in the General Assembly Building while we were making our Transit Legislative Day rounds, I was going to wheel right up to the bathroom door and demand that he pull his pants down right there in the corridor to prove he had male parts!"

Katie clapped both hands over her mouth as she and I burst out laughing together across our armrests.

"Did you see either of them?" I asked through my laughter.

"Sadly, no," Michael sighed, shaking his head with mock disappointment. "Neither of them crossed our path all afternoon, so I never got the chance—wait until he sees how fast that video is spreading across VDU right now, though. By Monday morning, neither of those guys is going to want to show his face near a microphone."

After spending twenty wonderful minutes catching up on CVTA bus routes, our old first-floor hallway on Greenway Court, and their computer information systems classes at VDU, we rolled alongside Michael and Rachel out to the fifth-floor elevators so they could catch their late-afternoon transit coalition bus back down Interstate 64 to Hampton Roads.

Right as the elevator doors opened for Michael and Rachel at two-forty, an entire New River Valley transit delegation wearing blue *RIDE TRANSIT VIRGINIA* stickers rolled off the elevator and into Room 518—three closely knit families from Christiansburg and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute in Blacksburg who rode Blue Ridge Transit Route 1 and Route 2 and the Radford Connector around town, and who had been sitting in the third-floor House gallery right beside Michael and Rachel when Katie questioned Delegate Cobb on House Bill 663.

Rolling in first was [Jordan in his power wheelchair alongside Aubrie Jean, Megan, Zoë, and Isabel](https://books.raggiesoft.com/aubrie/spring-semester-2016/off-precautions-megan-s-first-night/part-2-virginia-transit-legislative-day-house-bill-663-delegate-katie-prairie-in-room-518), followed right beside them by [Freddie in his matte-black titanium manual wheelchair, his sixteen-year-old sister Harper in her neon-purple wheelchair, and their sisters Paige, Jessica, Sarah, and Jenna](https://books.raggiesoft.com/paige/system-integration/the-closed-circuit/part-3-virginia-transit-legislative-day-the-bathroom-police-bill-room-518), and right behind them came [Ryan O'Connell propelling his matte-black titanium manual wheelchair with his sister Cassidy holding onto his rear push-handles like a rolling walker alongside their youngest sister Holly and their twin cousins Evan and Tyler Wright](https://books.raggiesoft.com/stardust-engine/book-5-burnout-hiatus-our-own-frequency-neon-on-the-huckleberry-trail-2005-2016/chapter-1-our-own-frequency-the-blue-ridge-regional-aquatic-center-virginia-transit-legislative-day-2008-2016/part-3-virginia-transit-legislative-day-protocol-t-10-vs-house-bill-663-seven-wheelchairs-in-room-518).

"Four powerchairs and three ultralight manual chairs in one legislative office!" I grinned from beside my laptop as Katie and I wheeled our two black SwiftChairs out from behind her mahogany desk to form a wide semi-circle of seven wheelchairs in the center of Room 518. "Now this is what the General Assembly Building is supposed to look like on Transit Legislative Day—and wait a second, Katie, that's Ryan, Cassidy, and Holly O'Connell and Evan and Tyler Wright of The Stardust Engine!"

Even though they lived up in Blacksburg and Christiansburg rather than in the Eighty-Fifth House District—though Aubrie Jean beamed as she told us she had grown up right across the city line from Kempsville in the Emerald Creek neighborhood of Chesapeake before heading to CPI, and Katie's face lit up as she told Ryan and Cassidy that she and I listened to *Neon on the Huckleberry Trail* while compiling bus timetables at night and admired how Ryan's rear push-handles were set at the exact height for Cassidy to use as a rolling walker—every single one of them hated House Bill 663 just as fiercely as we did. Speaking through the voice synthesizer on his tablet, Jordan thanked Katie for standing up against a bill that would turn public restrooms into hostile fifty-dollar police checkpoints for wheelchair users and the family members who help them; Freddie, adjusting his glasses with Harper's hand anchored firmly to his right sleeve and Paige's hands resting on his shoulders, told Katie that her ninety-five-second floor cross-examination had exposed a fatal runtime error in Line 18 of Delegate Cobb's statute; and Ryan and Holly thanked Katie on behalf of disabled travelers across the state and Engine Room Records. Before they headed back downstairs to return to Christiansburg and Progress Street NE in Blacksburg, Holly, Zoë, Aubrie Jean, Paige, and Jenna had already pulled out their tablets and phones and shared our captioned ninety-five-second floor video with every student organization, computer science mailing list, Engine Room Records channel, and transit group across Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute.

We spent the rest of the afternoon until five-thirty meeting with special-education parents from Virginia Beach and reviewing coastal flooding amendments with our legislative aide, with both of our laptops closed on our desks.

## Thursday, February 4, 2016, 6:00 PM — The Evening News in Richmond and Norfolk

"Hand me the pepper shaker, Nate," Katie said at six o'clock that Thursday evening, setting two warm plates of baked chicken and rice down on the dining table of Apartment 104 at the Broad & Boulevard Lofts on West Broad Street after we had ridden the 5:35 PM westbound JRTC Route 6 bus home from Ninth and Broad. "And turn on the television so we can see the weather forecast for the weekend."

Picking up the remote control from the corner of the table, I clicked on the wall-mounted television—tuned first to the Richmond local six-o'clock news broadcast, with my laptop open beside my glass of ginger ale streaming our hometown six-o'clock broadcast from Norfolk so we could keep up with Hampton Roads news while we were in Richmond for the winter session.

I barely had time to set the remote down before both my twin sister and I froze with our forks halfway to our mouths.

On the television screen on the wall—and simultaneously on the Norfolk local news stream playing on my laptop—was the exact ninety-five-second video clip I had captured from my wheelchair bay on the House Floor at lunchtime.

*"—and our top story from the State Capitol tonight,"* the Richmond evening anchor announced over the footage of Katie sitting upright in her compact SwiftChair on the House Floor holding up the two printed pages of House Bill 663, *"a fiery floor exchange in the House of Delegates this afternoon over House Bill 663, the controversial bathroom bill restricting public restrooms by 'anatomical sex,' after Virginia Beach Delegate Katie Prairie brought the House Chamber to a standstill with a single question for the bill's patron."*

Before our eyes, both the Richmond station and the Norfolk station aired the floor clip in full—every word of Katie asking Delegate Martin Cobb why she, or any woman or girl in the Commonwealth of Virginia, should have to pull her pants down for a government official or a police officer to prove she had female parts just to use the ladies' room, followed by the four seconds of Delegate Cobb standing frozen and beet-red behind his desk microphone before the Majority Leader hurriedly passed the bill by for the day.

Katie stared at the television screen, her blue eyes wide. "Nate... they're playing the whole floor clip on the six-o'clock news. In Richmond and back home in Norfolk."

I reached over and woke my laptop browser window, refreshing the server dashboard for Katie's legislative archive and The Grid.

My jaw dropped.

"Michael and Rachel weren't kidding when they said they shared your clip with everyone at VDU and CCHR!" I said, turning the laptop screen toward her wheelchair. "Look at the traffic logs! Between the Transit Legislative Day riders sharing your link from the Capitol and the beat reporters from the Richmond and Norfolk papers embedding your captioned video on their blogs at two-thirty while we were in meetings, thousands of office workers and college students at Virginia Dominion University, CCHR, and Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute watched it on their phones all afternoon. That's how the assignment desks at the Richmond and Norfolk TV stations grabbed it for the six-o'clock news!"

## Friday, February 5, 2016, 9:15 AM — The Speakerphone Call on Line One, Two Twin Meltdowns, & The Majority Leader's Triplets

The black multi-line telephone on Katie's desk in our fifth-floor General Assembly Building office rang once on Line One at nine-fifteen on Friday morning, and just as she had done ever since her very first week on the Virginia Beach City Council, Katie reached over from her SwiftChair to press the speakerphone button herself.

In almost every other legislative office in the Capitol complex, a constitutional officer never picked up their own main telephone line; incoming calls were strictly screened by a legislative aide or session receptionist at the front desk so the member wouldn't be interrupted by cold calls. Teaching Katie to let a ringing phone go unanswered when she was sitting right beside it, however, was impossible. In Katie's literal autistic mind, Room 518 was her office, her name was on the brass plate outside the door, and if she was at her desk with a free hand, the polite, honest thing to do was answer the phone herself so the constituent on the other end knew she was actually there working for them. At this point in our time in Richmond—and certainly up in Washington—people outside Virginia Beach were still learning that Katie answered her own phone directly, and as she pressed the button, she fully expected the caller to be a retired shipyard worker from Kempsville reporting a missed CVTA bus connection on Princess Anne Road.

"Delegate Katie Prairie's office, this is Katie speaking," she said, keeping one hand resting on her open municipal transit binder while I sat right beside her left wheel with my silver MagnaBook Pro laptop open on the side table.

There was a brief, startled pause on the other end of the speakerphone. "Oh! Good morning, Delegate Prairie—is this really Delegate Prairie herself? I honestly expected your front-desk scheduler or a legislative assistant to pick up the main switchboard line. My name is Arthur Vance, and I'm a senior producer for *Meet the Nation* at GBS News up here in Washington. I hope I'm not catching you at a bad time?"

Katie blinked, glancing sideways at me in surprise. To the two of us, *Meet the Nation* was the quiet Sunday morning news program our dad David used to put on the living room television in Virginia Beach while the three of us ate scrambled eggs and toast together.

"Not at all, Mr. Vance," Katie said politely. "My brother Nate and I were just looking over some bus shelter amendments. How can I help you?"

"Well, Delegate, David Vance and our editorial team here in Washington watched your exchange on the House Floor yesterday afternoon over House Bill 663," Mr. Vance said warmly, his voice remarkably courteous and unhurried. "You raised some serious constitutional questions about how a law like that could ever be enforced in public buildings without violating the Fourth Amendment. David would be honored if you would join him this Sunday morning at nine o'clock for a quiet, one-on-one conversation to walk through the text of the bill—if you'd be willing to make it a Sunday morning exclusive with *Meet the Nation*."

Katie's shoulders visibly relaxed in her SwiftChair. After answering questions from the local Richmond and Norfolk newspaper reporters the evening before, the word *exclusive* sounded like a wonderful relief to her: it meant she would only have to put on a stiff blazer, sit under bright lights, state the literal facts of House Bill 663 one time, and then go straight back to our apartment in Richmond without having to repeat the exact same sentences to anyone else.

"Do you mean if I talk to Mr. Vance at nine o'clock on Sunday morning, I only have to tell the story one time and nobody else will ask me to repeat it all afternoon?" Katie asked literally.

On the speakerphone, Arthur Vance let out a soft, warm laugh. "That is the exact agreement, Delegate Prairie. Just you and David Vance one-on-one at nine o'clock sharp, zero cross-talk, and you're completely done by nine-twenty. We would be happy to send a car down to your address in Richmond at five-thirty Sunday morning to drive you up Interstate 95—"

"Oh, you don't need to waste money on a car, Mr. Vance," Katie said right away. "My brother Nate and I always ride public transit and pay our own regular fare, and a car would just get stuck in traffic on the 14th Street Bridge anyway. The suburban Staples Mill station doesn't have Sunday bus service yet, so we'll take the early JRTC bus down Broad Street to Richmond Main Street Station, ride the six-oh-five ARTS Northeast Regional train up to Alexandria, and use our blue CapitalPass cards from our family trips with our dad to take the Capital Transit Yellow Line across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge into L'Enfant Plaza, and then transfer to the Route P6 bus across the National Mall right to your door on North Capitol Street. We'll be at your studio desk by eight-thirty—and then we can catch the eleven-twelve train out of Alexandria with the connecting ARTS Thruway shuttle back to Main Street Station so I can get back to our apartment and out of my dress blazer by two o'clock."

"You two are taking a Richmond city bus, an intercity train, the Yellow Line subway across the Potomac, and a D.C. bus across the National Mall?" Mr. Vance asked, sounding genuinely charmed. "Well, whatever makes you most comfortable, Delegate. We'll have security passes waiting at the Washington bureau front desk at eight-thirty Sunday morning. Thank you so much for giving us your word."

"Thank you for being so polite, Mr. Vance," Katie said, and pressed the button to end the call.

Only as the speakerphone clicked off at nine-twenty-two did Katie and I look down at the plastic telephone console on her desk.

While we had been talking cleanly on Line One for the last four minutes, the little red hold lights on Line Two, Line Three, Line Four, and Line Five had all started blinking furiously at the exact same time, though the ringer had been automatically muted while the speakerphone was active.

Before I could even ask Katie why the whole console was lit up like a Christmas tree, our office door flew open. Katie's twenty-four-year-old legislative aide stumbled in carrying a thick stack of pink paper message slips, followed two seconds later by Greg, the Communications Director for the Virginia House Democratic Caucus, who was holding two vibrating cell phones in his hands and breathing like he had just sprinted all the way up the stairs from the third floor.

"Katie! Do not touch that phone until I clear a schedule!" Greg gasped, leaning over the front edge of her desk. "*Capitol Sunday* is holding on Line Two, *This Sunday* is on Line Three, *Beltway Sunday* is on Line Four, and the cable networks are blowing up my cell phone! They all want to book you for Sunday morning! We can set up a full Sunday morning sweep right now—four television shows back-to-back from eight in the morning until noon—"

"Oh, no thank you," Katie said calmly, shaking her head. "I would hate sitting in a stiff blazer repeating the exact same story four times in a row. Besides, I already promised Mr. Vance on Line One that I would only talk to *Meet the Nation*."

The room went dead silent.

Greg stared down at Katie, then over at my wheelchair, his mouth opening and closing without a sound.

"You... you answered your own phone and promised an exclusive to *Meet the Nation* without talking to me first?" he whispered, his face flushing red.

I looked up from my laptop keyboard, confused by his sudden anger. "Mr. Vance called while we were looking at CVTA bus stops. He was the first one to ask, he was very polite to Katie, and he told her that if she goes on at nine o'clock, she only has to explain the bill once and then we can catch the eleven-twelve train out of Alexandria and come home."

"Yeah," Katie agreed simply. "Why would I want to go on four television shows on a Sunday morning when I can explain the statute once at nine o'clock and be back in our apartment in sweatpants by two in the afternoon?"

Greg slowly lowered both of his ringing cell phones to his sides, staring at the two of us with his jaw clenched. "I have to go tell the other three networks you're off the board," he muttered tightly, backing out of our office doorway into the outer reception room.

As he pulled the heavy oak door behind him, the latch didn't quite catch, leaving a two-inch gap between the frame and the jamb.

Through that crack, Katie and I heard every single word as Greg snatched up the blinking phones at the legislative aide's desk in the outer room and started barking into the handsets back-to-back.

"Look, Sarah, I'm telling you, I can't put her on *Capitol Sunday*!" we heard him shout into Line Two, his voice loud and exasperated. "She's a backbench delegate from Virginia Beach who has never dealt with national press in her life! She picked up her own main office line five minutes ago before my staff could even get up the stairs and promised an exclusive to *Meet the Nation*! She had no idea what she was doing!"

Beside me, Katie's hands froze on the edges of her transit binder.

Out in the reception room, Greg slammed down Line Two, punched Line Three, and started all over again. "No, I'm not freezing *This Sunday* out on purpose! Delegate Prairie doesn't know how Washington booking rules work—she literally answered her own phone and gave away a Sunday morning exclusive without clearing it with Caucus Communications or the Minority Leader's office! Now I have to go down to the third floor and explain to leadership and the entire Democratic Caucus what just happened!"

The outer hall door banged shut as he hurried down the corridor toward the stairs.

Inside Room 518, the silence felt suffocating.

I looked over at my twin sister, and my stomach dropped right through the floor. Katie's fingers were trembling violently against the yellow highlighter in her lap, her breathing was coming in shallow, rapid gasps, and every trace of color had drained out of her face.

For two autistic twins who had spent our whole lives relying on literal written rules to survive, there was no phrase on earth more terrifying than *unwritten rules*. Whenever someone in authority started shouting about protocols that weren't printed in any handbook—about failing to clear things with leadership or breaking rules nobody had ever told us existed—it sent every alarm bell in our nervous systems screaming at once.

"Nate..." Katie whispered, her voice shaking so badly she could barely get my name out as her chest began to heave. "Did I just break a House rule? Was I not allowed to talk to Mr. Vance on the telephone without asking the Caucus first?"

"I—I don't know, sis," I stammered, and the raw panic in my own voice immediately set off a feedback loop between the two of us. My own hands were shaking so hard on the keyboard of my laptop that I could barely click open the PDF of the Rules of the Virginia House of Delegates, my eyes darting frantically down the table of contents for anything about national television interviews or caucus clearance while Katie's breathing grew faster and louder beside me. "It's not in the written standing rules on my laptop, Katie, I swear it isn't, but the way Greg just ran downstairs to report you to the Minority Leader..."

Katie burst into tears, a full-blown autistic meltdown overtaking her as she buried her face in both hands and began sobbing and shaking from head to toe.

"What if they kick me out of the Caucus, Nate?!" Katie cried, gasping for air between sobs as her whole body trembled in her wheelchair. "What if I broke an ethics rule and they expel me from the House of Delegates?! All the voters in Virginia Beach trusted me, and Dad was so proud of us, and I ruined everything just because I answered the telephone!"

Seeing my twin sister in a full autistic meltdown—and spiraling straight into a sympathetic meltdown of my own as the terrifying thought seized my chest that Katie was really about to be kicked out of the House of Delegates because neither of us understood some secret Richmond rule—I released the electromagnetic brakes of my SwiftChair with a trembling hand, wheeled myself flush against the side of her chair, and wrapped both of my arms around her shoulders, hugging her as tight as I could across our armrests.

Katie pressed her wet face into the shoulder of my charcoal suit jacket, sobbing uncontrollably against my lapel while my own tears spilled hot down my cheeks. I held onto my sister with everything I had, resting my chin against the top of her blonde hair while my chest shook right alongside hers and my heart hammered at a hundred and thirty beats a minute.

Just then, out of the corner of Katie's tear-blurred eye—and mine—both of us saw three figures appear together in the open outer doorway of our fifth-floor reception room.

It was thirteen-year-old Sam Hawkins and his thirteen-year-old sisters, Amanda—who went by Mandy—and Sarah Hawkins: three eighth-grade triplets who were all serving together this session as middle-school House Pages. Sam was the fraternal triplet, born first, while Mandy and Sarah were identical twins, born second and third. All three of them wore the iconic daily uniform of a crisp navy blue House Page blazer with the official Virginia Page patch on the breast pocket, a brass House Page name badge reading HAWKINS, and a button-down shirt—Sam in gray slacks and a striped tie that looked almost identical to what I wore to work every day, and Mandy and Sarah in matching gray skirts and navy Page ties—with Sam clutching a stack of yellow transportation committee bill folders against his chest while Mandy and Sarah held onto either side of his blazer sleeve.

They were eighth-graders from down near Danville along the North Carolina line—the rural region everybody at the State Capitol called Southside Virginia. When Katie and I had first arrived in Richmond from Virginia Beach and Norfolk, we had been completely baffled whenever senior lawmakers referred to legislators from the Southside. Growing up on our side of the harbor, whenever Katie and I heard the word Southside, we automatically thought of Southside Hampton Roads—everything south of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel from Norfolk and Portsmouth out to Kempsville and the Oceanfront. It had actually been Sam, Mandy, and Sarah who politely explained to us during Page orientation week in January with a printed highway map of Route 58 that in Richmond Capitol politics, Southside meant the rural tobacco and textile counties stretching west from Emporia to Danville and Martinsville, well over a hundred miles inland from our coast.

Right after that map lesson during the first week of January—when the thirteen- and fourteen-year-old Pages were getting started learning how state government worked—an impatient senior legislator had barked three vague errands at the triplets at once in the noisy hallway outside the Clerk's office. At the sound of an adult shouting at them, all three thirteen-year-olds had frozen against the marble wall, clutching each other's hands so tightly their knuckles turned white as they trembled on the verge of a sensory and panic meltdown. Recognizing that look in an instant, Katie and I had rolled our two SwiftChairs right up in front of them to block the hallway foot traffic and give them a quiet protective perimeter. Speaking in a soft, calm voice, Katie had told Sam, Mandy, and Sarah that she and I were autistic too, and that just like the two of us were each other's safe person, we could tell the three of them were each other's safe person too.

All three triplets had looked up at Katie with wide, shining eyes and told her that all three of them were autistic as well. Ever since that afternoon, Katie had been Sam, Mandy, and Sarah's absolute favorite Delegate in the entire chamber. Whenever Katie pressed her Page Call button on the House Floor, all three of them practically sprinted together to her wheelchair bay—because Katie never shouted, never used sarcasm, never gave them conflicting tasks at once, and always wrote the exact room number and bill number clearly on a sticky note before thanking Sam, Mandy, and Sarah by name. Even more than that, after watching Katie and me famously ride the city bus down Broad Street every morning and take the intercity passenger train between Hampton Roads and Richmond, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah had decided that public transportation was the biggest issue they wanted to work on as House Pages—volunteering for every House Transportation Committee courier run and choosing statewide bus and passenger rail expansion as their Page legislative project.

Standing together in our fifth-floor doorway, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah froze in their tracks as they saw their favorite Delegate crying and shaking in her wheelchair. Their eyes went wide with alarm, Mandy and Sarah grabbed Sam's hands, and without a single word, all three triplets spun on their heels and took off running together down the hallway toward the elevators.

In our panic, watching even the three young House Pages bolt from our doorway only made us hold onto each other tighter, terrified that the entire Capitol was about to find out Katie had broken a rule and was being expelled.

Three minutes later, urgent footsteps echoed from both ends of the fifth-floor corridor at the exact same moment.

From the marble stairwell came Democratic House Minority Leader Tom Toscano with Greg trailing half a step behind him—and at the exact same second, the brass elevator doors down the hall chimed open and Republican House Majority Leader Richard Callaway came racing down the corridor right alongside thirteen-year-old Sam, Mandy, and Sarah.

"Right in here, Uncle Richard!" Sam panted outside our doorway, pointing straight toward our open inner door while Mandy and Sarah nodded urgently beside him.

"That press man in the outer office was yelling so loud about breaking an unwritten rule and reporting her," Mandy said breathlessly, holding tight to Sarah's hand, "and the Gentlewoman from Virginia Beach is in there crying in her wheelchair!"

Both the Democratic Minority Leader and the Republican Majority Leader reached our doorway at the same time and stopped dead in their tracks as they saw the two of us huddled together in our power wheelchairs—Katie sobbing into my shoulder in a full autistic meltdown, and me crying and shaking right along with her like I was trying to shield my twin sister from an oncoming train.

"Whoa, whoa, hold on a second," the Minority Leader breathed, the color draining from his own face as he realized what had happened. He spun on his heel and shot a furious glare at the communications director—"Stay out here in the hall, Greg. Do not move a muscle"—before stepping quietly into Room 518 right alongside the Republican Majority Leader, with Sam, Mandy, and Sarah standing right between them clutching each other's sleeves and the stack of yellow transportation committee bill folders.

The two floor leaders pulled the wooden guest chairs right up in front of our footrests so they were sitting side by side at eye level with us.

Katie lifted her tear-stained face an inch off my shoulder, gasping for breath and wiping furiously at her red eyes with the back of her trembling wrist as she tried to sit up in her chair.

"Mr. Minority Leader, Mr. Majority Leader, I am so sorry," Katie choked out through her tears, her whole frame still shaking against my arms. "I didn't know there was a rule that a delegate wasn't allowed to answer Line One or agree to talk to a television show without asking the Caucus first! When Mr. Vance asked for an exclusive, I only said yes because we used to watch that show with our dad over breakfast and I thought it meant I could tell the story one time and come home on the eleven-twelve train! Please don't kick me out of the House of Delegates—I'll call him back right now and cancel it!"

"Please don't expel her, sirs," I pleaded, my own voice cracking as my hands shook on Katie's shoulders. "I searched the whole House Manual on my laptop and we didn't know—"

Both floor leaders looked at Katie and me, and their faces softened with deep, unmistakable gentleness.

"Expel her? Kicked out of the House?" the Democratic Minority Leader repeated softly, holding up both hands palm-out in a calm, steady gesture. "Katie, Nate—look at both of us. Take a slow breath. You are not getting kicked out of the House of Delegates, you are not getting kicked out of the Democratic Caucus, and you did not break a single rule. Not a written rule, and not an unwritten rule."

Seeing Katie's eyes still pink around the edges and my wheelchair parked tight against hers, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah stepped half a pace forward past their uncle toward Katie's desk.

"Delegate Prairie?" Sam asked quietly, holding out the stack of yellow folders with both hands while Mandy and Sarah stood shoulder to shoulder with him. "We brought the morning transportation dockets you asked for on the sticky note. Are you and Nate okay?"

"When that man in the outer office started shouting that you broke an unwritten rule, we got really scared for you," Sarah added in a small, earnest voice, her fingers laced through Mandy's. "So the three of us ran right downstairs to get Uncle Richard."

Katie blinked in astonishment at the words *Uncle Richard*, looking from the brass HAWKINS name badges on Sam, Mandy, and Sarah's navy blazers up to the Republican Majority Leader before offering all three triplets a warm, steady smile and taking the folders gently from Sam's hands to set them neatly on the corner of her blotter. "Thank you, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah. These are the exact bills I needed, and you three were right on time. Nate and I are okay now."

The Republican Majority Leader rested his hands gently on the shoulders of his nephew and nieces, taking off his reading glasses and tucking them into his breast pocket as he looked at Katie and me with his warm Danville drawl.

"You two have been looking out for Sam, Mandy, and Sarah on the House Floor every single day since January," Majority Leader Callaway said, his voice thick with genuine emotion. "Because I'm their maternal uncle—their mother's brother—their Page badges say Hawkins while my desk plaque downstairs says Callaway, so neither of you had any way of knowing. My wife—their Aunt Marge Callaway—and I have custody of all three of them. We rescued these three kids and took them into our home about a year ago after the unspeakable things their mother and stepfather put them through."

Inside Room 518, both Katie and I inhaled sharply, our hearts clenching as we looked at the three thirteen-year-old triplets clinging to each other's hands beside their uncle—and in a single heartbeat, remembering our own mother Brenda and how our dad David had fought to rescue the two of us when we were sixteen, along with how Katie's controlling jerk of a boyfriend Brandon Mercer in Williamsburg in 2004 and my manipulative girlfriend Tiffany Sloan in Ocean View in 2005 had used every closed-door trick in the book trying—and failing—to drive a wedge between us under the same roof, we understood everything about why Sam, Mandy, and Sarah never let go of each other in the hallways.

"After what Sam, Mandy, and Sarah survived before Marge and I got them safe, they are each other's safe person, and Marge and I don't let anyone raise a hand or a voice to them ever again," Uncle Richard continued quietly, drawing the three triplets close against his sides. "Out of all one hundred members of this House—including their own Uncle Richard—you are their absolute favorite Delegate in the entire building, and running errands for your desk is their favorite activity in the Capitol. Over dinner with their Aunt Marge and me on Sunday night, all three of them couldn't stop talking about how Delegate Prairie's wheelchair bay is the only spot on the carpet where nobody ever yells at them or barks confusing orders on the fly, and how much it meant to them when you shielded them in the hallway with your wheelchairs and told them that you and Nate were autistic too. So when Sam, Mandy, and Sarah come sprinting into my office telling me somebody is up on the fifth floor shouting about unwritten rules at their favorite delegate from the other Southside, that's my business too—party lines be damned."

Katie blinked, a tear of pure gratitude slipping down her cheek as a warm smile broke across her face, looking from Sam, Mandy, and Sarah up to the Majority Leader's eyes while the last trace of panic in my chest melted away completely.

"Now, you and I sit on opposite sides of that green carpet downstairs, Delegate, and lord knows you got my Republican caucus all up in arms yesterday afternoon over House Bill 663," the Majority Leader continued warmly. "That's just politics. We're going to disagree on plenty of votes while you're in Richmond. Personally, though? Marge and I like you a whole lot, Delegate Prairie, because of how kindly you treat Sam, Mandy, and Sarah every single day—and after Marge and I spent all month coaching these three through their Page Manual at our kitchen table, a staffer bullying you and your brother into an autistic meltdown over made-up unwritten rules is personal to me."

He pointed a finger toward the copy of the Manual of the House of Delegates sitting beside Katie's telephone.

"Here is the only rule you, Nate, and my three kids here ever need to know in the Capitol," the Majority Leader said firmly. "Every single unwritten rule you hear a staffer or a lobbyist holler about in the hallways of Richmond is completely made up. The only rules that govern this House are the Constitution of Virginia, the printed House Manual, and the Page Manual—and I know for a fact the five of you are the only people on this floor who have actually read every word of them."

Beside their uncle, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah nodded solemnly in unison.

"Page forty-two," Sam said clearly.

"It says a Delegate controls her own office communications at all times," Mandy added, looking straight at Katie.

"And it doesn't say a single word about asking a press guy first," Sarah finished.

Both the Democratic Minority Leader and the Republican Majority Leader burst out laughing, and Katie let out a bright, genuine laugh too, reaching over to give my forearm a squeeze.

"Sam, Mandy, and Sarah are better parliamentarians than half the lawyers on the Courts of Justice Committee," the Minority Leader chuckled, clapping a hand on his colleague's shoulder before reaching into his breast pocket and handing Katie a clean, folded white handkerchief. "And as for why Greg was losing his mind over your phone call, Katie, it's because neither of you saw what happened to your floor video overnight, did you? After the local stations in Richmond and Norfolk aired your question on the six-o'clock news last night, the national news desks in Washington and New York picked it up. Your ninety-five-second floor clip crossed ten million views across the United States before sunrise this morning."

Both Katie and I froze in our wheelchairs, staring at the two leaders with wide eyes. "Ten million?" I whispered.

"Ten million and climbing," the Minority Leader nodded warmly. "And what you did on the telephone ten minutes ago without even trying, Katie, is the single biggest triumph our Caucus has had in a decade. *Meet the Nation* isn't just a Sunday morning show—it is the most prestigious public affairs broadcast in the United States. Members of Congress in Washington spend ten years lobbying just to get invited onto that set. Every President, every U.S. Senator, every Governor, and every Fortune 500 CEO in America watches *Meet the Nation* at nine o'clock on Sunday morning. Because you picked up your own phone and politely agreed to give Arthur Vance an exclusive so you only had to tell your story once, he gave a Virginia Beach delegate the solo nine o'clock A-Block lead at the very top of the broadcast."

"Do you know how many sitting Governors and U.S. Senators in both parties would give their right arm to get invited onto the opening A-Block of *Meet the Nation* for a one-on-one exclusive with David Vance?" Majority Leader Callaway added with a chuckle. "Whenever some overcaffeinated press staffer starts hollering because you answered your own phone instead of playing Washington media games, you two didn't do a doggone thing wrong—he did. My job as Majority Leader is to help my caucus govern effectively, not assure our members that everything they write is brilliant. As I told the gentleman from Spotsylvania and Speaker Harwell right after the gavel yesterday—and as Sam, Mandy, and Sarah proudly informed Marge and me over supper last night after telling us how Sarah looked at Line 18 on your desk before noon and asked if somebody had to see her parts to prove she was a girl—you didn't kill his bill, Delegate. His bill killed his bill. Half my own caucus wishes he had put House Bill 663 through the Prairie-and-triplet quality-assurance test before he embarrassed the whole Commonwealth on television."

"Wait," I whispered, the vise around my ribs finally vanishing completely. "So when Greg went downstairs to tell the Caucus what Katie did..."

"We were cheering, Nate!" the Minority Leader smiled, standing up from his chair. "The only people upset this morning are the booking producers at the other three networks who called three minutes too late—and that's their problem, not yours. Now, if you will excuse me for sixty seconds, I'm going to step out into the hallway and make sure Greg understands how things work in this building."

"They take after their favorite delegate," the Majority Leader said, resting a proud hand on Sam, Mandy, and Sarah's shoulders and giving Katie a warm wink as he stood up beside the Minority Leader. "In fact, because the three of them know how famously you and Nate ride the bus and the train everywhere you go, they already told Marge and me that public transportation is the number-one issue they want to work on as House Pages—they've been drafting their Page Mock Session bill all week to connect rural Southside bus routes in Danville and Martinsville with the passenger trains to Richmond and Hampton Roads."

"Because everyone should be able to take a bus or a train wherever they need to go, just like you and Nate do, Delegate Prairie!" Mandy beamed, while Sam and Sarah nodded enthusiastically beside her.

"We'll help you three check every schedule table in your bill on Monday afternoon," Katie promised warmly, her eyes crinkling with genuine joy.

"And just so we're clear on the bipartisan record," the Majority Leader added with a kindly smile, "if you and Nate are taking the six-oh-five train out of Main Street Station on Sunday morning to go talk to David Vance on *Meet the Nation*, don't let anyone in this building bother you all weekend. Safe travels across the Potomac, Delegate—and Marge, the kids, and I will all be watching you Sunday morning before we see you at noon on Monday to strike House Bill 663 off the calendar for good."

As the two leaders turned toward the door, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah all gave Katie and me a bright, relieved wave together before stepping back out into the fifth-floor hallway beside their Uncle Richard.

Once the heavy oak door latched shut behind them—and while the marble acoustics of the corridor outside carried thirty seconds of the Minority Leader blistering Greg by the stairwell and ordering him to apologize to both Katie and me before noon—Katie looked over at me across our touching armrests inside our quiet office, the color completely back in her cheeks and her hands steady on the yellow transportation folders Sam, Mandy, and Sarah had delivered.

"Page forty-two," Katie said softly, smiling as I pulled our bank card out of my wallet and clicked the confirmation button on my laptop to lock in our Business Class wheelchair bays on Sunday morning's ARTS Northeast Regional Train 164 from Richmond Main Street Station to Alexandria, along with the eleven-twelve southbound return on Train 143 and its connecting ARTS Thruway 8443 shuttle. "A Delegate controls her own office communications at all times."
