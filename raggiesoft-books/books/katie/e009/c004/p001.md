## Monday, March 14, 2016, 4:30 PM — Sine Die, Unloading Boxes on Arvin Road, & Brittany Joining Us Inside the Closet

"Careful with the bottom flap on that one, Steven—that's all of her math workbooks and flashcard boxes!" Aunt Karen called out cheerfully from the top of our aluminum front-porch ramp on Arvin Road as Uncle Steven carried a taped cardboard carton past our two parked SwiftChairs into the living room.

"Got it right against my belt buckle, Karen," Uncle Steven grinned, setting the box gently beside the sofa while our dad followed right behind him with Brittany's quilted winter duffel bag slung over one shoulder and a freshly cut brass front-door key resting in his palm.

Three days after the Speaker's wooden gavel had dropped on the final afternoon of the 2016 General Assembly session in Richmond, the March breeze blowing off the pines along Indian River Road smelled of salt air, damp oak bark, and home. Out by the curb behind Dad's accessible minivan, Uncle Steven's pickup truck sat with its tailgate down and only two laundry baskets left in the bed. Standing in the center of our sunlit hallway in her plum knit sweater and jeans, thirty-year-old Brittany adjusted her thin silver wire-rimmed oval glasses, looked around at the framed blue butterfly print down the hall and our dual-desk office across from our bedroom, and let out a long, quiet breath that seemed to let go of fourteen hard years all at once.

Dad stepped up beside Katie's armrest and held out the newly cut brass house key.

"Welcome home to Arvin Road, Brittany," Dad said warmly, closing her fingers gently around the brass bow before glancing down at Katie and me with proud eyes. "You three talked about sharing a roof back when you were walking home from Northwood High School. Took a few extra detours to get here, maybe, but a promise kept is a promise kept."

"Thank you, Uncle David," Brittany whispered, her voice catching slightly before she leaned down to hug Dad around the shoulders, hugged her mom and dad tight at the front door when they headed back over to Earlton Court for supper, and then stood with Katie and me in the quiet living room listening to the refrigerator hum.

For the rest of that Monday evening—after Dad drove back north up Interstate 64 and Interstate 95 toward Alexandria so he wouldn't miss his Tuesday morning shift in Tysons Corner—the three of us fell right back into the easy, unhurried rhythm we had known since the Blizzard of '96. We heated up a big pot of vegetable soup and grilled six golden cheddar sandwiches in the skillet, sorted Brittany's sweaters into the freshly cleared dresser drawers in Dad's spare room so she had plenty of closet rod space of her own, and quizzed her for an hour over ginger ale at the kitchen table from her Coastal Adult Learning Center placement packet.

At ten-fifteen, after we took turns brushing our teeth at the roll-under bathroom sink and changed into soft flannel pajamas, Katie and I rolled side by side into our bedroom and braked our SwiftChairs just short of the untouched navy-and-slate-blue queen bed.

Both of us hesitated at the exact same second, glancing shyly from the smooth comforter on the queen mattress over to the open double doors of our bedroom closet, where our wide navy-blue quilted sleeping bag and four pillows lay spread across the vacuumed carpet.

"Brittany," Katie began gently, lacing her fingers through mine across our wheelchair armrests, "back in Richmond on that Saturday night after the church, we brought you into the walk-in closet because you were terrified Tyler might look through the loft window. Now that he's locked inside the prison ward up at Tidewater General and can never hurt anybody again, you don't have to squeeze onto a closet floor on our account. Dad's spare room has a full mattress with clean sheets, or you can take this whole queen bed right here."

"Yeah," I added quietly, feeling the familiar warmth rise in my ears even with our closest cousin in the world. "Ever since Brenda bang-searched the back alley behind our apartment on Greenway Court when we were eighteen, Katie and I just... haven't been able to turn our brains off on an open bed. We always sleep in the closet. We don't want you thinking you're stuck on carpet just because our nervous systems are wired sideways."

Brittany stood by the foot of the queen bed looking down at the two of us behind her wire-rimmed lenses, and her whole face softened with fierce, unmistakable affection.

Without saying a word, she walked straight past the untouched queen bed, pulled a third soft pillow in a clean cotton case off the mattress, knelt down inside our wide bedroom closet, and tucked that third pillow snugly right beside Katie's and mine at the top of our oversized navy-blue sleeping bag.

"Scoot over and make room under the zipper, both of you," Brittany said softly, patting the open flannel-lined sleeping bag inside the closet alcove and smiling up at our wheelchairs.

Katie blinked in surprise. "Brittany, seriously—your back doesn't need to squeeze into a sleeping bag on the closet carpet just because ours do."

"My back slept better inside that West Broad Street sleeping bag between my two cousins than it slept in five straight years across the river," Brittany answered simply, reaching out to squeeze Katie's hand and then mine. "Listen to me, you two. You opened an elevator door for me when I didn't have an appointment or a friend left in the world. You sat in a church pew and stood up for me when I was shaking too hard to speak. Until the night both of you genuinely feel safe enough to climb into a proper bed without your hearts racing, I don't want a mattress out here by myself. My spot is right here in the closet sharing this sleeping bag and these pillows with you—and one day, when we're ready, we'll figure out the bed part together, as slow as it takes."

My throat went tight in the warm lamplight, and beside me Katie smiled with bright, grateful eyes.

Locking our SwiftChair brakes side by side at the closet threshold and clicking off our joystick controllers, Katie and I transferred smoothly down into our wide flannel-lined sleeping bag while Brittany climbed right inside the sleeping bag beside us with all three of our pillows lined up side by side, set her wire-rimmed glasses safely inside her felt case by the baseboard, and helped me pull the closet doors nearly shut until only a thin ribbon of hallway nightlight remained. With Katie nestled against my left shoulder holding my hand and Brittany wrapping a warm, protective arm across Katie's shoulder to clasp my forearm under the shared sleeping bag, all three of us drifted peacefully to sleep inside our sanctuary.

## Tuesday, March 22 & Thursday, May 26, 2016 — Riding Route 15 and Route 20 to the Coastal Adult Learning Center, Passing the HSE, & A Ninety-Second Call to Braxton

![Along the south sidewalk of eight-lane divided U.S. Route 58—Virginia Beach Boulevard—outside the single-story brick Coastal Adult Learning Center on a sunny March 2016 morning as an eastbound CVTA Route 20 transit bus (#2520) sits at the right-hand curb beside four approaching eastbound lanes and four receding westbound lanes across the raised grassy median, 30-year-old Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs smiling up at their 30-year-old cousin Brittany Fletcher standing safely on the concrete sidewalk in her thin silver wire-rimmed oval glasses, burgundy knit sweater, and blue jeans holding her blue HSE study binder.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/mar_2016_coastal_adult_learning_center_route_20_hse_enrollment_brittany_katie_nate.jpg)

*"Route 20. Virginia Beach Boulevard — Municipal Center / Oceanfront. Welcome aboard CVTA!"*

Eight mornings later under crisp March sunshine, the hydraulic front flip-ramp of the eastbound Route 20 bus folded back into the right-hand vestibule stepwell and the forty-foot coach eased back into the rightmost of the four eastbound lanes along the wide eight-lane divided expanse of U.S. Route 58—Virginia Beach Boulevard—leaving Katie, Brittany, and me on the sunny concrete sidewalk in front of the single-story brick Coastal Adult Learning Center.

Brittany stood very still for three seconds, clutching her navy three-ring study binder against her plum sweater as she stared at the glass entrance doors where adult students in scrubs, work boots, and hoodies were walking inside for the nine o'clock morning session.

"My palms are sweating worse than ninth-grade algebra," Brittany admitted softly, adjusting her glasses with two fingers. "Everyone inside is going to take one look at a thirty-year-old woman who walked out of Northwood High in the spring of 2003 and wonder what took me thirteen years."

Katie rolled her SwiftChair six inches forward and took Brittany's free hand firmly in hers while I rolled right up on Brittany's other side.

"Every single adult walking through those glass doors is here for the exact same reason—because life knocked them sideways once and they came back to finish what they started," Katie told her warmly. "And remember what Nate and I learned when we went back to finish our associate degrees at CCHR: nobody in an adult classroom cares what year you started. They only care that you showed up today."

"Besides," I grinned up at her from my chair, tapping the canvas messenger bag slung across my lap where Katie and I had packed our laptops to work remotely from the public library study tables half a block down while Brittany was in class, "we rode Route 15 up from Military Highway and transferred onto Route 20 right beside you, and we'll be waiting right out here on this bench at twelve-fifteen to quiz you on quadratic equations all the way home."

Brittany let out a genuine laugh, squeezed Katie's hand and mine, squared her shoulders, and walked through the glass doors with her head held high.

For the next nine weeks through April—including a cheerful thirty-first birthday supper Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven cooked for Katie, Brittany, and me with Dad down from Alexandria—and into late May, that morning bus relay down Military Highway and Virginia Beach Boulevard became our favorite part of every weekday. While Brittany tackled language arts, constitutional civics, laboratory science, and algebra inside the Coastal Adult Learning Center, Katie and I answered constituent emails for the Eighty-Fifth House District and wrote custom PHP inventory and scheduling applications for Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC. Every evening after supper on Arvin Road, the three of us spread index cards across the dining table until linear equations and reading-comprehension passages felt like second nature to Brittany, before brushing our teeth and curling up side by side inside our quiet bedroom closet.

Then, at five-thirty on the warm Thursday afternoon of May 26, when Dad drove down early for the Memorial Day weekend and sat with Katie and me around the Arvin Road kitchen table with four chilled bottles of ginger ale waiting on coasters, Brittany practically floated up the front porch ramp waving an official Commonwealth of Virginia testing printout stamped *PASSED — HIGH SCHOOL EQUIVALENCY CREDENTIAL AWARDED*.

"Four for four on the first try—and I got College-Ready honors on Language Arts and Social Studies!" Brittany cheered, throwing her arms around Dad, Katie, and me in turn as Katie clapped excitedly and Dad beamed like a proud father.

"And starting Monday morning," Katie announced happily, sliding a freshly printed independent-contractor household-and-operations agreement from Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC across the oak tabletop beside Brittany's ginger ale, "if you still want the job Nate and I talked about, Prairie Twin Interactive is officially hiring our favorite HSE graduate on private company payroll—zero state or taxpayer dollars—to help manage our back-office bookkeeping and household operations right here on Arvin Road."

Brittany's eyes misted behind her lenses as she signed her name neatly across the bottom line. "Are you kidding? Living and working right here with my favorite people on earth is the best job in Virginia."

As we clinked our four ginger ale glasses together over the signed page, Dad glanced up at the wall clock above the stove—six o'clock on the fourth Thursday of the month—and his warm smile tightened slightly around the corners of his eyes.

"Speaking of family," Dad said quietly, pulling his cell phone from his shirt pocket and setting it in the center of the table on speakerphone, "it's six o'clock on the fourth Thursday. That's the ten-minute window when Regalis Community House up in Braxton lets me call your Cousin Chelsea before their supper cleanup."

Brittany looked curiously from Dad to me. "Uncle David's niece up in Rhode Island—Aunt Liz's daughter?"

"Yeah," I said softly, feeling an old, nagging knot tighten in my chest every time I remembered hugging a crying twenty-two-year-old Chelsea goodbye at the top of her wooden ramp on Dirty Old Tonomy Hill on December 27, 2004 and whispering in her ear that next time it really would be just Katie and me. "After Newport tore down the old World War II public-housing apartments on Tonomy Hill a few years after Aunt Liz and Grandma died, Chelsea tried living on her own in a private apartment, and because of her developmental delays it fell apart. Rhode Island adult services placed her in a contracted group home on the edge of the King's College campus up in Braxton. Before Brittany moved home, with Katie and me in full-time powerchairs riding buses to Richmond and Dad working full-time up in Northern Virginia, we didn't have anyone ambulatory at home during the day who could safely help look after Chelsea."

On the table, the speakerphone rang four times before clicking open—not in a private bedroom, but with the hollow echo of a tiled institutional hallway.

*"Regalis Community House, Director Sterling speaking! How may we brighten your evening?"* a man's voice boomed through the speaker in syrupy, fake-jovial church-deacon sweetness that sounded like molasses poured over sheet metal.

"Good evening, Mr. Sterling—it's David Prairie calling from Virginia with Katie and Nate for our monthly check-in with my niece Chelsea," Dad said politely.

*"Why, Mr. Prairie! Wonderful to hear from you, sir!"* Director Sterling chuckled warmly without missing half a beat, though none of us heard footsteps walking away to give Chelsea privacy—only the heavy scuff of hard-soled men's dress loafers staying planted right beside the wall receiver. *"Chelsea, step right up here and tell your nice uncle in Virginia how wonderfully you're doing before seven-thirty quiet hours!"*

Katie, Brittany, and I all stiffened at the exact same instant. *Seven-thirty quiet hours?* Outside our kitchen window in late May, the sun was still bright above the pine tops, and up in Rhode Island it wouldn't even get dark until after eight-fifteen.

*"H-hi, Unca David... hi, Nate... Katie,"* thirty-four-year-old Chelsea's familiar, thickly slurred voice came through the line—halting, effortful, and pitched high with unmistakable fear as her severe speech impediment blurred the consonants together—followed immediately by a tight, whistling intake of breath deep in her bronchial tubes that I knew by heart from Christmas Eve 2004.

"Hi, Chelsea!" I leaned close to the speaker, keeping my voice warm and gentle while Katie gripped my left hand hard. "You sound a little wheezy tonight, Cous. Do you have your rescue inhaler right there in your cardigan pocket?"

For two full seconds, dead silence hung on the line—except for the faint rustle of Director Sterling's starch-stiff sleeve shifting right beside the microphone.

*"U-uh-huh... me fine, Nate,"* Chelsea whispered in a flat, scared cadence that sounded nothing like our affectionate cousin at all, working hard to push each short word out. *"G-gotta fold purple papers now... bedtime soon. Bye, Unca David."*

*"There we are—she's excited to finish her evening crafts with her housemates, Mr. Prairie!"* Director Sterling's syrupy baritone cut right back across the speaker before Chelsea could form another syllable. *"Have a blessed Memorial Day down in Virginia, folks! Buh-bye now!"*

*Click.*

Inside our sunlit kitchen on Arvin Road, nobody spoke for five seconds.

Brittany stared down at the silent phone on the oak table, her face pale behind her wire-rimmed glasses because she knew the sound of someone speaking with a warden standing two feet over their shoulder better than anyone in the room.

"She wasn't doing arts and crafts for fun, Uncle David," Brittany said quietly, her voice trembling with sudden, protective recognition. "That man was standing right over that wall phone glaring down at her—and she couldn't catch her breath."

Katie turned her SwiftChair sharply toward Dad and me, her blue eyes blazing with the exact forensic focus that had brought the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates to a standstill three months earlier.

"Seven-thirty bedtime in May, 'purple papers' for King's College, and Chelsea wheezing without her rescue inhaler while that man cuts the line off after ninety seconds," Katie said flatly. She looked across the table at Brittany, at Dad, and at me. "We aren't calling ahead next month so Mr. Sterling can rehearse her at the wall phone. We have Brittany right here under our roof now, we have Prairie Twin Interactive paying the bills, and Dad has vacation days saved up in July. We're driving Dad's wheelchair van straight up Interstate 95 to Braxton unannounced—and we're bringing our cousin home."
