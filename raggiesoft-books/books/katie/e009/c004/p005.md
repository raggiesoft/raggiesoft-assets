## Friday, October 14, 2016, 9:00 PM — Eighty Sardine Nights in the Closet, Our Nine-Foot Solid-Oak Alaskan King, & Retreating at Minute Twelve

"Six warm mugs of chamomile-mint, zero caffeine, and one very strict no-heroics rule," Brittany announced cheerfully, carrying a wooden tray down the Arvin Road hallway into our lamp-lit master bedroom alongside Heather on a crisp mid-October Friday night and setting six ceramic mugs along the built-in headboard shelf and side table of our brand-new solid-oak Alaskan King platform bed.

Right behind Brittany and Heather rolled Katie and me in our SwiftChairs, with Chelsea walking happily beside my armrest in soft cream tagless cotton pajamas—one hand resting gently on Katie's shoulder for balance and her other hand patting the two asthma inhalers tucked inside her stitched front puffer pockets—and Wesley walking right beside Heather in his navy-blue flannel pajamas turning his smooth blue silicone ring contentedly in his palm.

Reaching October together had taken steady, loving teamwork every single day—especially helping Chelsea remember to keep her clothes on inside the house. Because of her developmental disability, learning a brand-new routine didn't happen from a single conversation in July; over August and September, there had been plenty of warm afternoons after a walk or sleepy mornings after a bath when Chelsea simply forgot all over again, tugging a warm shirt right over her head in the living room because soup had made her toasty or stepping out of the bathroom carrying her towel instead of wearing it.

Every single time—whether it was reminder number three or reminder number fourteen—our whole household kept the exact same gentle, unhurried routine. Wesley and I would immediately turn our eyes respectfully toward our sketchpad or laptop screen without a hint of shock or irritation; Katie would roll up with a warm smile reminding Chelsea about keeping the cool air-conditioning draft off her lungs; and Heather and Brittany would step over with an airy, tagless short-sleeved cotton top fitted with soft front puffer pockets so Chelsea never felt scolded, embarrassed, or rushed. Just two hours earlier after Friday supper, when Chelsea's autumn sweater had felt too warm in the kitchen and her hands had gone straight to the bottom hem to pull it off altogether, she had paused halfway on her own, tapped her hip with a proud grin, and called out through her slurred speech impediment, *"Wait—me get cool shirt w-with puffer pockets!"* while Katie, Heather, Wesley, Brittany, and I cheered her around the dish rack.

Our nighttime sleeping arrangement, on the other hand, was leaving physical bruises on four of us—and raising an honest question for all six of us.

For eighty-five consecutive nights since late July, Katie, Brittany, Chelsea, and I had slept wedged wall to wall inside our six-foot bedroom closet underneath our two zipped-together navy sleeping bags with four pillows crammed in a row. Emotionally, none of us had ever felt safer. Physically, sixty-eight inches of thin residential carpet laid directly over plywood subflooring was never engineered for four adult skeletons. Every morning at seven-thirty, Brittany climbed out rubbing a stiff purple spot on her left hip where the closet drywall corner met the baseboard; Katie and I stretched aching shoulders and stiff lower backs as we transferred up into our wheelchairs; and at least twice a week, whenever Chelsea rolled over in her sleep hugging Shelly Turtle, her elbow accidentally bonked the point of my chin under our hanging winter parkas.

One Sunday afternoon in late August, after Dad chuckled gently at finding all of our winter coats re-hung inside the hall closet so our heads would stop tangling in parka zippers, Katie, Heather, Brittany, Dad, and I had sat down around the kitchen table with my MagnaBook Pro to face the practical puzzle waiting on the other side of that closet door: once Katie and I were finally coaxed out of the closet onto an open bed, who was actually going to sleep beside whom?

I loved Heather with my whole heart—she had been my wife for more than eight years—and I wanted to wake up holding her every morning, yet thirty-one years of autistic co-regulation meant my nervous system still slept deepest right beside my twin sister Katie. At the exact same time, down the hall in their two side-by-side bedrooms, Wesley still woke up two or three nights every week whenever thunder boomed or a siren wailed down Military Highway and climbed straight into Heather's bed so he wouldn't feel scared alone, while Brittany and Chelsea refused to hear a single word about sleeping down the hall away from Katie and me.

"Then stop trying to divide one household into little boxes," Heather had smiled warmly across the oak tabletop while Wesley rested his chin contentedly beside her shoulder. "Build one bed wide enough for all six of us, and move Wes onto it full-time right next to me. Nate gets his wife on one side and his twin sister on the other, Katie keeps Chelsea and Brittany right beside her, and Wes never has to wake up alone in a dark room wondering where I am—he'll go to sleep every single night knowing I'm right there beside him."

In late September, after Dad helped us shift the non-load-bearing study partition back three feet to widen the master bedroom, a master cabinetmaker off Witchduck Road had delivered and assembled that exact nine-foot-by-nine-foot solid-oak Alaskan King platform bed—one hundred and eight inches wide, built to the exact nineteen-inch seat-to-floor transfer height of our SwiftChairs so all six of us could stretch out side by side with plenty of breathing room, topped with deep orthopedic memory-foam mattresses, smooth bolstered oak side rails, a recessed headboard shelf for glasses and Chelsea's inhalers, and a custom oversized navy-and-cream patchwork quilt.

For its first two weeks in our bedroom, however, Katie, Chelsea, and I still hadn't lasted past sunset on top of it—until Brittany and Heather proposed their twenty-minute chamomile-tea plan tonight.

"Remember the deal before anybody transfers," Brittany said warmly, picking up a small round brass kitchen timer and turning its dial solely to the twenty-minute mark before setting it beside the bedside lamp. "Nobody is forced to sleep out here tonight. Your giant sleeping bag is still spread out ready and waiting inside the open closet, and Heather's bed down the hall still has fresh sheets for Heather and Wes if we call time early. All six of us are just stretching out under the patchwork quilt until our chamomile tea cools down—twenty minutes tops—and the very second *anybody* feels scared of the window or wants the closet walls, we stop the timer together. Deal?"

"Deal," Katie and I answered together, glancing gratefully at each other before locking our SwiftChair brakes flush against the polished oak transfer rail.

"Twenny minutes tea—all six!" Chelsea echoed earnestly in her thick, slurred voice, setting Shelly Turtle carefully on the patchwork quilt first.

Sliding smoothly onto the wide orthopedic mattress in the exact six-person line we had mapped out on my laptop—Wesley settling happily onto the left side in his navy flannel pajamas, Heather right beside Wesley where her shoulder touched his and her hand rested warmly against my right arm, me in the center with my left fingers laced through Katie's, Chelsea snuggled right beside Katie with Shelly Turtle, and Brittany on the far right with our warm ceramic mugs—felt almost unreal for the first eleven minutes. After thirteen years of feeling hard plywood subflooring through half an inch of compressed closet batting, my shoulder blades actually sank downward without hitting timber, while beside Heather, Wesley let out a contented sigh and tapped his blue silicone ring softly against his knee, loving having everyone together on one giant quilt.

Then, just as the brass timer clicked past minute twelve, a heavy late-evening delivery box truck rumbled down Arvin Road, let out a sharp, pneumatic *pssshh* of air brakes near the corner stop sign, and rattled our front windowpane at the exact moment an October wind gust scraped a dry oak branch across the vinyl siding behind our blackout curtains.

My body didn't ask my brain's permission.

In half a heartbeat, every muscle across my chest and stomach locked iron-tight—yanked thirteen years backward to the dark rear brick alley behind Greenway Court in August 2003, listening in frozen terror as Brenda banged down the iron stairwell hunting for our first-floor window above the humming box air conditioner. Beside my left shoulder, Katie's hand went ice-cold around her ceramic mug, her pulse jumping visibly at the base of her throat.

And right past Katie, Chelsea gasped out loud, clutching Shelly Turtle tight against her puffer pockets with wide, terrified eyes fixed on the curtain rod.

"T-truck!" Chelsea whimpered through her speech impediment, a thin, frightened tightness catching at the top of her breath. "Group Home Two-Face truck! H-him outside window! Closet bag now, Nate! Please, closet bag!"

Neither Heather nor Brittany sighed, told us it was only a parcel truck, or asked for *"just two more minutes."*

Before the echo of the air brakes had even faded down Indian River Road, Brittany reached calmly over and clicked the brass kitchen timer off at twelve minutes while Heather kissed my cheek warmly and gave Wesley's hand a reassuring squeeze.

"Twelve whole minutes on our very first six-person try!" Heather praised us gently, smiling at Katie, Chelsea, and me as if we had just climbed Mount Everest. "Yesterday we did zero minutes, and tonight all six of us did twelve! Wes and I will sleep right across the hall with our door cracked open—you four go cozy up in the fort."

Thirty seconds later, while Wesley stayed calm and secure walking right beside Heather into her room next door for the night, Katie and I transferred smoothly onto our SwiftChairs and down onto the familiar closet carpet with Chelsea and Brittany, pulling the bifold closet doors nearly closed until the bedroom window disappeared from sight.

"Twelve minutes brave," Chelsea whispered sleepily in the warm dark.

"Twelve minutes brave, Cous," Brittany whispered back, holding us close until morning.

## Thursday, November 24, 2016 & Sunday, January 22, 2017 — Forty-Five Minutes on Thanksgiving Night & All Six Falling Asleep in Winter Sunlight

Because Heather and Brittany never once treated retreating into the closet as a failure, our nervous systems slowly stopped dreading nine o'clock.

Through late October and November, twenty-minute chamomile-tea tries on the solid-oak Alaskan King became everyone's favorite quiet part of the evening—especially for Wesley, who eagerly lined up all six pillows across the nine-foot oak headboard every night at eight-forty-five because having Heather on one side and the rest of us right beside her made him beam from ear to ear. Some windy nights when acorns pattered hard on the roof shingles, Katie, Chelsea, and I only lasted fourteen or fifteen minutes before asking for the closet sleeping bag; other quiet nights, when the twenty-minute brass timer gave its soft single *ping*, nobody rushed to move right away.

On Thanksgiving night, November 24—after a joyful afternoon feast around our extended dining table with Dad down from Alexandria and Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven over from Earlton Court, where Chelsea had stayed happily dressed from morning till night in a soft, tagless cranberry-red fleece tunic fitted with matching velvet puffer pockets, proudly showing Uncle David and Wesley how Shelly Turtle had a matching cranberry ribbon—steady autumn rain pattered softly against the pine needles outside at nine-fifteen when all six of us climbed under the patchwork quilt with warm mugs of spiced pear-chamomile tea.

At nine-thirty-five, the little timer clicked softly on the nightstand.

Heather reached over to turn it off and glanced kindly across me at Katie and Chelsea. "Ready for the closet bag, guys, or a few more minutes?"

Katie rested her head against my left shoulder while Heather's warm hand stayed laced through my right, and glanced at Chelsea, who was lazily stroking Shelly Turtle's plush flippers without a trace of wheezing while Wesley lay peacefully relaxed against Heather's arm.

"Could we leave the lamp low and stay under the quilt ten more minutes?" Katie asked softly. "My shoulders feel really good tonight."

Ten extra minutes turned into twenty-five—stretching all the way to forty-five peaceful minutes together across the nine-foot orthopedic mattress—before a distant fire engine wailing half a mile south along Military Highway finally set Chelsea's shoulders tensing and kicked Katie's and my Greenway Court reflexes awake at ten o'clock. Even then, after Heather kissed my temple and walked next door with Wesley while the four of us transferred back into our closet sleeping bag for the rest of Thanksgiving night, Katie smiled against my collarbone in the dark because forty-five quiet minutes on an open bed was longer than we had managed since we were eighteen years old.

Then came the third Sunday afternoon of January 2017.

Home in Virginia Beach for the weekend during the forty-five-day odd-year winter session—where Heather, thirty-nearly-thirty Wesley, Brittany, and Chelsea had ridden up and down with us between Broad Street and Arvin Road—all six of us rolled back up our sunny front-porch ramp after a brisk Sunday walk and bagel run down Military Highway feeling pleasantly worn out at two-thirty in the afternoon.

"My feet are frozen and my eyes are heavy from session bills," Katie admitted with a yawn as golden winter sunshine slanted cheerfully around the edges of our open bedroom curtains. "Can all six of us just stretch out on top of the Alaskan King quilt in our sweaters for fifteen minutes to warm up before we start Monday's amendment packet?"

"Fifteen-minute family warm-up coming right up," Heather grinned, kicking off her sneakers right after Wesley climbed happily onto his left-hand spot and patted the quilt beside him for Heather, while Katie and I transferred into the center and Chelsea and Brittany settled cozily onto Katie's side.

Nobody set the brass timer because nobody intended to sleep. Sunlight pooled warm and gold across the wide navy-and-cream quilt; the house furnace hummed softly down the hall; Heather rested her cheek contentedly against my right shoulder with her left hand resting reassuringly against Wesley's forearm; Katie laced her fingers through my left hand; and Chelsea let out a soft, rhythmic breath beside Katie and Brittany.

When my eyes blinked open again, the golden rectangle of winter sunlight had crept all the way across the oak floorboards to the open closet door, and the digital clock read *4:12 PM*.

I froze—not in panic, but in pure, stunned disbelief.

To my right, Heather blinked awake smiling warmly up at me while Wesley slept deeply and peacefully beside her without a single anxious twitch. To my left, Katie lifted her head off the pillow at the exact moment Brittany blinked awake behind her silver wire-rimmed glasses beside a soundly sleeping Chelsea.

For five seconds, Katie, Heather, and I just looked into one another's eyes.

"Nate," Katie whispered, barely daring to breathe out loud lest she wake Wesley and Chelsea up, "all six of us fell *all the way asleep* on the bed. For an hour and forty minutes. And nobody woke up scared."

When darkness fell at ten o'clock that Sunday night, thirteen and a half years of nighttime habit still pulled Katie, Chelsea, Brittany, and me back inside our closet sleeping bag one more week. Yet as I lay on that hard closet carpet, I knew in my bones that the closet door didn't own the rest of our lives anymore.
