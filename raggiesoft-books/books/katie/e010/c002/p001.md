## Tuesday, November 5, 2019, 7:30 PM — Winning the Seventh Senate District & Flipping the Senate of Virginia

"Norfolk precincts around Newtown Road and Glenrock just uploaded to the State Board of Elections server," I called out at seven-thirty on Election Night—Tuesday, November 5, 2019—as my custom PHP precinct-aggregation script refreshed on my laptop screen inside our packed campaign headquarters off Kempsville Road. "And here come the early-vote and seven-o'clock precinct totals from CCHR, Bellamy Manor, Brandon, Pembroke, and the polling place over by Fairlawn Recreation Center on Marlborough Drive!"

Sitting right beside my left armrest in her compact SwiftChair, thirty-four-year-old Katie leaned in while Dad stood behind our two wheelchairs with his hand resting proudly on my shoulder.

Unlike our first House race six years earlier—when we had watched eighteen Kempsville precincts around our kitchen table—the Seventh Senate District spanned fifty-four precincts across western and central Virginia Beach and eastern Norfolk. For seven straight months from April through November, Katie, Dad, and I had ridden every CVTA bus route connecting the two cities—Route 1, Route 12, Route 15, Route 20, Route 25, Route 29, Route 33, and, starting in October 2019, the brand-new Route 24 running along Kempsville Road and Witchduck Road to Pembroke East—canvassing thousands of doors from Kempsville and Larkspur all the way north to Bayside and west across Newtown Road into Norfolk. In fact, the launch of that new Route 24 corridor along Kempsville Road and Witchduck Road in October 2019 (a completely separate north-south route from the historic, long-retired Laskin Road route that had merely shared a recycled route number years earlier) had marked a personal milestone for Katie and me: the exact month Route 24 began rolling down Kempsville Road, we had pooled our software consulting savings to buy our own single-story brick ranch on Lord Nelson Drive strictly fifty-fifty right off the new Route 24 bus line, installing a second custom two-person roll-up hydrotherapy tub and outfitting the master walk-in closet as our enclosed sleeping sanctuary while Dad kept the original brick ranch on Arvin Road off Indian River Road and Military Highway in the family.

And because voters across Virginia Beach and Norfolk remembered Delegate Katie Prairie as the lawmaker who had stood up to the bathroom-bill bullies on Meet the Nation in 2016 and fought every single winter for their buses, schools, and disability waivers, the corporate attack mailers against her hadn't stood a chance.

"Look at the margin in Kempsville and around CCHR, Katie," I said, my voice catching as the green rows populated down my screen at 7:54 PM. "You're carrying your home Eighty-Fifth House precincts by twenty-two points—Andre Askew just won your House seat right on your coattails—and you're winning the Bayside, Pembroke, and eastern Norfolk precincts by eleven points!"

Ten minutes later, at 8:04 PM, the statewide television monitors mounted above the headquarters room chimed in unison.

First came the Seventh Senate District projection:

*PROJECTED WINNER — 7TH SENATE DISTRICT: DEL. KATIE PRAIRIE (D-VIRGINIA BEACH) ELECTED TO THE SENATE OF VIRGINIA (55.8% – 44.2%).*

And eighteen minutes after that, a roar went up across the room that rattled the storefront windows when the statewide banner flashed across the bottom of the screen:

*BREAKING NEWS: DEMOCRATS FLIP CONTROL OF BOTH THE SENATE OF VIRGINIA AND THE VIRGINIA HOUSE OF DELEGATES.*

Katie turned her head toward me, her eyes bright with tears as she threw her arms around my neck across our wheelchair armrests while Dad wrapped both of us in a bear hug from behind.

"No more watching our bills die across the rotunda, Nate," Katie whispered against my shoulder. "Our caucus isn't in the minority anymore. Come January, I hold a committee gavel."

## December 2019 to Wednesday, January 8, 2020 — The Custom-Milled Mahogany Ramp & Swearing In as Madam Chair

![Inside the Senate of Virginia Chamber at the State Capitol in Richmond on Opening Day, January 8, 2020, 34-year-old State Senator Katie Prairie sits in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair beside the newly milled solid-mahogany wheelchair ramp, taking the secular constitutional affirmation of office on the Constitution of Virginia held by her twin brother Nate while their father David watches proudly.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/jan_2020_senate_of_virginia_mahogany_ramp_madam_chair_affirmation.jpg)

In the weeks between Election Night and the January 2020 Opening Day of the General Assembly, the transition from the House of Delegates to the Senate of Virginia looked very different from our first arrival in Richmond six years earlier.

After the Electoral Boards of the City of Virginia Beach and the City of Norfolk completed their official multi-locality canvass and the Virginia State Board of Elections transmitted Katie's gold-sealed Certificate of Election as Senator-Elect for the Seventh Senatorial District under Code of Virginia Section 24.2-675, Katie received a phone call at our home in Kempsville on the first Monday of December.

It wasn't from a mid-level facilities bureaucrat telling her to wait until March. It was from William, the veteran Clerk of the Senate of Virginia, calling personally alongside the newly elected Senate Majority Leader.

"Senator-Elect Prairie," the Clerk said warmly on speakerphone across our desk, "in the two-hundred-and-forty-year history of the Senate of Virginia, you are the very first State Senator ever elected to sit in our chamber in a wheelchair—and the Majority Caucus has just designated you to serve as Chair of the Senate Committee on Rehabilitation and Social Services, as well as a senior member of Senate Transportation and Senate Finance. I want you and Nathaniel to ride up to the Capitol this week so our master architectural woodworkers can measure your SwiftChair in person. When you roll into the Senate of Virginia on January eighth, everything in our chamber is going to look like Thomas Jefferson built it for you himself."

Three days later, when Katie and I rode the morning ARTS train and the JRTC Route 6 bus up to Capitol Square and rolled into the second-floor Senate wing, the Clerk of the Senate was waiting for us with the Capitol's master cabinetmakers and historic preservation carpenters.

Inside the neoclassical Office of the Clerk of the Senate—where tall white marble Ionic columns framed the floor-to-ceiling volumes of the Virginia Acts of Assembly—the carpenters were already constructing a lowered, wheelchair-accessible section of the polished mahogany reception counter so Senator Katie Prairie could sign official Senate parchments and pre-file bills at eye level with me parked right beside her.

And inside the Senate of Virginia Chamber itself, under the tall arched windows and the crimson carpet of the forty-member upper house, the Clerk led us up to the front aisle where the senior committee chairs sat.

Not only had the cabinetmakers modified the front-aisle mahogany desk for the Chair of Rehabilitation and Social Services so Katie's compact SwiftChair rolled smoothly underneath the desktop without a single obstruction, they had also designed a permanent, custom-milled solid-mahogany wheelchair ramp leading directly up to the Clerk's well and the Senate dais—complete with matching turned mahogany balusters and brass handrails stained to the exact eighteenth-century patina of the chamber's historic woodwork.

"Look at the grain on that mahogany, Katie," I murmured as Katie tested the gentle one-in-twelve slope of the new Senate ramp in her SwiftChair and rolled effortlessly up to the Clerk's well. "Anyone looking down from the Senate gallery is going to assume that ramp has been part of this Capitol for two hundred years."

"And it's staying here for the next two hundred years," the Clerk of the Senate smiled.

On Opening Day—Wednesday, January 8, 2020, at twelve o'clock noon—after we had unpacked our familiar winter-session sanctuary at Apartment 104 of the Broad & Boulevard Lofts on West Broad Street the weekend before and ridden the eastbound JRTC Route 6 bus down to Ninth and Broad, thirty-four-year-old Katie rolled down the center aisle of the Senate of Virginia in her compact SwiftChair.

Because the second-floor Senate Chamber had a level ground-accessible entrance off the Capitol elevator lobby and allowed a Senator's immediate family onto the floor specifically for the Opening Day swearing-in ceremony, Dad sat beaming in his best suit in the front row of the Senatorial family section while I rolled my SwiftChair right up beside Katie's desk holding our bound leather copy of the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia.

Up on the high marble rostrum, the Lieutenant Governor of Virginia—serving under the state constitution as President of the Senate—called the forty Senators-Elect forward to take the constitutional oath of office under Article II, Section 7 of the Constitution of Virginia.

Rolling smoothly up the newly installed custom-milled mahogany ramp to the front well with me right beside her left armrest as her ceremonial guest, Katie didn't reach for a Bible while the opposition senators across the carpet held leather Bibles angled toward the press gallery cameras. Neither Katie nor I believed in any religion or any god, and just as she had done on the Virginia Beach City Council and in the House of Delegates, Katie placed her left palm squarely on the gold-stamped cover of the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia resting across my hands, raised her right hand toward the rostrum, and spoke the secular constitutional affirmation under Article II, Section 7 of the Constitution of Virginia and Section 49-9 of the Code of Virginia—omitting the optional parenthetical religious phrase at the end:

"I do solemnly affirm that I will support the Constitution of the United States, and the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia, and that I will faithfully and impartially discharge all the duties incumbent upon me as a member of the Senate of Virginia, according to the best of my ability."

Ten minutes later, after I rolled back out to the accessible viewing bay to watch the formal floor proceedings alongside Dad, the Clerk read the standing committee assignments for the 2020 session and handed Senator Katie Prairie the polished wooden gavel as Madam Chair of the Senate Committee on Rehabilitation and Social Services—sitting right across the chamber from the exact seats where Senator Franklin Ward and his allies had killed her transit and disability bills twenty-two months earlier.

And just as we knew they would, the Republican caucus, right-wing cable networks, and evangelical media had an absolute field day over both what was resting under Katie's left hand and the four words she had deliberately left out of her oath.

Before the Senate had even gaveled out of its Opening Day ceremonial session at two-thirty that afternoon, an opposition party operative up in the press gallery had blasted out a zoomed-in video clip of Katie's left hand resting on the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia instead of a Bible while she said "I do solemnly affirm" and stopped at "according to the best of my ability" without saying "so help me God." Within forty-five minutes, the opposition party's state committee had sent out a breathless statewide fundraising email accusing the newly minted committee chair from Virginia Beach of "refusing to touch a Holy Bible or say 'So help me God' on the floor of Thomas Jefferson's Capitol." Conservative talk-radio switchboards from Richmond to Tidewater were melting down, right-wing cable pundits on Beacon News and LibertyMax were running red "WAR ON FAITH IN VIRGINIA" banners across the bottom of the screen, and back home in Virginia Beach—just five minutes down Indian River Road from our house in Kempsville—the televangelist hosts of *The 777 Circle* on the Covenant Broadcasting Network were interrupting their afternoon broadcast to warn viewers that their own hometown State Senator had "defied the sacred oath precedent set by George Washington in 1789."

When Katie, Dad, and I rolled out of the mahogany doors of the Senate Chamber into the Capitol Rotunda at two-forty-five, camera crews from Beacon News, LibertyMax, and the Covenant Broadcasting Network were waiting in ambush beside Thomas Jefferson's marble statue.

"Senator Prairie! Senator Prairie!" the Beacon News correspondent called out, thrusting a microphone toward Katie's lap tray alongside a reporter holding a Covenant Broadcasting Network mic flag while two opposition state senators lingered behind the marble columns to watch the ambush. "The Republican state chairman and the hosts of *The 777 Circle* back in your own district in Virginia Beach just condemned you for refusing to place your hand on a Holy Bible and refusing to say 'So help me God' during your Senate swearing-in today! Why did you break the precedent that President George Washington set at the founding of our country?"

Katie braked her SwiftChair on the black-and-white marble tiles beneath Thomas Jefferson's statue, looked straight into the cluster of television lenses without a flicker of hesitation, and picked up the leather-bound volume off my lap tray so the gold seal of the Commonwealth gleamed under the camera lights.

"First of all, I would gladly encourage the hosts of *The 777 Circle* down on Indian River Road—and my Republican colleagues hiding behind those marble columns—to actually read the Constitution of the United States, the Constitution of Virginia, and a basic history book," Katie answered, her voice calm, crisp, and loud enough to echo across the entire Capitol Rotunda. "Let's start with the law. Article VI, Clause 3 of the United States Constitution states in black and white that all state legislators shall be bound by Oath or Affirmation to support the Constitution, and that no religious test shall ever be required as a qualification to any office or public trust under the United States. And right here in Article II, Section 7 of the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia—the exact document I placed my left hand on today—the framers put the words 'or affirm' and 'so help me God' inside parentheses, paired with Section 49-9 of the Code of Virginia, specifically so citizens who do not subscribe to a religion can make a solemn, binding secular affirmation without being subjected to an unconstitutional religious test."

The Covenant Broadcasting Network reporter opened his mouth to interrupt—"But George Washington himself added 'So help me God' in 1789—"

"No, he didn't—and historians debunked that myth decades ago," Katie cut in smoothly, her literal, encyclopedic memory firing on all cylinders as the cameras rolled live. "Even the Presidential Oath written by the Founders in Article II, Section 1, Clause 8 of the United States Constitution does not contain the words 'So help me God.' Not a single eyewitness, diary, newspaper, or foreign diplomat standing at Federal Hall in New York on April 30, 1789 recorded George Washington adding 'So help me God' to his oath. That story was invented out of thin air sixty-five years later in an 1854 book written by Rufus Wilmot Griswold, who wasn't even born when Washington was alive, and the first President confirmed by contemporary historical records to have said those words was Chester A. Arthur in 1881. And look at whose marble statue we are gathered under right now: Thomas Jefferson, who wrote the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom right here in Richmond declaring that our civil rights have no dependence on our religious opinions."

Behind the marble columns, the two opposition senators suddenly found something very interesting to look at on their shoes as Katie lowered the Constitution back onto my lap tray and added one final, unbothered smile for the cameras:

"In the Seventh Senate District across Virginia Beach and Norfolk, I represent Christians, Jews, Muslims, Hindus, Buddhists, and non-religious citizens like my brother and me alike. I wasn't elected to be anybody's pastor, and Virginia is a constitutional republic, not a theocracy. My legal obligation as a State Senator is to the Constitution of the United States and the Constitution of Virginia—and if the pundits screaming on Beacon News, LibertyMax, and *The 777 Circle* spent half as much time reading the Constitution as they spend inventing phony history lessons about George Washington, maybe they'd know what Article VI actually says. Now if you'll excuse my brother, my dad, and me, we have a Route 6 bus to catch on Broad Street and a committee docket to gavel in tomorrow morning."

When we rolled into Katie's new Senate of Virginia office at eight-thirty the next morning and the main constituent telephone line rang on her desk before her legislative assistant had even finished hanging up her winter coat in the outer room, Katie didn't wait for staff to screen the call. Just as she had done as a Virginia Beach City Councilwoman and as a Virginia State Delegate—because in her literal autistic mind it was her office and it was only polite to answer the phone when she could so constituents knew she was actually at her desk working for them—she reached over from her SwiftChair, pressed the speakerphone button, and said warmly, "Senator Katie Prairie's office, this is Katie speaking," greeting a stunned retired Navy chaplain calling from Norfolk to thank her for defending Thomas Jefferson's Statute for Religious Freedom.

Down on the Senate floor, just like the House of Delegates across the Rotunda, the Senate of Virginia had its own official Senate Page Program for thirteen- and fourteen-year-old eighth-graders appointed from across the Commonwealth—and within forty-eight hours, Katie and I had become the favorite Senator and brother of the eighth-grade Senate Pages for the exact same reason the House Pages had gravitated to our desks four years earlier. Every floor errand from Senator Prairie's desk came on a neat, color-coded sticky note with the exact room number, committee name, and bill number written in clear block print, and nobody in Katie's orbit ever barked a vague instruction or raised their voice when the brass division bells rang.

As for our three favorite eighth-grade House Pages from February 2016—the autistic triplets Sam Hawkins and his identical-twin sisters Mandy and Sarah Hawkins from rural Southside Virginia near Danville, who had told us over sub sandwiches at the Oak Lawn Diner on West Broad Street after riding JRTC Route 6 that Katie was their absolute favorite constitutional officer in Richmond—they had aged out of the middle-school Page program years earlier, graduated together at the top of their high-school class under the loving roof of their Aunt Marge and their Uncle Richard Callaway (who had since retired as Republican House Majority Leader), and enrolled together that fall at age eighteen as freshmen at Central Virginia University right on the West Broad Street Route 6 bus corridor. True to her word on that winter afternoon four years earlier, the moment Sam, Mandy, and Sarah aged out of high school and arrived in Richmond for college, Katie hired all three triplets to work side by side in her Senate of Virginia office—first as paid legislative session and transit-policy interns throughout their four years at Central Virginia University, and then, the morning after all three graduated with honors in May 2024, as her full-time State Senate Legislative and Transit Policy Assistants.
