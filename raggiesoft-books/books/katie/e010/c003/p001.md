## Saturday, January 10, 2026, 7:30 PM — The Living Room Decision on Lord Nelson Drive & The 10,000-Signature Rule

![Inside their warm living room on Lord Nelson Drive in Kempsville, Virginia Beach on January 10, 2026, 40-year-old State Senator Katie Prairie and her fraternal twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands across their armrests while their 67-year-old father David sits on the sofa beside them with his yellow legal pad and three tall glasses of ginger ale.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2026_kempsville_living_room_senate_campaign_decision_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Listen to what Senator Martin Warren's chief of staff just told the political desk at The Potomac Post," I said, reading aloud from my MagnaBook Pro screen inside the warm living room of our brick ranch house on Lord Nelson Drive in Kempsville while the January rain tapped against the front bay window. "Gregory said, and I quote: 'State Senator Katie Prairie and her brother are a cute regional novelty down in Tidewater, but two software developers in wheelchairs riding city buses cannot possibly raise the twenty million dollars required to compete against Preston Holloway in a statewide U.S. Senate primary.'"

Sitting on the sofa right beside my right armrest with his familiar yellow legal pad resting on his knee and a mug of black coffee in his hand, our sixty-seven-year-old dad let out a low, dry chuckle and looked across at my forty-year-old twin sister, while across the coffee table in the matching armchairs forty-year-old Brittany and forty-three-year-old Chelsea looked up from sorting Prairie Twin Interactive client folders over tall glasses of ginger ale.

"A cute regional novelty," Dad repeated, shaking his head behind his glasses. "That's the fourth election in sixteen years where the party bosses in Richmond or Washington have called the two of you a long shot before a single voter has cast a ballot."

Parked flush against my left armrest in her compact black-and-silver SwiftChair power wheelchair, Katie didn't look intimidated in the slightest. Her blue eyes were fixed on the muted television screen across the living room, where three-term Democratic U.S. Senator Martin Warren stood at a crystal-chandeliered ballroom podium in McLean raising the arm of his handpicked successor—wealthy Northern Virginia venture capitalist businessman Preston Holloway, whose newly formed super PAC had already banked fourteen million dollars from K Street defense contractors, private-equity hospital chains, and prescription-drug middlemen.

And right below the split-screen video of the McLean donor reception, Global News Network was replaying a clip from the White House South Lawn two hours earlier, where President Garrett Thorne had stopped in front of the Marine One chopper blades to sneer into the press pool microphones about Virginia's newly opened Senate seat.

"Did you hear what Garrett Thorne called you on the South Lawn this afternoon, Katie?" Dad asked quietly, his jaw tightening the way it always did whenever a bully came after his daughter. "He called you 'Broken Katie Prairie rolling around Richmond' and said if Democrats were crazy enough to nominate a spinster on wheels instead of a corporate suit, his Restore the Republic nominee Richard Sterling would bury you in November. And then Vice President Conrad Drayton went straight onto the Liberty First Network at six o'clock to say unmarried, childless, non-religious twins who live together have no stake in the future of the American family."

"No stake in a family?" Brittany scoffed from her armchair, reaching over to squeeze Chelsea's hand as Chelsea frowned behind her glasses and shook her head firmly. "Conrad Drayton wouldn't recognize a real family that actually takes care of each other if we rolled past him on a city bus."

Katie reached her right hand across our wheelchair armrests, laced her fingers tightly through mine, and looked warmly around the living room at Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and me.

Six winters had passed since Katie had rolled up the newly milled solid-mahogany ramp in the Senate of Virginia Chamber in January 2020 to take the gavel as Chair of Rehabilitation and Social Services. Across those six years—through the August 2020 pandemic special session inside the Central Virginia University Student Commons Ballroom on West Broad Street, where Katie had legislated behind a three-sided clear polycarbonate shield wearing a NIOSH-approved trifold N95 respirator fitted with an airtight medical drinking valve while I logged classroom and bus ventilation rates on our pocket NDIR carbon-dioxide monitor and wiped our SwiftChair joysticks down with non-alcoholic benzalkonium chloride and hypochlorous acid wipes—she had never missed a single roll-call vote or a single constituent phone call. She had won re-election overwhelmingly in the renumbered Twenty-Second Senate District in November 2023, and just yesterday afternoon at the Capitol in Richmond, she had co-patroned and cast her second-passage YES votes to place three historic Constitutional Amendments directly onto the November 3, 2026 statewide ballot: enshrining the fundamental Right to Reproductive Freedom, locking Marriage Equality into the Constitution of Virginia while repealing the defunct 2006 ban, and establishing automatic Restoration of Voting Rights upon completion of a prison term.

Yet every single morning when we opened the federal register, we watched the Restore the Republic administration in Washington—and their billionaire Apex Efficiency Commission—try to rip away the exact civil rights, Medicaid home-and-community-based disability waivers, rural hospitals, and Federal Transit Administration operating grants that working Virginians depended on to survive. And instead of fighting back with teeth, Senator Martin Warren's handpicked corporate heir Preston Holloway was going on cable television preaching polite, poll-tested incrementalism so he wouldn't offend his K Street donors.

"Martin Warren doesn't own that Senate seat, Dad," Katie said, her voice calm, steady, and ringing with the exact same crystalline resolve she had carried onto the four buses in 2008. "That seat belongs to the eight point seven million people of the Commonwealth of Virginia. Preston Holloway thinks he can buy the June primary from a ballroom in McLean without ever setting foot on a public bus, and Garrett Thorne and Conrad Drayton think calling me 'Broken Katie Prairie' is going to make Nate and me hide in our house. I want to beat Preston Holloway in the June sixteenth primary, I want to beat Richard Sterling on November third, and on January third, 2027, I want to roll down the aisle of the United States Senate and watch Vice President Conrad Drayton—in his constitutional capacity as President of the Senate—be forced to swear me in on the Constitution of the United States without a Bible and without saying 'So help me God.'"

I grinned across our armrests, feeling the familiar electric surge run straight through my ribs. "He is going to turn purple at the rostrum, Katie."

"Let him," Katie smiled, squeezing my hand before turning to Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea. "Are all four of you with me?"

"Try stopping us, cuz," Brittany grinned, while Chelsea clapped both hands softly and nodded: "Ride all the buses, Katie!"

Dad uncapped his black rollerball pen, tapped it against the top of his yellow legal pad, and wrote *Katie Prairie for United States Senate — 2026* in neat, square block letters across the top margin.

"Every single mile, sweetheart," Dad said warmly. "Alright, Mr. Campaign Manager and Chief Technology Officer—walk your Treasurer through the statutory jump from a forty-district State Senate race to a statewide United States Senate campaign."

I tapped a shortcut on my MagnaBook Pro, pulling up the federal and state election code checklists I had spent the past forty-eight hours compiling in Quantum Code.

"First," I said, "because the United States Senate is a federal office, our campaign finance reporting moves from Virginia's state COMET system to the Federal Election Commission. Tonight we file FEC Form 2—Katie's Statement of Candidacy—and FEC Form 1 registering *Katie Prairie for U.S. Senate* as her principal campaign committee, with you as official Campaign Treasurer and me as Campaign Manager and Chief Technology Officer, plus her mandatory Senate Public Financial Disclosure Report with the Secretary of the United States Senate. And because our campaign donations will process through CivicPulse and Meridian Pay behind Aegis Cloud on the PHP infrastructure we write and maintain ourselves, zero dollars are going to beltway fundraising consultants."

"And what about getting Katie's name onto the June sixteenth Democratic Primary ballot under Virginia law?" Dad asked, poised with his pen.

"That's the biggest hurdle—and the exact reason the Washington insiders think we can't do it without corporate money," I replied, rotating my laptop slightly toward Dad's side of the sofa so both he and Katie could read the statutory table. "When Katie ran for the House of Delegates, Code of Virginia Section 24.2-506 required one hundred and twenty-five voter signatures. When she ran for the Senate of Virginia, it required two hundred and fifty. To qualify for the statewide United States Senate primary ballot in Virginia, Section 24.2-506 requires at least ten thousand qualified voter signatures statewide—and there's a mandatory geographic distribution rule: those ten thousand signatures must include at least four hundred qualified voters from each of Virginia's eleven Congressional Districts."

Dad whistled softly, jotting the numbers onto his legal pad. "Ten thousand verified signatures statewide, with a hard floor of four hundred in all eleven Congressional Districts—from Accomack County on the Eastern Shore and Virginia Beach here in the Second District all the way out to the Ninth District in Blacksburg, Bluewater, Abingdon, and Cumberland Gap."

"Which means if our campaign standard is always collecting more than double the statutory minimum so no party lawyer can challenge us off the ballot," Katie said, her blue eyes lighting up with mathematical focus, "we aren't stopping at ten thousand signatures and four hundred per district. We're collecting at least twenty-four thousand signatures statewide, and at least twelve hundred verified signatures in every single one of the eleven Congressional Districts."

Just then, Katie's smartphone buzzed on the coffee table beside our three tall glasses of ice-cold ginger ale. It was a Washington campaign consultant who had been leaving voicemails all afternoon offering to sign on as a paid strategist. When Katie tapped the speakerphone button and told him she was entering the race, the consultant immediately launched into a slick sales pitch offering to lease our campaign a forty-five-foot custom diesel motorcoach wrapped with a giant photograph of Katie's face for forty-two thousand dollars a month.

Katie looked at me, looked at Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea, and leaned toward the speakerphone.

"Thank you for calling, but our campaign will not be leasing a private tour bus," Katie told him crisply. "For the past sixteen years, my brother Nate, my dad, my cousins Brittany and Chelsea, and I have ridden regular public transit buses and passenger trains everywhere we go, and we pay standard regular fare at the farebox alongside our constituents. If a rural county or a working-class corridor in Virginia has a public bus route, we are riding that bus with the people who live there. And if a rural county doesn't have fixed-route bus service between towns, or if a wheelchair ramp breaks down in the rain, my dad is going to rent a wheelchair-accessible minivan to bridge the gap so we experience that exact transit desert the same way disabled and working-class Virginians do every day. Have a good evening."

She tapped the red disconnect button, and Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea raised their glasses of ginger ale in a five-way living-room toast.

## Saturday, January 10, 2026, 8:45 PM — Written Permission From The Paper Wall & The Brooks Family for "The Accessibility Project"

Forty-five minutes after we filed Katie's FEC Form 2 electronically, a chime sounded on my laptop as an incoming OmniMeet video call connected from the New River Valley.

When I clicked accept and angled my screen so Katie, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and I could all see it clearly from our seats, two joyful living rooms filled the split window at once.

On the left side of the screen, gathered inside the sunlit control room of Engine Room Records in Blacksburg alongside Holly O'Connell—the youngest O'Connell sister and longtime Day-to-Day Band Manager and General Counsel—sat the members of The Paper Wall: Julian Croft ("Leo"), Maya Sterling ("Olivia"), sisters Izzy ("Luna"), Bea ("Stella"), and Charlotte ("Charlie") Croft, and cousins Diana Hale, Ellie Cross, and Fiona Croft.

And on the right side of the screen, gathered inside the wide, oak-bumpered communal living room of the Brooks Family Compound along U.S. Route 11 between Christiansburg and Radford, sat the found family whom The Paper Wall's music had chronicled—centered around the original trio from Bennett Hall Room 214: thirty-year-old Jordan Shawn Kelly (clean-shaven, wearing his rectangular prescription glasses and behind-the-ear hearing aids, seated in his custom manual wheelchair with his Pinnacle Vanguard Pro laptop open to his dark-screened NeoEdit terminal on Aura Glade) flanked right beside his armrests by his wife Aubrie Jean Kelly and Madison Elizabeth Scott, along with their Room 216 and Constellation family: Zoë Wren Brooks, Lauren Nicole Barnes, Hannah Parker Anderson, Mark Shawn Anderson, Isabel Claire Dawson, Jessamine "Jess" Mercer, Daniel, Laura, and Megan Brooks, and Australian expats Sarah Kristyn Braithwaite and Jessica Gallagher.

"Senator Prairie! Nate! Mr. Prairie!" Aubrie Jean called out warmly, her long brown side-braid resting over her shoulder as she held up a freshly signed, notarized legal document beside Jordan's wheelchair while Holly O'Connell held up the matching Engine Room Records master licensing execution sheet in Blacksburg. "We just saw your FEC filing go live on the wire five minutes ago, and we wanted to be the very first people in the Commonwealth of Virginia to hand you this!"

On the right half of our screen, Zoë shared a high-resolution PDF onto our workspace: a permanent, royalty-free campaign synchronization and public-performance license for Track 13 of *The Eleventh Child, Book 2: System.Restore*—"The Accessibility Project"—signed in ink by every member of The Paper Wall, by Holly O'Connell on behalf of Engine Room Records, and by Jordan, Aubrie Jean, Madison, Zoë, Lauren, Hannah, Mark, Sarah, Jessica Gallagher, Isabel, Jessamine, and Daniel, Laura, and Megan Brooks.

Katie's hand flew to her collar, her blue eyes shining as she looked at the twenty-plus signatures spanning both pages.

"You all signed this for our campaign?" Katie asked softly.

"Every single one of us," Julian Croft smiled from Blacksburg, his arm around Maya's shoulders while Izzy, Bea, and Charlie cheered behind them. "When we wrote and recorded 'The Accessibility Project,' we wanted it to sound like a triumphant mission briefing—turning accessibility mapping and disability solidarity into an unstoppable anthem. There isn't a candidate in America that song belongs to more than you and Nate."

Beside Aubrie Jean and Madison on the right side of the screen, Jordan's fingers moved swiftly across the tactile keys of his Pinnacle Vanguard Pro laptop, and a moment later he triggered EchoType—filling our Kempsville living room with the warm, clear Australian female cadence of his signature "Sydney" voice profile:

<aac>Listen to the first verse of that song, Senator Prairie. Back in the spring of 2015, when the six of us from Suite 214 and 216 were undergraduate freshmen at Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute mapping the Hillside Pass-Through elevators at Eleanor Bennett Hall for our class project, the lyric in our song was: "We cannot pour the concrete for a ramp, we cannot buy an elevator for the camp—the budget's zero, but the mission's clear." Back then, our budget really was zero. All we could do was draw the map and share the key.</aac>

Jordan smiled warmly behind his glasses as Aubrie Jean and Madison rested their hands on his shoulders and Daniel and Laura Brooks nodded proudly behind him. He typed another line and pressed the speak key:

<aac>Every single eligible Virginian voter in our family—Aubrie Jean, Madison, Zoë, Lauren, Hannah, Mark, Isabel, Jess, Daniel, Laura, Megan, and me—is voting for you in the June sixteenth Democratic Primary and again on November third. And even though Sarah and Jessica are Australian nationals who can't cast a ballot yet, all of us are going to help you gather signatures and campaign across the New River Valley, Bluewater, and Southwest Virginia. Because when you roll onto the floor of the United States Senate and take your seat on the Senate Appropriations Committee, the budget won't be zero anymore. You're going to Washington to pour the concrete for the ramps and fund the elevators for the whole country.</aac>

Across our armrests, Katie squeezed my fingers so tightly I could feel her pulse beating against mine.

"When Nate, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and I ride the bus up to Montgomery County and Carroll County this spring," Katie promised them, her voice clear and steady as Brittany and Chelsea waved happily into the webcam behind our shoulders, "'The Accessibility Project' is going to be playing from every speaker we bring—and we'll meet the Room 214 crew right at the Route 3 bus shelter at the end of your driveway on Lee Highway."
