## Saturday, March 28, 2026, 10:15 AM — The Jammed Wheelchair Ramp in the Rain at 3400 Victoria Boulevard in Hampton

![In the pouring March 2026 rain at 3400 Victoria Boulevard in Hampton, Virginia, 40-year-old State Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs on the wet sidewalk beside a coastal transit bus whose front flip-out wheelchair ramp has jammed halfway out, talking warmly with the bus operator and Peninsula shipyard and hospital commuters while their father David holds an umbrella.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/mar_2026_hampton_3400_victoria_blvd_jammed_ramp_rain_katie_nate_david.jpg)

*<pa>Caution: ramp is deploying!</pa>* the exterior speaker of the twelve-year-old Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Route 103 bus crackled in the pouring March rain at 3400 Victoria Boulevard in Hampton—and then a harsh metallic grind echoed off the wet asphalt as the front flip-out aluminum wheelchair ramp seized halfway out of the threshold, sticking straight up at a forty-five-degree angle twelve inches above the flooded concrete curb.

Inside the open front doorway of the bus, the uniformed operator hit the hydraulic toggle switch twice more, winced as the worn actuator motor whined without budging an inch, and stepped out into the cold rain in his high-visibility vest.

"Oh, Senator Prairie, I am so sorry," the operator said, kneeling on the wet sidewalk beside the jammed hinge plate as water dripped from the bill of his cap. "This coach has three hundred and eighty thousand miles on the chassis, and the shop has been waiting six weeks on replacement hydraulic actuators because the spare-parts budget is tapped out. Let me call dispatch for a road supervisor and a relief bus—it might take thirty-five or forty minutes in this weather."

Parked side by side in our compact SwiftChairs in our rain slickers while Dad stood behind us holding a wide clear umbrella over our laptop bag and petition clipboards, forty-year-old Katie didn't reach for a phone to summon a campaign car, and she didn't get angry at the driver.

Instead, she looked at the seven working commuters waiting under the narrow aluminum shelter overhang at 3400 Victoria Boulevard—two pipefitters from Peninsula Shipbuilding & Drydock in Newport News still wearing their steel-toed boots after an overnight hull shift, three certified nursing assistants in damp scrubs heading to Hampton, and a retired Navy machinist with a cane—each of whom had already signed our Third Congressional District primary petitions five minutes earlier.

"You don't owe us a single apology—you can't drive a bus safely when Washington starves your maintenance shop of spare parts," Katie told the operator warmly, rolling her SwiftChair two feet closer so he could step back under the dry overhang of the shelter. "Nate, Dad, and I aren't going anywhere. If the riders on Victoria Boulevard have to wait forty minutes in the rain for a road supervisor with a pry bar, then the candidate asking to represent them in the United States Senate is waiting right here in the rain with them."

I lifted my mPhone on its armrest mount and tapped the live broadcast button on StreamCast and The Grid.

For the next thirty-eight minutes, while the March downpour drummed against the shelter roof on Victoria Boulevard and Preston Holloway was up in Arlington hosting a two-thousand-dollar-a-plate brunch inside a Sovereign Regency hotel ballroom, forty-two thousand Virginians watched live on their phones as Katie sat in her wheelchair in the Hampton rain listening to shipyard workers and hospital aides explain what happens to an hourly worker's paycheck when a twelve-year-old transit bus breaks down on the way to a shift. When the road supervisor's pickup truck finally splashed up to the curb with a heavy steel pry bar and freed the corroded hinge pin so Katie and I could roll up into the front four-point securement bays, the entire bus broke into applause—and by the time we rolled down the ramp at the Newport News Transit Center, volunteers waiting at the bays had filled nineteen more petition sheets in a single hour.

That same week, Katie, Dad, and I rode every corridor across Eastern and Central Virginia to show voters what a truly connected Commonwealth looked like. In the First Congressional District, we rode the morning ARTS passenger train into the Williamsburg Transportation Center—the exact station where Katie had begun her five-bus escape from Brandon Mercer in November 2005—and rolled thirty yards across the brick plaza from the train platform to pay regular fare on the local CTTA Route 1 bus straight to the front gate of Brewster Gardens, proving to hospitality workers and car-free families that Virginia didn't need to waste millions on duplicative consultant studies when an existing train-to-bus connection was already running and simply needed evening frequency and public promotion.

Two days later, in the Second Congressional District, we experienced firsthand how Virginia's Eastern Shore was stranded on a public-transit island at both ends of U.S. Route 13: at the south end, CVTA Routes 1, 27, and 35 met at the Shore Drive and Pleasure House Road transfer point right at the southern foot of the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel, yet neither CVTA nor ShoreLine Transit ran a single public bus across the seventeen-point-six-mile span to Cape Charles; and at the north end in Accomack County, ShoreLine Transit stopped at Oak Hall and Chincoteague Junction just eleven miles short of the Maryland state line—stopping dead before Pocomoke City, Maryland, where Maryland Shore Transit connected north to Salisbury and Delaware's First State Transit network. So after riding CVTA Route 1 to the Shore Drive and Pleasure House Road transfer point at the foot of the bridge-tunnel, Dad met us with a rented silver side-entry wheelchair-accessible ramp minivan, secured our two SwiftChairs into the four-point floor tie-downs, and drove us across the Bridge-Tunnel to Cape Charles, where we boarded a ShoreLine Transit cutaway bus with a rear hydraulic lift and rode the entire length of Northampton and Accomack Counties up U.S. Route 13 through Eastville, Exmore, Onley, Accomac, and Oak Hall. Outside the processing plant at Eastern Shore Poultry at shift change, women in hairnets and rubber boots crowded around Katie's SwiftChair to sign her petitions as she pledged to fight on the Senate Appropriations Committee for dedicated federal interstate transit funds to close both gaps at once—running a permanent public bus connection from Cape Charles across the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel (where public transit buses were already classified under the Bridge-Tunnel District's Class 8 schedule at a nominal one-dollar toll per crossing) directly into the Pleasure House Road CVTA transfer point on Shore Drive with a mandatory statutory cross-honoring agreement so CVTA and ShoreLine Transit fares and passes would be honored seamlessly on both systems, and extending ShoreLine Transit north across the state line into Pocomoke City and Salisbury with a direct Delmarva corridor link to First State Transit in Delaware.

And when we rode the morning ARTS train from Norfolk up through Petersburg in the Fourth District and Richmond in the Fourth and First Districts—riding JRTC Route 6 down Broad Street with its familiar automated voice announcing *"Route 6. Downtown. Welcome aboard JRTC!"* and calling out every single cross street along the corridor—we continued north to inspect the rail diamond at Doswell Junction in the Seventh District, where the East-West mountain branch tracks crossed the North-South main line less than half a mile from the front gate of Old Dominion Adventure Park.

"Look how close those tracks run to the park entrance, Nate," Katie said, pointing from the window of our ARTS Café Car as we sipped our complimentary ginger ales. "Thousands of teenagers and working families ride up here every summer for seasonal jobs, and there isn't a single platform or bus shuttle off Interstate 95. When we build the East-West Commonwealth Rail Corridor linking Norfolk and Richmond through Doswell Junction to Charlottesville, Fishersville, Roanoke, Christiansburg, and Bristol, we're putting a high-level ADA station right here at Doswell with a dedicated ramp shuttle straight to the gate."

## Tuesday, April 14, 2026, 1:30 PM — Wearing the National Firearms Association's "F" Grade as a Badge of Honor in Fairfax

When we rolled off the Capital Transit Orange Line elevator in Northern Virginia two weeks later and boarded a local Fairfax Link bus across the Tenth, Eighth, and Eleventh Congressional Districts, our grassroots petition count had already surged past twenty-two thousand verified signatures—and the gun lobby decided it was time to try to scare Katie off the ballot.

At one o'clock that Tuesday afternoon inside their glass corporate headquarters in Fairfax, the National Firearms Association convened a press conference alongside Republican Senate nominee Richard Sterling to announce that they had just issued State Senator Katie Prairie a lifetime "F" rating—pointing to a viral town-hall clip from Alexandria where Katie had called the civilian MR-15 semiautomatic rifle a "weapon of war" and demanded universal background checks, federal extreme-risk red-flag laws, safe storage rules, and a ban on high-capacity magazines and assault weapons.

"Katie Prairie has no business representing Virginia in the United States Senate!" the NFA's chief lobbyist barked into a bank of television microphones while holding up a red cardstock mailer stamped with a giant black letter F over a photograph of Katie in her wheelchair. "She got a lifetime F grade from the National Firearms Association, she calls America's most popular rifle a weapon of war, she wants a government takeover of healthcare, she wants taxpayer-funded abortion on demand, and she led the charge in Richmond to put biological men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms!"

What the NFA lobbyist didn't realize was that Katie, Dad, and I were riding the eastbound Fairfax Link bus right down the boulevard outside their building at that exact moment.

Hearing the live broadcast on my mPhone earpiece, Katie glanced up at the automated destination sign above the windshield, reached up from her SwiftChair, and pulled the yellow stop-request cord for the concrete bus shelter directly across the street from the NFA headquarters lawn.

When the bus knelt and deployed its front ramp at 1:28 PM, six television camera crews who were packing up their tripods on the sidewalk did a collective double-take as forty-one-year-old Katie Prairie and I rolled down the bus ramp side by side with Dad right behind us.

"Senator Prairie!" the GNN and Beacon News correspondents shouted, sprinting across the crosswalk with their cameramen as the NFA lobbyist and Richard Sterling froze on the headquarters steps behind them. "The National Firearms Association just gave you a lifetime F grade for calling the MR-15 a weapon of war, and Richard Sterling just attacked your record on healthcare, abortion, and transgender kids in schools! Do you regret calling the MR-15 a weapon of war?"

Katie braked her SwiftChair right beside the Fairfax Link bus stop sign, took one of the red NFA "F-Grade" mailers from a reporter's hand, and held it straight up beside her lapel so every lens had a crystal-clear shot.

"Regret it?" Katie's voice rang out across the Fairfax boulevard, fearless and unapologetic. "I wear this F grade from the National Firearms Association like a badge of honor! In fact, Nate, let's frame this right on the wall of our campaign headquarters when we get home to Kempsville! Unlike the lobbyists inside that building who cash checks from gun manufacturers, I served on the Virginia Beach City Council in the exact Municipal Center complex where twelve innocent public servants and citizens were murdered in Building 2 in May 2019, and I know the families who lost their loved ones at Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute in Blacksburg. I have zero problem calling an MR-15 what it is: a weapon of war designed to inflict maximum human carnage on a battlefield. Keeping weapons of war out of Virginia classrooms, college campuses, and grocery stores so our children come home alive from school matters a whole lot more to me than pleasing the gun lobby in Fairfax!"

"And what about Richard Sterling's attacks on healthcare, abortion, and his claim that you want men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms?" the Beacon News reporter pressed.

"Let's go down his list one by one, because unlike the politicians hiding on those steps, I don't need a pollster to tell me what I believe," Katie answered without missing a beat. "First: yes, I proudly champion universal healthcare for every single person in the United States of America, because no family should go bankrupt to pay for insulin, cancer treatment, or a power wheelchair! Second: abortion is a deeply personal, private medical decision strictly between a woman and her doctor—probably the hardest decision a woman ever has to make—and no politician, prosecutor, or senator belongs in that exam room, which is why Virginia voters are going to vote YES on Question 1 this November! And third: when Restore the Republic politicians scream their disgusting talking point that I 'want men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms,' they are bullying vulnerable transgender kids—who make up less than one percent of the population and just want to go to school safely—to distract working Virginians while they vote to gut your Medicare, your Medicaid waivers, and your public schools! Transgender women are women, transgender men are men, and as I told the Virginia House of Delegates back in 2016 when they tried to pass a fifty-dollar bathroom-police bill, no woman or girl in Virginia should ever have to pull her pants down for a government official just to use the ladies' room!"

Behind the camera line, a Fairfax County high-school teacher waiting at the bus shelter burst into applause, followed by a dozen office workers on the sidewalk who walked straight over to Dad's clipboard to sign Katie's Eleventh District petition sheets right in front of the stunned NFA officials.

Five days later, when Katie, Dad, and I rolled off the JRTC Route 6 bus at Capitol Square in Richmond to file our primary petitions at the Virginia State Board of Elections, we didn't turn in ten thousand signatures. We turned in twenty-four thousand, eight hundred and twelve verified voter signatures—with more than twelve hundred verified voters in every single one of Virginia's eleven Congressional Districts.
