## Monday, May 18, 2026, 11:30 AM — Roanoke's Third Street Station, The RVT Roanoke Express, & The Brooks Family Compound on Lee Highway

"Watch how fast the unified validator reads the card, Dad," forty-one-year-old Katie smiled at eleven-thirty on Monday morning, May 18, 2026, as the three of us detrained from ARTS *The Patriot* at Roanoke's Third Street Station and rolled across the bus plaza to the waiting forty-five-foot Roanoke Valley Transit coach.

Because the passenger rail extension to Christiansburg Cambria Station at 800 Depot Street NE wouldn't open for regular train service until the following year in 2027, Roanoke Valley Transit operated both the official *ARTS Train Connector* and *The Roanoke Express* out of Third Street Station—linking downtown Roanoke down Interstate 81 to the Christiansburg Transit Center at Falling Branch, New River Valley Mall, and the CPI Transit Center in Blacksburg.

As the operator deployed the coach lift and secured our two SwiftChairs in the front ADA bays, Katie, Dad, and I each tapped our blue-and-slate contactless Blue Ridge Go-Cards—printed with the regional slogan *One Card. Three Systems. The Entire Valley.*—against the electronic reader, deducting the standard three-dollar RVT Express fare with a crisp green beep.

Forty minutes later, our *Roanoke Express* coach glided off Interstate 81 at Exit 118 into the ground-level eight-bay bus terminal beneath VDOT's eight-level, six-thousand-space garage at the Christiansburg Transit Center at Falling Branch, exited directly onto the grade-separated buses-only flyover ramp onto the US-460 Bypass without touching a single local traffic light, made its intermediate regional stop at the New River Valley Mall Transit Center outside Hudson's and The Spot, and pulled into the multi-bay CPI Transit Center in Blacksburg at 12:30 PM.

For the next four hours, Katie, Dad, and I rode the entire New River Valley transit network alongside the students, faculty, and working families who used it every day.

Aboard a sixty-foot Prairie Coachworks articulated bus in the *Mountain Mist and River Ribbon* livery on Blue Ridge Transit Route 1—displaying <led>1 CBURG TRANS CTR</led> and <led>via S MAIN ST</led> on its amber LED destination sign—we rolled down Progress Street NE in Blacksburg, where Ryan and Cassidy O'Connell, their twin cousins Evan and Tyler Wright, and their youngest sister Holly O'Connell were waiting at the Progress Street bus stop after ten o'clock in the morning to wave and hand Katie an insulated flask of cold ginger ale for the road. Continuing south down Business 460 into downtown Christiansburg to the Pepper Plaza Transit Center right next door to the Montgomery County Courthouse across from 105 Pepper Street and ValueMart, we transferred onto a thirty-five-foot low-floor bus on BT Route 2 (*The Christiansburg Local*, displaying <led>2 NEW RIVER VALLEY MALL</led> / <led>via AQUATIC CTR & CAMBRIA</led>). Rolling past the two Ironwood Collective apartment complexes on Wades Lane and turning down Mill Lane NE past the Blue Ridge Regional Aquatic Center onto Depot Street NE, we inspected the newly paved off-street bus loop at 800 Depot Street NE—Christiansburg Cambria Station—where Katie recorded a campaign video pledging that when ARTS *The Patriot* passenger trains began pulling into Cambria Station in 2027, she would use her seat in the United States Senate to protect federal passenger rail and rural public transit funding all the way to Bristol.

After riding BT Route 4 east out Roanoke Street past the Exit 118C hotels and the Simmons Road NE deviation through the CPI Christiansburg Campus—universally called "the Law School" by every rider on the bus—out to the purpose-built VDOT sixty-foot turnaround loop at Woodland Drive, and taking a loop on 100-percent fare-free Radford Transit aboard *The Radford Connector* down Prices Fork Road and Peppers Ferry Road through Belview, Centerville, and Fairlawn across the New River Bridge into Radford's McConnell Transit Center, we boarded Blue Ridge Transit Route 3 (*The Radford Link*) heading west out of Pepper Plaza along U.S. Route 11.

Halfway between Christiansburg and Radford along Lee Highway, the Route 3 operator tapped his brakes for the privately funded, covered bus shelter sitting on the one-dollar perpetual public transit easement that Daniel Brooks had conveyed at the foot of his family's driveway back in 2014.

When the front ramp folded down onto the concrete pad and Katie, Dad, and I rolled up the heated, zero-grade brushed-concrete walkway onto the front porch, the front double doors swung wide open—and the real-life heroes of "The Accessibility Project" came out to meet us, led by the original trio from Bennett Hall Room 214.

Thirty-year-old Jordan Shawn Kelly rolled his custom manual chair straight up to Katie and me to shake our hands, wearing his rectangular prescription glasses and behind-the-ear hearing aids, flanked on either side by his wife Aubrie Jean Kelly and Madison Elizabeth Scott, along with Zoë Wren Brooks, Lauren Nicole Barnes, Hannah Parker Anderson, Mark Shawn Anderson, Isabel Claire Dawson, Jessamine "Jess" Mercer, Sarah Kristyn Braithwaite, Jessica Gallagher, and Daniel, Laura, and Megan Brooks—every single one of them wearing royal-blue *Katie Prairie for U.S. Senate* lapel buttons.

Rolling side by side down the sweeping forty-eight-inch hallways lined with recessed oak wall bumpers and through the zero-threshold pocket doors into the communal kitchen—where dual-height islands let Jordan, Katie, and me roll right underneath the counter beside Laura, Aubrie Jean, and Madison—Katie looked around the room in quiet awe.

"We've spent twenty-five years designing our own living spaces around our wheelchairs," Katie said warmly, looking from the heated concrete bus-stop walkway outside to the dual-height kitchen islands and the first-floor grandparents' suite. "Rolling off a regular fixed-route Route 3 bus on Lee Highway straight into a house built like this feels like seeing the exact future we're fighting for."

Beside us at the accessible island, Jordan tapped a sentence into NeoEdit on his Pinnacle Vanguard Pro laptop and triggered EchoType's warm "Sydney" voice:

<aac>When Daniel and Laura built this compound on Lee Highway, they proved that universal design isn't a medical burden—it's what makes a home belong to everyone. Now let's go show the rest of Virginia.</aac>

"And we've already registered over fourteen hundred new student voters across CPI and Radford University for the June sixteenth primary," Zoë added, pulling up her tablet angled toward her eyes to read off the campus precinct tallies she, Aubrie Jean, and Mark had organized with the student disability union at Callahan Hall. "Preston Holloway's campaign sent a surrogate to Blacksburg last week who parked in a hundred-and-fifty-dollar VIP lot and left after twenty minutes. Meanwhile, every student on the Route 1 articulated buses and the Radford Connector knows you and Nate actually ride the bus and play our song at every stop."

"And speaking of buses," thirty-year-old Madison Elizabeth Scott smiled, tapping the Blue Ridge Go-Card clipped to her lanyard as Aubrie Jean and Jordan grinned beside her, "the three of us from Room 214 are riding down to Carroll County with you today! Bluewater is my hometown—and the Bluewater Bunker where my parents, my brother Bob, and my cousin Emily live is forty-five minutes down Interstate 81 and Interstate 77 from here. The next southbound *Maroon Express* pulls into the Christiansburg Transit Center at 1:55 PM. Ready to go show Bluewater what a real Senator looks like?"

## Monday, May 18, 2026, 2:30 PM — Riding Bluewater Transit's Maroon Express Into Bluewater With the Room 214 Crew & Winning the June 16 Primary

![At the Bluewater Transit Center across from Bluewater High School in Bluewater, Virginia in May 2026, State Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs alongside the Room 214 trio—Jordan Shawn Kelly in his wheelchair with his prescription glasses, his wife Aubrie Jean Kelly with her signature side braid, and Madison Elizabeth Scott—in front of a 45-foot Bluewater Transit Maroon Express motorcoach.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/may_2026_bluewater_transit_center_maroon_express_katie_nate_madison.jpg)

*<pa>Bluewater Transit Maroon Express to Bluewater Transit Center via Christiansburg Transit Center,</pa>* the exterior ADA speaker of the gleaming forty-five-foot Sainte-Claire 45H3 over-the-road highway coach announced at the Christiansburg Transit Center bay while its two-screen amber LED sign alternated between <led>MAROON EXPRESS</led> and <led>BLUEWATER EXP via CBURG TC</led>.

Unlike the low-floor city transit buses on Blue Ridge Transit, Bluewater Transit's *Maroon Express* highway coaches were equipped with heavy-duty mid-coach hydraulic ADA wheelchair lifts mounted above the under-floor luggage bays and expanded multi-bay wheelchair securement rows inside the cabin. With the entire Room 214 trio—Jordan, Aubrie Jean, and Madison—riding right alongside us and Dad tapping our Blue Ridge Go-Cards for the five-dollar Express fare, Katie, Jordan, and I rode the hydraulic platform up into the coach cabin, locked into the floor tie-downs, and watched the Blue Ridge mountains rise outside the tinted coach windows as we cruised south down Interstate 81 past the Dublin Park and Ride at Justin's Station (Exit 98) and the Max Meadows Park and Ride (Exit 84), turned south onto Interstate 77 up the mountain grade past the Bluewater Park and Ride at Exit 14 (where the coach's GPS automatically dropped the Go-Card validator to one-dollar local mode for the final hop into town), and rolled into the Bluewater Transit Center at 2:35 PM.

Directly across the street stood Bluewater High School—and five minutes earlier, at 2:30 PM on the dot, the final dismissal bell had rung.

As the operator lowered Katie, Jordan, and me down the mid-coach hydraulic lift onto the sunny concrete transit plaza beside Aubrie Jean, Madison, and Dad, hundreds of high-school students, teachers, cafeteria workers, and parents were pouring across the crosswalk into the transit center for the 2:45 PM clockface pulse—where Madison's parents David and Diane Scott, her younger brother Bob in his wheelchair, and her cousin Emily were waiting by the shelter to wave and cheer us in. Surrounding the bays stood Bluewater Transit's twenty-nine-foot and thirty-five-foot low-floor buses waiting to depart in every direction across the Blue Ridge plateau: the hourly Purple Line heading west on US-58 and US-221 to Galax Community College; the Gold Line heading northeast up US-221 through Dugspur and Willis to the Floyd County Courthouse (where riders connected down State Route 8 to Riner and BT Route 8); the Emerald Line heading east on US-58 across the Blue Ridge Parkway at Meadows of Dan and down scenic Lovers Leap into Stuart and the Martinsville Transit Center (where riders transferred directly onto Piedmont Regional Transit to Danville); the Silver Line heading south down US-52 and Interstate 77 past Fancy Gap to the state-line transfer hub; and a thirty-five-foot low-floor Mountain Empire Transit *Highlands Express* coach waiting to carry riders west toward Wytheville, Marion, Abingdon, and Bristol.

"People in Washington look at a county map of Southwest Virginia and write Carroll County off as deep-red Restore the Republic territory without ever setting foot here," Madison told Katie and me proudly as she, Aubrie Jean, and Jordan greeted Bluewater High School teachers, working parents, and Galax Community College nursing students who gathered around our three wheelchairs on the plaza while "The Accessibility Project" played crisp and bright from our portable speaker. "What the out-of-town pundits never understand is that Bluewater is the largest incorporated town in the entire Commonwealth of Virginia—solely because the voters here in Bluewater have always refused to break away from Carroll County and become an independent city! If Bluewater were an independent city like Galax or Radford, it would show up as a solid Democratic blue circle on every election map in America."

Right there in front of the *Maroon Express* coach during the 2:45 PM pulse—with Katie, Jordan, and me parked side by side in our wheelchairs and Aubrie Jean and Madison handing out campaign literature and voter-registration clipboards beside Dad—Katie spent an hour talking eye-to-eye with Carroll County voters before we boarded Bluewater Transit's Emerald Line east across Lovers Leap through Meadows of Dan and Stuart into the Martinsville Transit Center and transferred onto Piedmont Regional Transit eastbound along U.S. Route 58 into Danville.

Waiting for our bus under the Danville transfer shelter—surrounded by television camera crews from Roanoke, Lynchburg, and Danville who had gotten word an hour earlier that a political bombshell was about to drop—were twenty-four-year-old Sam, Mandy, and Sarah, our three favorite autistic House Pages from February 2016 whom Katie had hired onto her Senate of Virginia staff as soon as they enrolled at Central Virginia University, standing side by side with their Aunt Marge and their Uncle Richard.

For more than two decades in the Virginia House of Delegates—culminating in his years wielding the floor gavel in Richmond as Republican House Majority Leader—Richard had been a household name in Virginia politics and the undisputed conservative lion of Southside Virginia. Every county sheriff, poultry and tobacco farmer, small-business owner, and civic club from Martinsville and Danville to South Boston knew Richard by his first name. As the Piedmont Regional Transit operator lowered the front hydraulic ramp and Katie, Jordan, and I rolled down onto the Danville sidewalk beside Dad, Aubrie Jean, and Madison, Uncle Richard stepped forward in his navy blazer, shook Dad's hand and mine, leaned down to hug Katie warmly, and peeled the paper backing off a royal-blue *Katie Prairie for U.S. Senate* sticker—pressing it squarely onto his left lapel right in front of the rolling television cameras.

An audible gasp rippled through the cluster of Roanoke and Danville political reporters as Uncle Richard stepped up to the microphone stand with Aunt Marge and the triplets standing directly behind Katie's SwiftChair.

"Everybody along Route 58 knows I served twenty-four years in the House of Delegates as a conservative Republican, and that I had the honor of serving as your Republican House Majority Leader in Richmond," Uncle Richard said, his steady Southside baritone carrying across the entire transit plaza. "Marge and I don't agree with State Senator Katie Prairie on every partisan roll-call vote, and Katie has never once asked us to. Eleven years ago, after Marge and I took legal custody of our thirteen-year-old autistic triplet nephew and nieces—Sam, Mandy, and Sarah—after they survived unspeakable abuse, our kids went up to Richmond to serve as eighth-grade House Pages. When a loud, crowded corridor in the General Assembly Building overwhelmed our kids during their first week on the job, it wasn't the loudmouth politicians preening on cable television about family values who stopped to protect them. It was Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate, who parked their two wheelchairs around our kids like a shield, sat with them at eye level until their breathing steadied, and rode the Route 6 city bus with our whole family to supper on Broad Street."

Uncle Richard rested a proud hand on Sam's shoulder while Aunt Marge squeezed Mandy's and Sarah's hands.

"Four years later, the day Sam, Mandy, and Sarah graduated high school right here in Southside and started college at Central Virginia University, Senator Katie Prairie kept her promise and hired all three of them to work side by side in her Senate office so they would never be separated," Uncle Richard continued, pointing back toward the Piedmont Regional Transit bus idling at the curb. "And that bus sitting behind us—connecting Martinsville and Danville—is running down Route 58 today because Katie Prairie took a transit bill that three eighth-grade Pages from Southside wrote for their Mock Session ten years ago and fought in the Senate of Virginia until it was funded into law. So when Washington consultants for Preston Holloway or Richard Sterling roll through Southside in chartered luxury buses telling you that Katie Prairie can't win rural voters or doesn't care about families, don't buy a word of it. Marge and I are crossing party lines to endorse Katie Prairie for the United States Senate—because for ten years, Katie Prairie has had our family's back."

In the front row of the press scrum, a Roanoke political correspondent thrust his microphone forward, almost tripping over his own camera cable.

"Leader Richard, are you leaving the Republican Party?" the reporter shouted.

"No," Uncle Richard answered flatly.

"But you're endorsing the Democratic nominee for the United States Senate!"

"I am."

"How do you reconcile those two things after twenty-four years as a Republican in the House of Delegates?"

Uncle Richard looked straight into the cluster of television lenses, his jaw set with the quiet granite authority of a man who had counted votes and governed a real legislative chamber for nearly a quarter of a century.

"I didn't leave the Republican Party. The Republican Party left me," Uncle Richard said, and you could hear every camera shutter on the sidewalk freeze. "The people of Danville and Southside Virginia didn't send me to Richmond for twenty-four years to serve a President, and they didn't send me there to serve the Republican Party. They sent me there to serve them. They didn't elect me because they wanted to watch Republicans and Democrats scream at each other on cable television—they sent me there to solve problems, pass budgets, maintain our roads and bridges, fund our schools, support our farmers and small businesses, and make sure the Commonwealth of Virginia still functions tomorrow morning. Twenty-four years in Richmond teaches you that ideological speeches don't fill potholes. Sometimes solving problems meant working with Republicans, and sometimes it meant working with Democrats—and one of those Democrats was Katie Prairie."

Uncle Richard glanced down at my sister with a dry, affectionate glint in his eye.

"Katie and I disagreed plenty—if she tells you otherwise, don't believe her," Richard added, making Katie laugh aloud from her SwiftChair along with the entire crowd at the bus shelter. "But I never once doubted that she came to Richmond for the exact same reason I did: to do the work. Ten years ago on the floor of the House of Delegates, when one of my own members brought a badly drafted bathroom bill onto the calendar and tried to call Katie out of order for asking how the Commonwealth was supposed to enforce it, I stood up as Republican Majority Leader and told the Speaker that the Gentlewoman from Virginia Beach didn't introduce anatomy into the legislation—the bill did. Because I learned the hard way in Richmond that when Katie Prairie reads a bill line by line and says there is a problem with how it actually works, you had better hear her out before assuming she's wrong. I've supported Republican Presidents, I've opposed Democratic Presidents, and I've probably been angry at every Governor Virginia has had at least once. That's politics. Nobody elected me to swear personal allegiance to another politician. First the Liberty Tax Movement came along and started screaming that any conservative who compromised to pass a budget was a traitor to be primaried. And then Restore the Republic took over and inverted everything our party ever stood for—replacing principles with a single loyalty test to Garrett Thorne. I'll tell you right here on camera: before 2016, I had never voted for a Democratic candidate in my entire life. Not once. Then Garrett Thorne came down that escalator, mocked disabled Americans on a stage, and demanded personal fealty above the Constitution. In 2016, in 2020, and in 2024, Marge and I voted for the Democrat at the top of the presidential ballot—not because those were votes for the Democrat, but because Garrett Thorne's name was on the ballot and I refused to put that man in the White House. Back then, I still told myself it was just one man at the top of the ticket. And for a man who spent twenty-four years working inside a Capitol alongside clerks, custodians, Capitol Police officers, and thirteen-year-old Pages like my own kids, watching a mob attack the United States Capitol on January sixth while lawmakers and teenaged Pages were locked inside carrying out a constitutional duty was the day I knew that movement had nothing to do with conservatism. Then came our Virginia Governor's race last November, in 2025. Garrett Thorne's name wasn't on the ballot in 2025—that was an election for Governor of the Commonwealth I spent twenty-four years serving. The Governor of Virginia should answer to the People of the Commonwealth of Virginia, not to President Garrett Thorne! Walking into my Danville precinct and voting against the Republican nominee for Governor of Virginia was the hardest ballot I ever cast, and the first one that truly crossed the Rubicon for me—because when our statewide nominee aligned with Thorne and promised to answer to the White House instead of the People of the Commonwealth of Virginia, I realized this sickness wasn't just one man in Washington anymore. Any candidate who aligns themselves with Garrett Thorne has to learn at the ballot box that Virginia does not belong to him. Now Restore the Republic calls me a RINO and an 'Enemy of the Republic' because I refuse to pledge personal fealty to Garrett Thorne—when the whole point of a constitutional republic is that no citizen owes personal loyalty to a President in the first place! And when they call Katie Prairie an enemy of the Republic, I ask them one simple question: *Have you ever actually worked with her? No? I have.* No Super PAC commercial can tell me she hates families when I've watched her take care of my kids for ten years, and no cable pundit can tell me she hates rural Virginia when she listened to three thirteen-year-olds from Danville talk about public transportation. In 2016, 2020, and 2024, I voted against Garrett Thorne himself. In 2025, I voted against a Republican candidate for Governor to send a message that aligning yourself with Garrett Thorne costs you Virginia. And this year is different again—because this year, I am not just voting against Thorne or his movement in the privacy of a voting booth. I am standing here in broad daylight voting wholeheartedly *for* Katie Prairie. I'm still a Republican. Katie's still a Democrat. I haven't changed parties. I've simply reached an age where I don't need anybody's permission to recognize a good public servant."

On the evening bus ride back up U.S. Route 58 toward Bluewater to catch the northbound *Maroon Express*—while video of the former Republican House Majority Leader declaring *"I didn't leave the Republican Party; the Republican Party left me"* at a Danville bus shelter led every six o'clock television newscast from Roanoke to Norfolk and Washington—Sam, Mandy, and Sarah watched our rural Southside traffic and voter-pledge numbers vertical-spike on my laptop screen and grinned in unison, coining a phrase that every political desk in Virginia would be repeating by November: "the Uncle Richard Effect."

Four weeks later—on Primary Night, Tuesday, June 16, 2026—the political establishment in Washington and Richmond learned the hard way what happens when you dismiss the people who ride the bus.

Despite retiring Senator Martin Warren's handpicked corporate candidate Preston Holloway spending fourteen million dollars in Super PAC television ads, voters across all eleven Congressional Districts—powered by historic turnout in Hampton Roads, Richmond, the CPI and Radford corridors, Bluewater, Charlottesville, and working-class neighborhoods across Northern Virginia—delivered a seismic upset.
