## Friday, July 24, 2026, 9:30 AM — Speaking Directly to RTR Voters Aboard Mountain Empire Transit & Cumberland Coalfield Transit

![Inside a Mountain Empire Transit bus rolling through the Appalachian ridges of Southwest Virginia in July 2026, 41-year-old State Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs in the front ADA securement bays speaking at eye level with a retired coal miner and a rural clinic nurse while their 68-year-old father David sits across the aisle with his yellow legal pad.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jul_2026_southwest_virginia_coalfield_bus_town_hall_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"I'll be honest with you, Senator Prairie—I voted for Garrett Thorne twice, and I haven't seen a Democratic candidate for the United States Senate set foot on a bus out here west of Wytheville in thirty years," the sixty-six-year-old retired coal miner in the faded denim work shirt said across the aisle of the Mountain Empire Transit *Highlands Express* bus as we rolled west through Smyth County between Marion and Abingdon at nine-thirty on Friday morning, July 24, 2026.

Seated right beside him on the perimeter bench was a thirty-four-year-old rural clinic nurse in navy scrubs heading to her shift at Appalachian Mountain Health, while across the aisle our sixty-eight-year-old dad sat with his yellow legal pad resting on his knee and our New River Valley volunteers Lauren Nicole Barnes and Hannah Parker Anderson sat one row back wearing their royal-blue *Katie Prairie for U.S. Senate* buttons.

Outside the bus windows along Interstate 81 and old Lee Highway, huge red-white-and-blue *Thorne / Vance — Restore the Republic* barn signs and *Richard Sterling for U.S. Senate* banners dotted the pasture fences across the Ninth Congressional District—counties where Restore the Republic candidates routinely won seventy to eighty percent of the vote, and where Washington party consultants had begged Katie not to waste a single day of her summer schedule.

Instead, Katie had insisted on riding the entire Southwest Virginia transit spine: taking Bluewater Transit's *Maroon Express* to the Max Meadows Park and Ride at Exit 84, transferring with our Blue Ridge Go-Cards onto Mountain Empire Transit's *Highlands Express* through Wytheville, Marion, Abingdon, and Bristol Union Station, and then boarding Cumberland Coalfield Transit cutaway buses equipped with rear hydraulic Elevate-U wheelchair lifts up US-23 and US-58A through Big Stone Gap, Powell Valley Community College, Norton, Commonwealth College at Wise, Clintwood in Dickenson County, and Grundy in Buchanan County.

"The consultants in Washington told me not to come out here because they look at a spreadsheet instead of looking at people," forty-one-year-old Katie told the retired miner warmly from her SwiftChair, her left hand resting in mine across our armrests as the bus climbed the mountain grade toward Abingdon. "If I ask to represent the Commonwealth of Virginia in the United States Senate, I don't just represent the cities that vote sixty-five percent Democratic—I represent every single town, hollow, and bus route in all ninety-five counties and thirty-eight independent cities. Ask me anything on your mind, sir."

The retired miner nodded slowly, resting his calloused hands on the handle of his cane.

"Alright then," he said. "Every night on Beacon News and LibertyMax, Richard Sterling and Julian Vance run commercials saying you got an F grade from the National Firearms Association for calling an MR-15 a weapon of war, that you want a government takeover of healthcare, that you support unrestricted abortion, and that you want biological men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms. Out here in the mountains, people hunt, people go to church, and people worry about what's changing. Why should a working family in Smyth County or Wise County give you the time of day?"

Inside the quiet bus cabin, every passenger within five rows leaned in to listen. Katie didn't hedge a single word or change a single syllable from what she said in Virginia Beach or Fairfax.

"Because I respect you enough to tell you the exact truth to your face instead of lying to you to pick your pocket," Katie answered, looking him straight in the eye. "Let's start with guns. My granddad and plenty of my constituents hunt deer and turkey in Virginia. Not a single bill I have ever supported touches a hunter's shotgun or bolt-action deer rifle. What I called a weapon of war—and what got me that F grade from the National Firearms Association up in Fairfax, which I wear as a badge of honor—is the MR-15 high-velocity semiautomatic rifle with a thirty-round magazine that was used to slaughter twelve people in the Virginia Beach Municipal Center where I served on City Council. You know the difference between a deer rifle and a battlefield weapon, and keeping weapons of war out of our children's classrooms so grandkids come home alive from school is common sense."

The clinic nurse in the navy scrubs nodded firmly beside the miner.

"Second, let's talk about universal healthcare and abortion," Katie continued. "Right here in Southwest Virginia, Appalachian Mountain Health has closed rural maternity wards and emergency rooms while private health-insurance CEOs cash thirty-million-dollar bonus checks and Appalachian Electric Grid hikes your winter electric bill by forty dollars a month. When a pregnant mother in Wise County or Lee County has to drive ninety minutes over a mountain in winter just to reach an obstetrician, or when a woman faces the most heartbreaking medical complication of her life, that decision belongs strictly between a woman and her doctor—never a politician in Washington or Richmond. And when a retired coal miner with black lung or a diabetic bus rider gets denied a prescription or a wheelchair repair by an insurance bureaucrat, that's why I fight for universal healthcare—so healthcare is a basic human right for every American from Virginia Beach to Big Stone Gap."

Katie leaned forward an inch in her SwiftChair, her voice dropping to an earnest, unmistakable intensity.

"And finally, let's talk about why Richard Sterling and Julian Vance spend millions of dollars screaming on television that I 'want men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms,'" Katie said. "My twin brother Nate and I have used these power wheelchairs since the third Friday of September 2001, when we were sixteen-year-old juniors in high school. We know what it feels like to be stared at, mocked by the President of the United States as 'Broken Katie Prairie,' and locked out of buildings just for existing in the bodies we were born with. Transgender kids make up less than one percent of the population, and all they want is to go to school and live their lives without grown politicians bullying them or hiring bathroom police. The Restore the Republic billionaires scream about transgender kids every night on cable news for one reason: they hope that if they keep you angry enough at a sixteen-year-old kid you've never met, you won't notice Richard Sterling and Julian Vance voting to cut eight hundred billion dollars out of Medicaid, rural hospitals, and black-lung disability clinics!"

The retired miner stared at Katie for three full seconds—and then a slow, genuine grin broke across his weathered face.

"Well, I'll be damned," he said, reaching into his shirt pocket for his pen and gesturing toward Dad's yellow legal pad. "My brother-in-law has been waiting fourteen months on a Medicaid waiver for his wheelchair ramp in Norton while our hospital cut its night shift. Give me one of those campaign bumper stickers, Mr. Prairie—anybody who rides the morning bus into Smyth County and talks that straight has my vote."

## Saturday, July 25, 2026, 2:15 PM — Bridging the Transit Gap in Dad's Rented Wheelchair Van to Cumberland Gap

"Look right there on the bronze plaque, fifth name from the top—*Silas Prairie, 1775*," Dad said quietly, resting one hand on Katie's shoulder and one hand on mine as the three of us looked out from the paved, accessible cliffside trail at the Pinnacle Overlook of Cumberland Gap National Historical Park at 2,440 feet of elevation.

The afternoon before in Big Stone Gap, after we had ridden a Cumberland Coalfield Transit cutaway bus to Powell Valley Community College and held a standing-room-only town hall where retired coal miners with oxygen tanks and union rail workers lined up alongside our SwiftChairs while "The Accessibility Project" echoed through the auditorium, we had reached the westernmost edge of fixed-route public transit in Lee County. Forty miles of winding mountain highway still lay between Big Stone Gap and the western tip of the Commonwealth of Virginia at Cumberland Gap—and just as Katie had promised in our living room in January, whenever there was a gap in public transit connections across rural Virginia, Dad bridged it himself. At eight o'clock that morning in Norton, Dad had rented a silver side-entry wheelchair-accessible minivan equipped with a fold-out aluminum floor ramp and dual four-point floor tie-downs in the removed middle row, driving Katie and me west down U.S. Route 58 through Jonesville and Rose Hill all the way to the mountain pass where Virginia, Kentucky, and Tennessee met beneath the summer clouds.

"Two hundred and fifty-one years ago, Silas Prairie helped cut a path through this mountain pass so families could cross into a new frontier," Dad said, his voice warm with pride as he looked out across the endless green ridges of the Appalachian Mountains stretching back toward the Shenandoah and the Atlantic coast. "And today his forty-one-year-old twin descendants rolled all the way from the Atlantic Ocean at Virginia Beach to the westernmost tip of Virginia to make sure nobody ever gets left behind at the curb."
