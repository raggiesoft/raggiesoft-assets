## Saturday, September 19, 2026, 4:30 PM — Past Fishersville on Shenandoah Valley Transit & Over Shenandoah Mountain to The Cassini Center in Palo Alto

![Under the open roll-off roof of The Cassini Center for Space Exploration in Palo Alto, Highland County, Virginia beneath the starry Milky Way in September 2026, 41-year-old Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands in a circle with 41-year-old non-verbal autistic triplets Lyra, Orion, and Celeste Starling in their custom tilt-in-space wheelchairs around a 34-inch-high glowing Starlight Wing nebula table and wheelchair-height telescope eyepiece, while Valerie Anne Miller and David Prairie smile beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/sep_2026_cassini_center_palo_alto_observatory_katie_nate_valerie_starling_triplets.jpg)

"Look out the right window, Katie—there's Barton Hall and the front gate on Roosevelt Avenue," I murmured on Saturday morning, September 19, 2026, as our Shenandoah Valley Transit Route 250 Connector bus rolled through Fishersville between Waynesboro and Staunton in Augusta County.

Nineteen and a half years had passed since Sunday, March 18, 2007, when Dad had packed up our rooms at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center after our twelve unforgettable days as twenty-one-year-old Web Applications Developer students—twelve days in which we had rushed a two-week Vocational Evaluation in a single week after an evaluator suggested training us as Waynesboro ValueMart door greeters, refused to let staff grab our wheelchair push-handles without consent, stayed stuck on Level 2 in Barton Hall because we refused to be separated, got caught sleeping in our street clothes inside a locked seasonal storage closet on Tuesday night after a male client broke Katie's nose and tore her swimsuit top in the therapeutic pool, and rolled out with our friend Tina after watching the administration sweep that same male client's horrific Wednesday-night assault of our sweet dorm neighbor Natalie under the rug.

When the Route 250 Connector stopped at the Fishersville shelter outside the gates, four current disabled vocational students in power wheelchairs boarded the bus, recognized Katie immediately from her campaign commercials, and spent the twenty-minute ride into Staunton telling her how much it meant to see someone in a SwiftChair running for the United States Senate.

At the Staunton transit hub, fixed-route public bus service ended at the eastern foot of the Allegheny Mountains. West of Staunton lay forty-two miles of steep, winding mountain switchbacks up U.S. Route 250 across the George Washington National Forest, climbing over the crests of Calfpasture Mountain, Shenandoah Mountain, and Bullpasture Mountain into Highland County—Virginia's highest-elevation, least-populous mountain county, nicknamed Virginia's Little Switzerland.

So at one-thirty that afternoon in Staunton, Dad picked up the keys to another rented silver side-entry wheelchair-accessible ramp minivan, secured Katie and me side by side into the four-point floor tie-downs in the center cabin while forty-one-year-old Brittany and forty-four-year-old Chelsea buckled into the rear passenger bench, and drove all four of us west over the 3,000-foot summit of Shenandoah Mountain before turning north onto Cowpasture Road N—Route 614—into the quiet mountain hollow of Palo Alto, Highland County.

And at four-thirty that afternoon, we rolled down the minivan ramp onto the grounds of The Cassini Center for Space Exploration for the very first time.

Waiting for us on the forty-eight-inch-wide, ultra-smooth zero-threshold polished concrete arrival plaza beneath the timber-and-stone mountain pavilion was forty-five-year-old Valerie Anne Miller—and parked right beside her in their three custom-molded, high-back tilt-in-space wheelchairs were her forty-one-year-old non-verbal autistic triplet cousins: Lyra Carina Starling, Orion Rigel Starling, and Celeste Callisto Starling.

Born in the autumn of 1985—just months after Katie and I were born in April 1985—Lyra, Orion, and Celeste were the exact same age as the two of us.

Years earlier, after realizing that standard crowded science museums with glaring fluorescent lights, roaring echoes, stepped planetarium seats, and high-mounted telescope eyepieces locked her triplet cousins out of the cosmos they loved, Valerie had dedicated her life and her family's Palo Alto mountain sanctuary to building a world-class astrophysics and dark-sky observatory engineered from the ground up around her cousins' three wheelchairs and autistic sensory needs.

"Welcome to Palo Alto, Senator Prairie, Nate, Mr. Prairie, Brittany, and Chelsea!" Valerie beamed, greeting my sister by her sitting State Senate title as she stepped forward to shake Dad's hand and welcome Brittany and Chelsea while Lyra, Orion, and Celeste offered joyful, rhythmic hand-flaps and soft welcoming vocalizations from their chairs—prompting Chelsea to smile ear to ear behind her glasses and wave both hands gently back in greeting. "The triplets have been watching your bus videos on our tablet all summer—when I told them this morning that forty-one-year-old twins who use wheelchairs and love space were coming up the mountain with their family to visit them for the first time, Orion tapped his communication switch for 'Saturn' six times in a row!"

Katie rolled her SwiftChair right up beside Lyra's and Celeste's armrests while I rolled up beside Orion's, and over the next four hours—as Katie saw firsthand what Valerie and the triplets had built for the children and families of the Commonwealth, and watched Chelsea blossom in a museum with zero harsh fluorescent lights or echoing crowds—the Cassini Center became my sister's absolute favorite place in all of Virginia.

Rolling five chairs abreast with Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea walking right alongside us down the seamless, zero-threshold hallways lit by warm, indirect amber path LEDs with zero harsh overhead glare and whisper-quiet acoustic timber baffling, Valerie led us first into The Starlight Wing and The Cassini Experience. Instead of vertical glass cases hung at standing eye level, the gallery's six-foot-wide backlit ultra-high-resolution cosmic tables were mounted horizontally at an exact thirty-four-inch height with thirty inches of open knee and footplate clearance underneath—allowing Katie, me, Lyra, Orion, and Celeste to roll our wheelchairs flush around the glowing edge of the Andromeda Galaxy and the Pillars of Creation while Chelsea leaned gently beside Brittany's shoulder, tracing the swirling gold and violet starlight above the glass without anyone ever scolding her to hurry along.

Next, we rolled into The Tranquility Dome—the Cassini Center's planetarium, built with a completely flat, level-entry floor, zero stepped concrete rings, and open perimeter lock-in bays where all five of our wheelchairs parked in a tight circle beneath the dome alongside cushioned companion seats for Valerie, Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea while the sensory-safe sound system played a gentle, low-decibel cosmic soundtrack with zero sudden thunderclaps or startling strobe flashes.

And after sunset, when the Bortle Class 2 dark skies of Highland County blazed to life above the Allegheny ridges with the naked-eye ribbon of the Milky Way, we rolled out into the Roll-Off Roof Observatory.

As the timber-and-steel roof glided silently open on its cushioned rails, Valerie guided us to the twenty-four-inch Ritchey-Chrétien research telescope at the center of the polished concrete deck. Fitted to the rear cell of the telescope was a custom counterweight-balanced, articulated periscope relay eyepiece engineered specifically to swing down to the exact seated eye level of any manual, power, or tilt-in-space wheelchair—and smoothly adjust for any standing guest as well.

One by one—first Lyra, then Orion, then Celeste, then Katie, then me, and then Chelsea with Brittany's gentle steadying hand on her elbow—we peered into the crystal-clear optics at the golden, tilted rings of Saturn—the namesake of the Cassini spacecraft—floating jewel-bright against the black velvet of deep space.

"Pretty rings, Katie," Chelsea whispered in pure wonder through her slurred voice, her brown eyes shining in the starlight as Brittany and Valerie smiled warmly.

When Katie sat back from the eyepiece and reached her left hand across to hold mine while resting her right hand gently beside Lyra's armrest, her eyes were shining under the starlight.

"We've visited every corner of all ninety-five counties and thirty-eight independent cities in Virginia this year, Valerie," Katie whispered softly so she wouldn't disturb the quiet mountain sanctuary around Lyra, Orion, and Celeste. "Before we rolled down our minivan ramp at four-thirty this afternoon, I had never seen the Cassini Center in person—and after seeing what you and your cousins have built here for the children and families of Virginia, this place right here on Cowpasture Road in Palo Alto has officially become my favorite place in the entire Commonwealth. Everywhere else in the world, Nate and I have to fight to bolt a ramp onto a building that was designed to keep us out. Here, you built the entire universe at the exact height of our chairs."

## Thursday, October 22, 2026, 8:00 PM to 10:15 PM — The Empty-Podium WHRC TV-15 Debate on Hampton Boulevard & The 8:24 PM Three-Bus Sprint Home

"Come on, Nate, Dad—we have twenty-four minutes to make the curb on Hampton Boulevard!" Katie called out at 8:00:00 PM on Thursday evening, October 22, 2026, pivoting her SwiftChair down the studio stage ramp the exact second the red camera tally light winked off inside the WHRC TV-15 and WHRC 89.5 FM public broadcast studios adjacent to Virginia Dominion University in Norfolk.

Forty-eight hours earlier, trailing by seven points in every independent poll across Virginia, Republican nominee Richard Sterling had panicked and boycotted the marquee televised debate after WHRC TV-15 refused to ban moderator Catherine Lindsey from asking about his support for a national abortion ban, his plan to gut Medicaid disability waivers, or President Garrett Thorne's attacks on "Broken Katie Prairie." So for sixty straight minutes from seven to eight o'clock live across every public television and radio station in the Commonwealth, forty-one-year-old Katie had sat in her SwiftChair beside Sterling's empty mahogany podium and answered every voter question with surgical mastery—laying out the exact statutory math for universal healthcare, reproductive freedom, transgender civil rights, common-sense gun safety, the three Virginia Constitutional Amendments on the November ballot, the local one-percent public school construction sales tax referendum, and the East-West Commonwealth Rail Corridor.

Now, declining the studio green-room sparkling water and pundit small talk, the three of us had to thread a razor-thin, three-bus evening transfer chain before the suburban routes shut down for the night.

Rolling out the front doors of the WHRC studio to the Hampton Boulevard bus stop alongside Dad, we caught the 8:24 PM southbound CVTA Route 2 bus down Hampton Boulevard and across Norfolk into the Military Highway Station transfer hub. Rolling off Route 2 at 8:58 PM, we crossed the platform to board the 9:10 PM eastbound CVTA Route 20 along Virginia Beach Boulevard—watching the minutes tick down on my phone as the bus hit every red light near Newtown Road—until our driver pulled up to the intersection of Military Highway and Virginia Beach Boulevard at 9:41 PM, just four minutes before the 9:45 PM final outbound CVTA Route 15 of the night pulled up to the curb heading south toward Indian River Road and Kempsville!

As the Route 15 operator locked our four-point floor straps into place and the bus rolled south down Military Highway in the dark, a nursing student sitting across the aisle looked up from watching the replay of the WHRC debate on her phone, stared at Katie and me in our wheelchairs right in front of her, and gasped out loud.

"Wait—you're Senator Katie Prairie! You were just on television across the entire state of Virginia an hour and forty-five minutes ago!" the student exclaimed. "And you're really riding the last 9:45 PM Route 15 bus home to Kempsville?!"

"If I don't make the 9:45 PM transfer at Military Highway and Virginia Beach Boulevard, I don't get home tonight either," Katie smiled warmly across the aisle, squeezing my hand as the bus rolled toward Kempsville, where Brittany and Chelsea—who had stayed home on Lord Nelson Drive to watch the live broadcast without the late-night studio rush—were already waiting on the lighted front porch with warm mugs of caffeine-free chamomile tea. "And starting in January, we're taking this exact fight to the floor of the United States Senate."
