## Tuesday, November 3, 2026, 7:00 PM — Polls Close & The PHP Precinct Dashboard in Virginia Beach

![On Election Night, November 3, 2026, 41-year-old U.S. Senate candidate Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs at their dual MagnaBook Pro laptops and live Virginia precinct-return dashboard monitor while their 68-year-old father David stands behind them holding a mug of black coffee and pointing excitedly at the early precinct totals.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/nov_2026_kempsville_precinct_dashboard_7pm_polls_close_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Seven o'clock on the dot—polls are officially closed across the Commonwealth of Virginia," our sixty-eight-year-old dad said, setting five tall paper cups of ice-cold ginger ale down on the folding table beside my MagnaBook Pro inside the quiet green room behind the Virginia Beach convention ballroom while forty-one-year-old Brittany handed cups to forty-four-year-old Chelsea and me. "How fast is the State Board of Elections JSON endpoint pushing the early-vote abstracts tonight, Nate?"

"Refreshing every fifteen seconds, Dad," I replied, tapping a keyboard shortcut to refresh the custom PHP precinct-aggregation dashboard I had coded on my laptop while Katie rolled her compact silver-and-black SwiftChair power wheelchair flush against my left armrest and laced her fingers through mine.

Through the closed double doors at the end of the carpeted hallway, we could already hear six hundred supporters packing the ballroom floor—Coastal Virginia Transit Authority bus drivers still wearing their navy uniform jackets after finishing their afternoon shifts, community college students from CCHR, shipyard mechanics from Newport News, and nearly two dozen fellow wheelchair users who had ridden the evening bus pulse in from across Hampton Roads because they knew our campaign had picked a venue right on a regular bus line with a permanent, wide stage ramp.

Looking at the clean, color-coded precinct tables rendering on my laptop screen, Katie let out a quiet breath and rested her temple against my shoulder for two seconds.

Twenty-nine years had passed since the autumn of 1997, when Katie and I were twelve-year-old seventh graders at Morrison Middle School learning how to write raw HTML tags by hand in a plain text editor. Back then, watching his two autistic twelve-year-old twins stay up past supper building multi-page HTML websites from scratch had lit a fire under our dad—inspiring him to enroll at the Community College of Hampton Roads so he could earn his computer science degree and build a better life for the three of us. Four years later, during the summer and autumn of 2001—the year of our ill-fated Caribbean cruise on the *Sunward Tropics* and the September Friday when the borrowed time in our legs finally ran out—Katie and I had sat side by side reading Dad's mylar-wrapped *Quantum Basic 5.0* library textbooks cover to cover so we could learn *Quantum Basic 6.0* right alongside him before earning our Advanced Studies Diplomas from Virginia Beach City Public Schools at Northwood High School and our Associate of Applied Science degrees from the Community College of Hampton Roads. And in March 2007, after twelve unforgettable days at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville—where we rushed a two-week Vocational Evaluation in a single week after an evaluator actually suggested training us as ValueMart door greeters, got written up as Level 2 clients for locking ourselves inside a seasonal closet, and dropped out with our friend Tina after watching the administration sweep a classmate's assault under the rug—we had worked on the copied PHP and SQL modules unofficially from home for just a few days before deciding to do our own thing at our house off Military Highway in Kempsville, filming our twenty-three-year-old *Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit* four-bus mayoral loop in 2008, and building websites and desktop applications for the next nineteen years, buying our second Kempsville home on Lord Nelson Drive strictly fifty-fifty in October 2019 the moment CVTA launched the new Route 24 down Kempsville Road.

Every single line of code running our statewide U.S. Senate campaign's turnout model tonight had been written by the two of us at our own desks—without a single K Street data consultant or Washington party contractor touching our servers.

"First batch of Hampton Roads and Richmond early-vote precincts just dropped," I said at 7:18 PM as the green table rows populated down my screen. "Katie, look at Virginia Beach, Norfolk, and Chesapeake. You're outrunning the 2024 baseline by nine points in Kempsville and eleven points along the Military Highway Route 15 corridor."

"What about the Peninsula and the Shenandoah Valley?" Katie asked, leaning closer as her blue eyes tracked the numbers line by line.

"Newport News around Peninsula Commons Mall just came in at seventy-one percent for you, Williamsburg is running fourteen points ahead of projections, and Augusta County right around Fishersville and Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center is giving us the highest Democratic margin in twenty years," I said, my voice catching as the next wave of JSON packets streamed in from the New River Valley and the Alleghenies. "Look at Highland County up in Palo Alto—Valerie and the Cassini Center precinct just came in nineteen points above the 2024 baseline. And look at Montgomery County and Radford—the student, faculty, and Brooks family precincts around Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute and Radford University just reported. You're carrying Blacksburg and Christiansburg by thirty-eight points."

Dad adjusted his glasses, leaning over our shoulders as he turned up the volume on the center wall-mounted television monitor tuned to Global News Network, where GNN's signature Election Night data correspondent Stephen Kovacs—wearing his trademark rolled-up white shirtsleeves, khaki trousers, and striped tie—was pacing excitedly in front of the glowing interactive GNN Big Board.

"Hold on, look at what's happening down in Southwest Virginia along the Interstate 81 and Interstate 77 spine!" Stephen Kovacs said on the broadcast, tapping his fingers rapidly against the touchscreen of the GNN Big Board to zoom into the Ninth Congressional District. "At first glance on our county map, Carroll County down on the Blue Ridge plateau is shaded red. Wait a second—let's tap the municipal precinct overlay inside Carroll County and pull up the Town of Bluewater! Remember, folks, Bluewater is the largest incorporated town in the entire Commonwealth of Virginia because the voters in Bluewater have always refused to break away from Carroll County and become an independent city. If Bluewater were an independent city like Galax or Radford right up the highway, look at what you'd see on the Big Board right now: a bright blue circle right in the middle of Carroll County! State Senator Katie Prairie is winning the Town of Bluewater sixty-four percent to thirty-six percent—where she rode the Bluewater Transit Maroon Express bus right into town across from Bluewater High School—and she is cutting Richard Sterling's rural margins by twelve to eighteen points from Bluewater and Abingdon all the way out to Big Stone Gap!"

On the television screen, Kovacs swiped his hand east across the Blue Ridge escarpment along U.S. Route 58 into Southside Virginia, pulling up Martinsville, Henry County, Danville, and Pittsylvania County in the Fifth Congressional District.

"And slide right across Route 58 with me from Bluewater into Southside Virginia!" Kovacs exclaimed, circling Danville and Pittsylvania County in bright yellow on the touchscreen. "In any normal Virginia statewide race, a Republican nominee has to run up fifty-point margins in rural Pittsylvania and Henry Counties just to stay alive against Northern Virginia and Hampton Roads. Look at the numbers coming out of Southside tonight: Katie Prairie is carrying the Independent Cities of Danville and Martinsville by twenty-two points, and she is shaving nearly eleven points off Richard Sterling's Republican baseline across rural Pittsylvania and Henry Counties! Senator Prairie's own twenty-four-year-old legislative assistants Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins coined the name for this back in May—they call it 'the Uncle Richard Effect,' after their maternal uncle, longtime Southside delegate and retired Republican House Majority Leader Richard Callaway, a household name across Virginia politics who crossed party lines at a Danville bus shelter on live television to endorse Katie Prairie because she protected his three autistic kids when they were eighth-grade House Pages, hired all three of them onto her Senate staff, and championed the Piedmont Regional Transit bus link connecting Martinsville and Danville! When the former Republican House Majority Leader looks Southside voters in the eye and tells them Katie Prairie had his family's back for ten years, there is no Super PAC attack ad in Washington that can overcome that math!"

"Remember back in May when Senator Martin Warren's chief of staff Gregory told the newspapers that two software developers in wheelchairs riding public buses could never beat the party establishment's handpicked nominee Preston Holloway in the June primary?" Dad asked with a quiet, wry smile.

"They thought politics belonged to the people who wrote the biggest checks at Washington cocktail parties," Katie said softly, squeezing my hand across our wheelchair armrests. "They forgot that the people riding the bus vote too."

## Tuesday, November 3, 2026, 8:42 PM — The Decision Desk Call & Three Constitutional Amendments Pass

At twenty minutes past eight, the raw precinct feeds from Northern Virginia—Alexandria, Arlington, Fairfax, and Prince William County—flooded into our PHP database all at once.

I watched the statewide mathematical regression curve on my terminal window cross the ninety-nine-point-nine percent confidence threshold before any television anchor on the wall monitors even opened their mouth.

"Nate?" Katie whispered, seeing my fingers freeze over my mechanical keyboard.

I turned my head and looked straight into my forty-one-year-old twin sister's blue eyes.

"He can't catch you, Katie," I said softly, my throat tightening. "Even if he won seventy percent of every remaining uncounted precinct west of Interstate 81, our leads in Hampton Roads, Richmond, the CPI corridor, and Alexandria are mathematically insurmountable. You just won the United States Senate seat."

Ten seconds later, at 8:42 PM Eastern Standard Time, a sharp orchestral chime rang out simultaneously from all four television monitors on the green room wall.

On the center screen, the red-and-blue map of Virginia flashed bright gold as the banner across the bottom of the broadcast changed in bold white letters:

*GNN DECISION DESK — PROJECTED WINNER: STATE SEN. KATIE PRAIRIE (D-VIRGINIA) ELECTED TO THE UNITED STATES SENATE.*

Right beside it, the GBS Evening Report, Beacon News, and the Associated Wire flashed the exact same call within five seconds of one another.

Outside in the main ballroom, a roar went up so loud it rattled the framed pictures on the green room drywall—six hundred voices cheering, stomping, and chanting Katie's name while air horns and applause echoed down the corridor, right after Brittany gently slipped Chelsea's cushioned noise-dampening ear defenders up over her ears so the sudden thunder of celebration wouldn't startle her.

Inside our quiet room, Katie didn't look at the television screens first. She turned her wheelchair slightly toward me, threw both arms around my neck across our armrests, and buried her face against my shoulder while Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea wrapped their arms around the two of us in one tight, joyful family embrace.

"We did it, Nate," Katie whispered against my collar, her voice thick with tears. "From the Route 15 bus stop on Military Highway all the way to the United States Senate."

"Every single mile together, Senator," I whispered back, holding my twin sister tight while Dad kissed the top of her blonde hair and wiped his eyes behind his glasses.

When Katie sat back and wiped her cheeks with the back of her hand, she looked back at my MagnaBook Pro screen, where the three statewide ballot referendums and the local city question were finishing their final tallies.

"Tell me about the three Constitutional Amendments, Nate," Katie said immediately. "Did all three pass?"

"Every single one of them just crossed the projection threshold with statewide YES majorities," I told her, pointing to the three green confirmation banners at the bottom of our PHP dashboard. "Question 1—the Right to Reproductive Freedom Amendment—just passed by twelve points. Question 2—the Marriage Equality Amendment repealing the old 2006 ban and locking affirmative marriage rights into the Constitution of Virginia—passed by fifteen points. And Question 3—automatic Restoration of Voting Rights upon completion of a prison term—just passed by nine points. Everything you co-patroned and voted for across the 2025 and 2026 General Assembly sessions in Richmond is officially part of the Constitution of the Commonwealth of Virginia."

Katie closed her eyes for a heartbeat, nodding with deep, quiet satisfaction. "Then every single oath I ever swore on the Constitution of Virginia was worth it."

"Wait—what's that red banner on the local municipal tab at the bottom?" Dad asked, pointing toward the lower right corner of my screen.

I scrolled down two inches to the local municipal referendum tally—the one-percent local retail sales tax dedicated strictly to public school construction and major facility modernization, which the General Assembly had authorized cities and counties to put before their voters tonight. Remembering the aging classrooms and accessibility hurdles at Northwood High School when our legs gave out in September 2001, Katie and I had both campaigned hard for a YES vote and marked YES on our own ballots in Kempsville.

"The local one-percent school construction sales tax referendum failed," I said quietly, reading the certified precinct totals across the city. "The NO vote carried fifty-four to forty-six. Between grocery inflation and utility bills, working families just didn't have the stomach for another one-percent sales tax at the cash register."

Katie stared at the red NO tally for a long moment, her jaw setting with that familiar, unshakeable resolve I had known since we were children.

"I respect the voters' decision—working families are stretched thin right now, and nobody likes paying higher sales tax," Katie said evenly. "But the roofs on our public schools still leak, and disabled kids in wheelchairs still get stuck behind broken school elevators. If local taxpayers can't shoulder that burden through a sales tax, then my job in the United States Senate starting in January is to bring federal school infrastructure and IDEA modernization grants straight home to Virginia."

## Tuesday, November 3, 2026, 9:15 PM — The Ramp to the Podium & A Toast With Five Cups of Ginger Ale

At five minutes past nine, Katie's smartphone vibrated on the table beside her cup of ginger ale.

It was Republican nominee Richard Sterling calling from his hotel suite in Henrico County to concede the race. The call lasted less than ninety seconds—stiff, formal, and clipped—and when Katie thanked him politely and tapped the red end-call button, the ballroom coordinator knocked on our door with a wide grin.

"Senator-Elect Prairie?" the coordinator beamed. "The crowd out there hasn't stopped chanting for thirty minutes. Are all of you ready?"

"Every one of us," Katie said, checking that the small gold Seal of Virginia pin was straight on her royal-blue blazer lapel before reaching her left hand across to take mine while Brittany checked that Chelsea's ear defenders were snug and comfortable for the ballroom applause.

As the double doors swung open, the driving synthesizer pulse and soaring eighties rock chorus of our official campaign theme song—"The Accessibility Project" by The Paper Wall—blasted across the convention ballroom sound system: *We're building a map, we're sharing the key! Drawing the grid, unlocking the door, we're leveling the playing field, settling the score!* Beneath the sweeping spotlight beams at the base of the wide, gently sloped aluminum stage ramp, Katie and I nudged our right thumbs forward on our SwiftChair joysticks and rolled side by side up onto the ballroom stage with Dad walking proudly on his own two feet right beside Katie's chair while Brittany and Chelsea waved happily from our reserved quiet family bay at the top of the ramp.

![Forty-one-year-old U.S. Senator-Elect Katie Prairie and her fraternal twin brother Nate hold hands across their compact SwiftChair power wheelchairs on a ballroom stage in Virginia Beach with their proud father David standing on his own two feet beside them beneath falling confetti and the GNN Decision Desk projection screen]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/nov_2026_election_night_decision_desk_victory_katie_nate_david.jpg)

Above our heads, a shower of blue and gold paper confetti drifted down under the stage lights while the giant projection screen behind us blazed with the GNN Decision Desk banner proclaiming Katie Prairie the newest United States Senator from Virginia.

Looking out across the cheering sea of faces—seeing the CVTA bus operators waving their caps in the front row, the CCHR students holding handmade signs, and the kids in power wheelchairs lined up along the accessible front aisle looking up at a United States Senator whose chair looked just like theirs—Katie squeezed my hand tightly in front of the cameras before rolling two feet forward to the lowered podium microphone.

"Eighteen years ago, my twin brother Nate and I sat at the bus loop at the Community College of Hampton Roads waiting to see if a single candidate for local office would ride four buses across Virginia Beach with us," Katie's voice rang out clear and steady across the ballroom speakers. "We learned that day that if working people, transit riders, and disabled Virginians couldn't even catch a bus home from a six o'clock City Council meeting before the last Route 12 left CCHR at 5:48 PM, nobody was going to hand us a seat at the table—we were going to have to roll up the ramp and build that table ourselves! Tonight, you proved that the Commonwealth of Virginia doesn't belong to billionaire donors or Washington party gatekeepers. You enshrined reproductive freedom, marriage equality, and voting rights directly into the Constitution of Virginia, and on January third, Nate, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and I are taking the morning train up from Norfolk to bring your voices straight to the floor of the United States Senate!"

An hour later, after shaking every hand along the front Rope Line without a single drop of champagne anywhere near our stage, the five of us sat together in the quiet green room while the crew coiled the microphone cables outside.

Raising our five paper cups of ice-cold ginger ale—the exact same toast Katie and I had kept since New Year's Eve when we were seventeen years old, compatible with the daily maintenance medication that kept Katie, Dad, and me alive and shared wholeheartedly by Brittany and Chelsea—all five of us tapped our rims together in the center of the room.

"To the Senator from Virginia," Dad said softly.

"To our whole family," Katie replied, smiling across at Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and me. "Now let's go home to Kempsville and get some sleep—because tomorrow morning the real work starts."
