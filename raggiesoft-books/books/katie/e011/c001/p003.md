## Monday, November 16, 2026, 8:45 AM — Rolling Past the Chartered VIP Motorcoach & Retiring Senator Martin Warren's Stare

![Arriving on Capitol Hill via the Capitol South Capital Transit elevator in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs with their father David walking beside them past the Blue, Orange, and Silver Line station pylon, 41-year-old U.S. Senator-Elect Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate roll past the chartered VIP motorcoaches toward the Russell and Dirksen Senate Office Buildings for New Member Orientation.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_capitol_south_elevator_plaza_arrival_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Hold the charter bus door—is Senator-Elect Prairie on this shuttle from the hotel?" a Senate Sergeant at Arms protocol officer called into his lapel radio outside the bronze doors of the Russell Senate Office Building on Monday morning as two black-and-silver Capitol Police escort vans idled at the curb along Constitution Avenue.

"Right behind you on the sidewalk, actually," Katie called out cheerfully as she and I steered our two compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs down the concrete curb cut from First Street Southeast—right past the brown Capitol South station pylon striped with the Blue, Orange, and Silver lines—with our sixty-eight-year-old dad walking beside us carrying his leather briefcase.

The protocol officer spun around on the granite steps, blinking in disbelief as he looked from the chartered luxury motorcoach—where the other eleven members of the incoming freshman Senate class were stepping down the stairs in tailored overcoats—to the two forty-one-year-old twins who had just rolled up the public sidewalk from the Capitol South station elevator with CapitalPass transit cards tucked into their jacket pockets.

"You... you took the subway to New Member Orientation, Senator-Elect?" the officer stammered.

"The five o'clock morning ARTS train up from Norfolk, the mobile lift off the train at Alexandria so we could drop our suitcases at our dad's house on Sunset Drive, the Capital Transit Yellow Line across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge to L'Enfant Plaza, and the Blue Line two stops to Capitol South," Katie smiled, holding up her blue plastic fare card before reaching across our armrests to squeeze my hand. "Why sit in downtown traffic on a charter bus when the train drops you off a block from the front door?"

Ten minutes later, when the three of us rolled through the tall double doors of the historic Kennedy Caucus Room on the third floor of the Russell Building—under the crystal chandeliers and towering Corinthian columns where the Senate Democratic Caucus was gathering for its post-election orientation breakfast—the hum of conversation near the coffee urns went noticeably quiet.

Standing near the center aisle with a saucer in his hand was retiring three-term United States Senator Martin Warren of Virginia, flanked by his departing chief of staff, Gregory, and two senior consultants from the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee.

Katie hadn't flipped a Republican seat in November; both of Virginia's United States Senate seats were already held by Democrats—Senior Senator Thomas Keane and retiring Senator Martin Warren. Back in the winter, when Senator Warren had announced he was retiring after eighteen years in Washington, he and the big-money K Street establishment had tried to stage a coronation. They had handpicked Preston Holloway—a polished McLean venture capitalist businessman with a contact list full of billionaire donors—to take over Warren's seat, pouring fourteen million dollars of Super PAC money into the June 16 Democratic primary while Gregory told the Washington newspapers that two autistic software developers in wheelchairs riding public transit buses were a "cute regional novelty" and a hopeless long shot who wouldn't break twenty percent statewide.

Instead, the voters of Virginia had blown their coronation to smithereens. Riding regular public buses across all ninety-five counties and thirty-eight independent cities without taking a dime of corporate PAC money, Katie had crushed Senator Warren's handpicked corporate stooge by eleven points in the June primary and then defeated Republican nominee Richard Sterling by nine points in the November general election—while across the rest of the map, Democrats had flipped seven Republican seats to capture a fifty-four-to-forty-six Senate majority for the incoming One Hundred Twentieth Congress.

And when the National Firearms Association—the massive gun lobby headquartered right up the road in Fairfax—had plastered television airwaves and mailboxes across the Commonwealth with glossy red-and-black attack mailers branding her *"F-Rated Radical Katie Prairie"* because she unapologetically called the MR-15 semiautomatic rifle a weapon of war and championed common-sense gun safety legislation, Katie had laughed out loud at our kitchen table, asked me to put their postcard in a black wooden frame on our transition office wall, and worn that F grade from the National Firearms Association on every debate stage like a badge of honor. She had never once hedged or hidden who she was: she ran unabashedly for universal healthcare so no family would ever face bankruptcy over a spinal cord injury or a chronic illness, she stood up fiercely for transgender rights and bodily autonomy against Restore the Republic smear campaigns claiming she wanted "men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms," and whenever anyone asked her about reproductive freedom, she looked straight into the camera and said in plain, unmistakable words that abortion was a private medical decision between a woman and her doctor—and that no politician, judge, or bureaucrat in Washington belonged in that exam room.

Before Senator Warren or his red-faced chief of staff could decide whether to look at the ceiling or walk over, our other Senator from Virginia, Thomas Keane, broke away from the front table with a wide, genuine grin and walked straight down the carpet to meet our wheelchairs.

"Make way for my new partner from the Commonwealth of Virginia!" Senator Keane beamed, shaking Katie's hand warmly before shaking mine and Dad's. "Katie, Nate, David—welcome to the United States Senate. Did you see the final certified turnout numbers out of Hampton Roads, the Shenandoah Valley, and Montgomery County? You two put together the biggest grassroots margin in Virginia in twelve years."

A half-beat later, retiring Senator Martin Warren stepped over, clearing his throat as he offered Katie a stiff, polite handshake while Gregory stared fixedly at the carpet behind him.

"Congratulations on a well-run campaign, Katie," Senator Warren said, trying to sound magnanimous. "My staff and I have prepared a transition binder of our donor and stakeholder contacts so your office can maintain continuity—"

"Thank you, Senator Warren, that won't be necessary," Katie said politely, her blue eyes calm and crystal clear as she looked up from her SwiftChair. "The transit riders, shipyard welders, teachers, and disabled Virginians who nominated me in June and elected me in November already gave my brother and me all the stakeholder contacts we need. Enjoy your retirement."

Beside me, Dad had to disguise a grin behind a cough as Gregory's ears turned crimson and Senator Warren nodded stiffly before retreating toward the buffet table.

Moments later, as Senate Democratic Leader Charles Schuman of New York called the new Democratic Senators-Elect together at the center of the ornate Oriental rug for the official freshman class photograph, another wheelchair rolled smoothly out of the circle of tailored suits to meet us.

Sitting in an ultralight black ApexLite manual wheelchair—dressed in a navy suit, white shirt, red tie, and brown dress shoes, with a neatly trimmed beard and the powerful shoulders of an athlete—was forty-seven-year-old Senator-Elect Joshua "Josh" Novak of Iowa. Born with spina bifida, Josh was a two-time Paralympic gold medalist in wheelchair basketball and a former Iowa State Representative from Council Bluffs who had just stunned the national pundits on Tuesday night by flipping Iowa's open Republican Senate seat after scaling thousands of steep front-porch steps on his hands and backside to knock on doors across all ninety-nine Iowa counties.

"Senator-Elect Prairie—and you must be Nate and David," Josh beamed, braking his manual chair beside our two motorized SwiftChairs and shaking hands with all three of us. "I've been watching your bus campaign across Virginia all autumn from out in Iowa. Do you realize the two of us just made Senate history before we even got sworn in? Not only are there two wheelchair users in the incoming Democratic freshman class at the exact same time, we're about to triple the Senate's wheelchair caucus!"

"From coastal city buses in Virginia to front porches in Iowa," Katie grinned back at him as we positioned our chairs side by side at the front center of the incoming Democratic class for the photograph.

## Monday, November 16, 2026, 11:15 AM — Senator Tess Dunmore of Illinois, Senator-Elect Josh Novak of Iowa, & Why the Senate Ramp Is Already Waiting

At quarter past eleven, while the lame-duck One Hundred Nineteenth Senate stood in morning recess, the Deputy Sergeant at Arms and two preservation architects from the Office of the Architect of the Capitol met Katie, Josh Novak, and me outside the double doors of the United States Senate Chamber on the second floor of the Capitol.

Out of pure habit from fifteen years in Virginia politics, both Katie and I had arrived ready for an architectural battle. In 2010, after Katie won her seat on the Virginia Beach City Council, we had had to fight city facilities managers to modify the Municipal Center dais. In January 2014, when Katie arrived in the Virginia House of Delegates, I had sat beside her with a twenty-five-foot steel tape measure forcing Capitol Facilities to fix non-compliant fifty-one-inch restroom stalls. And in December 2019, when she was elected to the Senate of Virginia, the master woodworkers in Richmond had had to build the chamber's very first custom-milled mahogany wheelchair ramp from scratch because no wheelchair user had ever served in the upper house of the General Assembly.

As the brass-handled doors of the United States Senate Chamber swung open onto the blue carpet, however, a familiar voice called out from the center aisle:

"Put the steel tape measure away, Nate—none of you have to fight the building engineers from scratch this time!"

Rolling briskly up the blue carpet toward us in a sleek, ultralight black titanium wheelchair was forty-eight-year-old Senator Teresa "Tess" Dunmore of Illinois—the decorated former Army helicopter pilot who had lost both of her legs when her aircraft was hit by a rocket-propelled grenade in Iraq in 2004, and who had served in the United States Senate since January of 2017.

Katie's face lit up in an instant as Senator Dunmore braked her titanium chair right in front of our two motorized SwiftChairs and Josh's manual ApexLite chair, reaching out to clasp Katie's hand, Josh's hand, and mine.

"Senator Dunmore," Katie said warmly.

"It's Tess—we're colleagues now, Katie, Josh," Senator Dunmore grinned, looking back and forth across our wheelchairs with fierce camaraderie. "Do you have any idea how long I've been waiting for reinforcements on wheels in this chamber? For the last ten years I've been a one-woman wheelchair caucus on our side of the aisle. Now, with Virginia and Iowa showing up in the same freshman class, we've got a full fleet!"

For a full two seconds, neither Katie nor I spoke. Looking down the semicircular tiers of the one hundred historic mahogany Senate desks toward the marble rostrum at the front of the chamber, I realized that for the very first time in my sister's public life, she wasn't the only wheelchair user in the room—in fact, between Tess Dunmore, Josh Novak, and Katie, three of the fifty-four Democratic Senators in the One Hundred Twentieth Congress would be rolling onto the Senate floor in wheelchairs.

"Come on down the aisle—let me show you both what we already built, and what the cabinetmakers are customizing for each of your desks before January third," Tess said, wheeling alongside Katie, Josh, and me down the Democratic side of the chamber.

At the base of the front tiers, Tess pointed to the permanent, flush-mounted carpeted wheelchair ramp leading smoothly down into the well of the Senate, and then to the custom platform lift and ramp integrated into the white marble steps of the Presiding Officer's rostrum.

"When I got sworn in back in 2017—following in the tire tracks of veterans like Dan Inouye, Bob Dole, and Max Cleland—we made sure the well of the Senate and the Presiding Officer's rostrum were permanently accessible under the Congressional Accountability Act," Tess explained. "What the Senate cabinetmakers had to figure out this week is the difference between manual titanium frames like mine and Josh's and your two motorized SwiftChairs."

She led us to the back-row aisle desks on the outer curve of the Democratic side, where two master Senate carpenters were kneeling with brass calipers and blocks of seasoned Honduras mahogany.

"Because the hundred desks on the Senate floor date back to 1819 and have bolted-down swivel chairs," I noted, leaning over my left armrest to inspect the joinery, "for Senator-Elect Novak's desk two aisles over—just like Senator Dunmore's desk—unbolting the fixed chair pedestal gives his manual wheelchair plenty of clearance. And on Katie's desk here, they unbolted the chair pedestal and milled one-and-a-half-inch solid mahogany riser blocks under the bottom of the desk legs so the upright joystick controller on Katie's right armrest glides all the way under the writing surface without bumping the drawer apron."

"You nailed it on the first look, Nate," the lead carpenter smiled, standing up as Katie nudged her right thumb forward on her joystick and rolled her SwiftChair effortlessly beneath the historic mahogany desktop, testing the clearance with an inch and a half to spare. "And right behind Senator Prairie's desk along the back leather staff bench, we've widened the staff bay by thirty-eight inches. Since Mr. Prairie holds permanent Chief Data Strategist floor privileges under your office allotment, his SwiftChair can park right behind the Senator's left shoulder during floor debate without blocking the chamber door. We're making the exact same dual-powerchair modification to the witness and staff bays in Dirksen Room 124 for the Appropriations Committee."

Then Tess Dunmore gestured toward the high white marble rostrum at the front of the chamber, a wicked gleam in her dark eyes.

"Now come up the rostrum ramp with me for thirty seconds, Katie," Tess said. "There's one more perk of our new fifty-four-seat Democratic majority you need to test-drive before the Vice President shows up this afternoon."

Katie and I followed Tess up the accessible rostrum ramp to the highest tier of the Senate dais, right behind the Presiding Officer's carved marble desk and the heavy ivory gavel block.

"Under Senate custom," Tess said, patting the leather arm of the Presiding Officer's space, "the Vice President of the United States almost never sits in this chair except on Opening Day or when the Senate is tied fifty-fifty. Instead, the freshman Senators of the majority party rotate in one-hour shifts every legislative day to preside over the Senate as Acting President pro tempore. And do you know what every single Senator on the floor—Democrat or Republican—is required by Senate Rule XIX to call you when you are sitting at this desk with that ivory gavel in your hand?"

Katie looked out across the hundred mahogany desks below us, a slow, electric smile spreading across her face.

"Madam President," Katie said softly.

"Madam President," Tess laughed aloud. "Every single Republican who cheered when Garrett Thorne stood on the South Lawn calling you 'Broken Katie Prairie' and 'a bitter little cripple on wheels' is going to have to stand at attention at their desk, look up at your SwiftChair at this rostrum, and say, *'Madam President, will the Chair recognize the Senator?'* before they're even allowed to speak!"

## Tuesday, November 17, 2026, 2:00 PM — The Dirksen Room 511 Suite Lottery & Committee Assignments

On Tuesday afternoon, the Office of the Senate Sergeant at Arms convened the Freshman Office Suite Lottery in the Capitol Visitor Center congressional meeting rooms.

Under the rules of the United States Senate, newly elected Senators taking office on the same day are ranked in freshman seniority by prior federal service, prior service as a state governor, and then by the population of their state in the decennial census paired with prior state legislative service. With Virginia's population of nearly nine million people and Katie's twelve combined years in the Virginia House of Delegates and the Senate of Virginia, Katie drew the second pick in the entire twelve-member freshman class.

While the other incoming Senators fought over the high-ceilinged, chandeliered corner suites in the 1909 Russell Senate Office Building, Katie and I didn't even look at the Russell list. We had already audited the Sergeant at Arms CAD floor plans on my MagnaBook Pro the night before.

"Russell was built in 1909 with heavy brass-sill double doors, narrow interior staff hallways, and long detours to the accessible elevators," I said, pointing to our annotated blueprint as the Superintendent of the Senate Office Buildings leaned over our table. "Look at the fifth floor of the Dirksen Senate Office Building—specifically Suite 511."

"Dirksen Room 511 has forty-eight-inch interior doorways, flush marble-to-carpet thresholds, a wide reception corridor where two SwiftChairs can roll side by side in lockstep without turning sideways, and a private Senator's office large enough to place two custom-height mahogany desks side by side so Nate's workstation sits two feet from my left armrest," Katie told the Superintendent. "Plus, the elevator bank right outside Room 511 drops straight down to the first-floor Appropriations Subcommittee hearing rooms in Dirksen 124 and 138 and the subterranean subway to the Capitol. We'll take Dirksen Room 511."

An hour later, when the Democratic Steering and Outreach Committee met with Katie and Senior Senator Thomas Keane to finalize committee slates for the One Hundred Twentieth Congress, the leadership didn't repeat Senator Martin Warren's mistake of underestimating her. With Naval Station Norfolk, Newport News Shipbuilding, and the Port of Virginia anchoring her home base—and Appropriations Chairman Arthur Harrington personally requesting her on his committee—Katie rolled out of the Steering meeting with three powerhouse assignments: the Senate Committee on Appropriations (including seats on the Labor, Health and Human Services, Education, and Related Agencies Subcommittee and the Transportation, Housing and Urban Development Subcommittee), the Senate Committee on Banking, Housing, and Urban Affairs (which held one hundred percent Senate jurisdiction over the Federal Transit Administration and every public transit system in America), and the Senate Committee on Commerce, Science, and Transportation.

Because Katie had defeated retiring Senator Martin Warren's handpicked corporate successor in the June primary rather than inheriting a party machine, we weren't taking over a pre-packaged staff operation—we were building Senator Katie Prairie's personal office from the ground up. Following the model Senior Senator Thomas Keane used across the Commonwealth, Katie and I structured her incoming operation into two coordinated halves: a Washington legislative, administrative, scheduling, and communications staff in Dirksen Room 511, and a Virginia constituent-services operation staffed by caseworkers and geographically assigned regional directors across Hampton Roads, Richmond, the Shenandoah and New River Valleys, Northern Virginia, and a dedicated Southside and Eastern Shore Regional Director.

And before our transition team opened a single resume from Capitol Hill, Katie wrote three names at the very top of her Washington staffing chart:

"Sam, Mandy, and Sarah are coming with me."

In the United States Senate, unlike the Senate Page Program—where sixteen- and seventeen-year-old Pages are sponsored by Senators and employed by the Sergeant at Arms—every Senator's personal office is an independent equal-opportunity employer that sets its own positions, responsibilities, and professional salaries. At twenty-four years old, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah weren't children anymore, and Katie would never insult their intelligence or their autism by treating them as office mascots or handing them three generic, interchangeable make-work titles. Because Katie and I were autistic ourselves, we understood something that neurotypical managers almost always got backward: for Sam, Mandy, and Sarah, successful adulthood meant interdependence, not forced separation. They didn't need to be chained within a six-foot radius of one another every minute of the afternoon if Sam had to walk downstairs to a committee hearing room while Mandy stayed on a constituent phone call, yet they regulated and felt secure knowing they were based together in Dirksen Room 511, had workstations near one another, commuted together on Capital Transit, ate lunch together whenever they wanted, and would never have a supervisor arbitrarily split them across different cities to "teach them a lesson."

Even more importantly, during their years working for Katie in Richmond while earning their degrees at Central Virginia University, the three triplets had developed completely distinct professional strengths—inseparable as people, not interchangeable as professionals:

For twenty-four-year-old Sam Hawkins—who had turned into an unapologetic policy gremlin capable of happily reading a three-hundred-and-twelve-page Federal Transit Administration authorization bill at eleven o'clock at night—Katie created a full-time position as Legislative Correspondent covering Transportation, Infrastructure, Disability and Accessibility Policy, and Rural Development at a starting salary of sixty-two thousand dollars, bringing him full circle from the thirteen-year-old eighth-grade Page who had carried paper bills around the Virginia House floor in 2016 to a congressional staffer drafting federal transit legislation for a United States Senator.

For twenty-four-year-old Mandy Hawkins—who had discovered in Richmond that she loved the human-facing detective work of helping everyday Virginians who were stuck inside federal and state bureaucracies—Katie offered a sixty-six-thousand-dollar position as Constituent Services Representative and Federal Caseworker, designing a crystal-clear, predictable six-step casework protocol that suited Mandy's methodical mind down to the last checkbox: case received, Privacy Act consent paperwork, federal agency liaison contact, scheduled follow-up, constituent update, and resolution and closure.

And for twenty-four-year-old Sarah Hawkins—who had started at the front reception console handling phone lines, Capitol tour requests, visitor greeting, and mail triage before revealing a razor-sharp talent for digital accessibility and social-media graphics—Katie offered a fifty-eight-thousand-dollar position as Staff Assistant and Digital Accessibility Correspondent on a direct track into her press and legislative communications operation alongside me.

When Katie called the triplets from our transition room that Tuesday afternoon to offer all three of them the jobs in Washington, there was a ten-second stunned silence on the line before Mandy said earnestly, "Senator Katie, we want to come with you more than anything—we just told Uncle Richard and Aunt Marge we would talk to them first before we signed anything."

As Sam told us laughing the next morning, when the three of them walked into the kitchen in Danville that evening and said, *"Uncle Richard, Katie offered us jobs in Washington,"* their sixty-six-year-old uncle set his teacup down on the counter and looked at all three of them.

*"All three of you?"* Richard asked.

*"All three,"* Sam told him, listing their three distinct positions and salaries in Dirksen Room 511.

*"Well, of course she does,"* Richard said warmly. *"What did you tell her?"*

Mandy blinked. *"We said we needed to talk to you and Aunt Marge first."*

Richard stared at his twenty-four-year-old nephew and nieces for three full seconds.

*"You're twenty-four years old,"* the former House Majority Leader said softly. *"Go call your senator."*

Because Uncle Richard was their maternal uncle—their mother's brother—and the triplets carried the surname Hawkins rather than Callaway, the Washington reporters scanning Senator-Elect Prairie's initial federal transition payroll filings for Sam Hawkins, Amanda Hawkins, and Sarah Hawkins didn't immediately realize who the three twenty-four-year-old hires from Danville were related to. Forty-eight hours later, however, when a veteran Capitol beat reporter at the Richmond newspaper recognized the three names from their years in the General Assembly and cross-referenced the filing, the headline ricocheted across every newsroom in the Commonwealth: *FORMER VIRGINIA GOP MAJORITY LEADER RICHARD CALLAWAY ENCOURAGES NEPHEW AND NIECES TO JOIN DEMOCRATIC SENATOR'S WASHINGTON STAFF.*

By noon, television cameras were back in Uncle Richard's front yard in Southside Virginia.

"Leader Callaway," a reporter asked, holding a microphone over the porch railing, "does it bother you that your nephew and nieces will be working in Washington for a Democratic United States Senator?"

"Which Democrat?" Uncle Richard asked dryly.

"Senator-Elect Prairie."

"Katie? Lord, no," Richard chuckled, before his expression grew steady and serious. "I spent twenty-four years in the House of Delegates. I know that a newly elected United States Senator doesn't have unlimited payroll slots to hand out as sentimental favors, I know something about legislative offices, and I know something about Katie Prairie. Eleven years ago, when Sam, Mandy, and Sarah were thirteen years old and had only been living safely with Marge and me for a year, I sent them up to Richmond as House Pages worrying about them every single hour of the day—and Katie Prairie and her brother Nate looked after those kids when nobody asked them to. So when those three came home on Tuesday night and told Marge and me that Katie wanted them on her United States Senate staff, I told them to take the jobs. They're adults. They're not Republicans because I'm a Republican, and they don't owe my political party anything. But if you're asking whether I'm comfortable with Sam, Mandy, and Sarah working for Katie Prairie? I couldn't tell you another Senator in America I'd trust more with those three."

Right beside the local reporters, a correspondent holding a Liberty First Network microphone pushed forward, repeating the talking point that Restore the Republic commentators had been screaming all morning:

"Mr. Majority Leader, Restore the Republic state chapters are calling you 'RINO Richard' and 'an Enemy of the Republic,' and they're asking how a lifelong conservative could be sending his own autistic nephew and nieces to Washington after they were manipulated by 'Broken Katie Prairie'!"

At the word *RINO*, Uncle Richard let out a short, dry laugh.

"Twenty-four years in the Virginia House of Delegates. Republican Majority Leader. And now I'm apparently a Republican In Name Only," Richard said wryly. "That's an awfully long time to maintain the disguise."

Then his smile vanished, and the iron voice that had commanded the floor of the Virginia House for a decade rolled across his front porch as he locked eyes with the Liberty First Network correspondent.

"Sending them?" Uncle Richard said, his voice dropping an octave as every trace of diplomacy disappeared. "Let me get something straight right now. My nephew and nieces are twenty-four years old. Katie Prairie offered them jobs in the United States Senate. They wanted those jobs. They asked Marge and me what we thought, and I told them to take the jobs. Nobody stole my kids. Nobody brainwashed my kids. Nobody sent my kids anywhere."

He took one step closer to the porch railing, pointing a finger squarely at the Liberty First Network mic flag.

"And as for this 'Broken Katie Prairie' garbage?" Uncle Richard unloaded, his voice ringing like a gavel strike across the quiet Southside street. "Let me tell you something about your 'Broken Katie Prairie.' I knew Katie when my three kids were practically children. When my nephew and nieces were thirteen years old, that woman recognized when they were overwhelmed before half the adults in the Capitol did. She gave them instructions they could understand. She protected them when the House got too loud. She treated them with patience and dignity, and she never once screamed at my kids. If that's what Restore the Republic calls broken, then perhaps somebody ought to take a long look at what Restore the Republic calls whole. And if you think autism makes Katie Prairie broken, you're also talking about my nephew and my nieces—so choose your next words very carefully. You people didn't inherit my Republican Party merely because you inherited its name. If my party thinks three adults taking good jobs working for the Commonwealth is a scandal, my party knows where to find me."

That night—as Aunt Marge told Katie over the phone while laughing so hard she could barely catch her breath—Uncle Richard sat back in his recliner watching the eleven o'clock news replay his interview, rubbed his forehead, and muttered:

*"Twenty-four years as a Republican in the House of Delegates. Republican Majority Leader. And somehow all three of my kids wound up working for the Democratic Senator from Virginia Beach."*

*"You introduced them to Richmond,"* Marge reminded him sweetly from the sofa.

Richard let out a long sigh. *"...I suppose this is my fault."*

## Wednesday, November 18, 2026, 4:30 PM — Face-to-Face With Vice President Conrad Drayton in the President's Room

The final event of New Member Orientation took place at four-thirty on Wednesday afternoon inside Room S-216 of the United States Capitol—the ornate, frescoed President's Room just off the Senate lobby—where Vice President Conrad Drayton, in his constitutional capacity as President of the Senate, was hosting the traditional ceremonial welcome reception for the Senators-Elect and their families.

To say that Katie hated Vice President Conrad Drayton—and his boss in the Oval Office, President Garrett Thorne—would have been an understatement.

For the past nine months, ever since it became clear that Katie was going to win the Virginia Senate seat, President Thorne had used every rally stage and South Lawn helicopter gaggle to mock my twin sister's disability—calling her *"Broken Katie Prairie,"* mimicking the way her hands rested on her wheelchair joystick, and jeering that *"a forty-one-year-old spinster on wheels who still lives with her crippled twin brother and rides city buses has no stamina for the United States Senate."* And Vice President Conrad Drayton had been even slimier: going on *Patriot Watch* on the Liberty First Network five nights a week to sneer with smug, sanctimonious condescension that because Katie and I were unmarried, childless, secular twins who relied on each other instead of a "traditional nuclear marriage," we had *"no moral or spiritual stake in the future of the American family."*

Now, beneath the gilded Renaissance-revival ceiling frescoes and crystal chandelier of the President's Room, Conrad Drayton stood in a charcoal suit with a forced, oily television smile plastered across his face, shaking hands with the incoming Senators one by one in front of the official Senate photographer.

When Katie and I rolled our two compact SwiftChairs side by side through the arched doorway with Dad walking right behind us, the chatter in the small room dropped to a hush.

"Well, well—look who made it all the way up from Virginia Beach," Vice President Drayton said smoothly, stepping across the patterned tile floor toward Katie's wheelchair with his right hand extended and his left hand reaching out automatically toward the rear push-handle bar behind her headrest to angle her chair toward the camera lens. "President Thorne and I were taking bets on whether you'd actually roll all the way up Interstate 95, Senator-Elect Prairie."

Katie nudged her joystick half an inch in reverse and pivoted twenty degrees to the left—just enough that Conrad Drayton's left hand closed on empty air six inches from her chair.

"First of all, Mr. Vice President, nobody touches my wheelchair or my brother's wheelchair without our permission," Katie said, her voice calm, crisp, and loud enough that every Senator-Elect in the room heard every syllable. "Second, we didn't drive up Interstate 95—we took the morning train from Norfolk and rode the Capital Transit Yellow Line across the river, which you'd know if anyone in your administration ever set foot on public transportation."

Conrad Drayton's jaw tightened, a flash of ugly crimson rising above his starched white collar as he pulled his hand back.

"I'm sure you'll find Washington very different from the Virginia Beach bus stops, Senator-Elect," Drayton said coldly, trying to recover his smirk in front of the photographer. "Enjoy the reception—I'll see you on January third when I administer the oath. I assume my protocol clerk can put you down for the standard King James Bible?"

Katie didn't flinch. Sitting tall in her royal-blue blazer between Dad and me, she looked straight up into the eyes of the man she despised and smiled—a bright, razor-sharp smile of pure constitutional triumph.

"Actually, Mr. Vice President, my transition counsel filed my formal oath notice with the Secretary of the Senate and your protocol office at nine o'clock this morning," Katie said pleasantly. "Pursuant to Article VI, Clause 3 of the Constitution of the United States and Title 5, Section 3331 of the United States Code, I am not using a Bible, and I am not saying the words 'So help me God.' I am taking the solemn secular affirmation of office—*'Do you solemnly affirm'*—with my left hand resting on a bound copy of the Constitution of the United States of America."

For a full second, the polished television mask slipped right off Conrad Drayton's face, replaced by a look of pure, seething hatred.

More than anything else, Drayton hated that she was swearing in on the Constitution of the United States. His entire political brand on the Liberty First Network depended on wrapping himself in sanctimonious religious theater and accusing anyone who disagreed with him of hating America—and by placing her left palm squarely on the United States Constitution under Article VI, Clause 3 while forcing him, as President of the Senate, to read the secular affirmation card aloud without "So help me God," Katie was turning him into an involuntary participant in her secular oath in front of every camera in the country.

"You really think thumbing your nose at the Holy Bible is going to play well outside your little progressive bubble, Senator-Elect?" Drayton hissed, his voice dropping low and venomous. "Between refusing to touch a Bible, getting an F grade from the National Firearms Association down in Fairfax for calling the MR-15 a weapon of war, pushing government universal healthcare, wanting men in women's sports and men in women's restrooms, and pushing unrestricted abortion, you're going to have every church and gun owner in the country marching outside your office."

"Oh, I'm counting on it—in fact, I wear every single bit of their outrage, and that F grade from the National Firearms Association, as a badge of honor," Katie replied without missing a beat, her voice ringing clear and unbothered across the room. "An MR-15 is a weapon of war designed for a battlefield, not a civilian neighborhood, and if calling an MR-15 a weapon of war and passing common-sense gun safety legislation—universal background checks, red-flag laws, safe storage, and keeping weapons of war out of Virginia classrooms and grocery stores so children come home alive from school—is why the gun lobby in Fairfax gave me an F, then I'll frame that F and hang it right on the door of Dirksen Room 511. Healthcare should be a universal human right for every person in this country, transgender Americans deserve the exact same dignity and bodily autonomy as everyone else without your Restore the Republic talking points bullying kids or trying to station government bathroom police outside public restrooms like your party tried and failed to do in Virginia back in 2016, and abortion is a private medical decision between a woman and her doctor—where neither you, nor President Thorne, nor any senator belongs in the exam room."

She tilted her chin up from her SwiftChair, her blue eyes gleaming as she delivered the final twist of the knife.

"And every time the televangelists at *The 777 Circle* down on Indian River Road in Virginia Beach tried to attack me in 2014 and 2020 for affirming my oath on the Constitution instead of a Bible, we won re-election by an even bigger margin, because ordinary voters understand that a Senator is elected to defend the Constitution, not run a church," Katie said. "Do you know what the very best part of winning this election is, Mr. Vice President?"

Katie leaned an inch forward in her SwiftChair, her voice dropping to a clear, surgical purr that made three neighboring Senators-Elect bite the insides of their cheeks to keep from laughing aloud.

"For the last nine months, your boss has gone on television calling me 'Broken Katie Prairie,' and for the last two weeks since Election Night he's been screaming on cable news that a woman in a wheelchair couldn't possibly have won by three hundred and sixty thousand votes without 'stealing the vote'—ignoring the sixteen years I've served on the Virginia Beach City Council, in the Virginia House of Delegates, and in the Senate of Virginia, and ignoring the paper ballots that the State Board of Elections and your own party's county registrars already verified," Katie said. "And yet at twelve o'clock noon on Sunday, January third, Article I, Section 3, Clause 4 of the Constitution of the United States gives you zero choice. You have to stand right in front of my wheelchair in the Old Senate Chamber, hold the card with the secular affirmation printed on it, leave 'So help me God' out of your mouth, and personally swear 'Broken Katie Prairie' into the United States Senate while my hand rests on the Constitution. I've had January third circled on my calendar since Election Night, Mr. Vice President. See you at noon."

Nudging her joystick in tandem with mine, Katie didn't even wait for his reply. We spun our two SwiftChairs one hundred and eighty degrees in unison on the tiled floor of the President's Room and rolled side by side out into the marble Senate corridor with Dad grinning from ear to ear behind us, leaving the Vice President of the United States standing frozen under the chandelier.
