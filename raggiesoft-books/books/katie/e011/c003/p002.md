## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 9:25 AM — King Street–Old Town & The Yellow Line Over the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge

![Emerging from the Capitol South street elevator onto the sunlit brick plaza with the United States Capitol dome gleaming behind them on January 3, 2027, 41-year-old Senator-Elect Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate roll side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands while their 68-year-old father David walks proudly beside them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_capitol_south_elevator_plaza_arrival_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Excuse me—Senator-Elect Prairie!" a young man in a navy wool overcoat with a congressional staff ID lanyard around his neck called out as Katie and I rolled out of the platform elevator with Dad walking right beside us onto the elevated concrete island platform at King Street–Old Town Station. "If you're heading to the Capitol for noon, don't get on that train—that's a Yellow Line train! The Blue Line on the other track goes straight to Capitol South without having to transfer!"

Katie and I looked at each other across our wheelchair armrests and laughed—the exact laugh of two people who had spent twenty-five years memorizing every route map, transfer pulse, and rail bridge from Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Route 15 on Military Highway to the Washington metropolitan grid.

"Sure, you can take the Blue Line straight through to Capitol South," Katie told the young staffer warmly as the silver Capital Transit Yellow Line train glided up to the platform edge and its pneumatic doors chimed open level with the concrete sill. "If you want to take the long way all the way around through Crystal City, Rosslyn, and Foggy Bottom! We're taking the Yellow Line straight across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge to L'Enfant Plaza and hopping the Blue Line two stops to Capitol South. Come on—you'll beat your Blue Line train to the Hill by twelve minutes!"

Blinking in surprise as he did the mental map calculation, the staffer grinned and stepped into the Yellow Line car right behind Dad as Katie and I rolled our two SwiftChairs into the center wheelchair bays.

Nine minutes later, right after our train sped past Reagan National Airport and the Pentagon, the tunnel walls fell away and our Yellow Line car burst out into the brilliant January morning sunlight high above the Potomac River on the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge.

Through the wide glass window beside our wheelchairs, the entire skyline of the capital opened up across the sparkling water: the white marble colonnade of the Jefferson Memorial in the foreground, the towering obelisk of the Washington Monument to the left, and straight ahead on Jenkins Hill, the cast-iron dome of the United States Capitol gleaming against the clear blue winter sky.

Right inside the middle of that span over the river, Katie's phone buzzed inside her coat pocket.

She glanced at the caller ID—*Office of the Senate Sergeant at Arms*—and tapped the speaker button just loud enough for Dad and me to hear over the rhythmic click of the welded rail joints beneath the bridge.

"Senator-Elect Prairie?" the protocol liaison's voice came through the speaker, breathless and slightly panicked. "Ma'am, I'm standing here with the Capitol Police detail and the press pool at Gate D inside Washington Union Station! The five o'clock ARTS train from Norfolk just pulled onto the track two minutes ago, and the conductor told us you got off the train back in Virginia! Are you alright? Do we need to send a car down the George Washington Parkway to pick you up?"

"Put your SUVs back in the garage, Marcus—we're completely fine," Katie said, her voice calm and ringing with good humor as our Yellow Line train glided off the north end of the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge and dove into the tunnel beneath Seventh Street toward L'Enfant Plaza. "Washington Union Station is way too loud and crowded for my brother and me, and the ARTS train takes twenty-five minutes just to crawl from Alexandria into the terminal. We got off on the mobile lift at Alexandria, walked over to King Street–Old Town, and rode the Yellow Line across the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge. We're pulling into the lower level at L'Enfant Plaza right now to catch the Blue Line two stops to Capitol South—we'll see you at the First Street entrance in ten minutes!"

When she tapped the screen to end the call, our dad chuckled, shaking his head as he braced one hand on the stainless-steel grab pole beside us.

"You two just outran a Capitol Police motorcade and twenty television vans on a three-dollar CapitalPass card," Dad said proudly.

"And we're going to keep doing it for the next six years, Dad," Katie said, looking from Dad to me as the doors opened at L'Enfant Plaza and we rolled smoothly out onto the lower-level platform toward the elevator up to the Blue Line track. "When we started *Ideas for Coastal Virginia Transit* back in 2008 and realized we couldn't even stay past five-thirty at the Virginia Beach Municipal Center for a six o'clock City Council meeting because the last Route 12 left CCHR at 5:48 PM, I promised myself I would never turn into one of those politicians who only sees Virginia through the tinted window of a town car. Every single time I visit constituents anywhere in the Commonwealth as their United States Senator—whether it's CVTA back home in Hampton Roads, Blue Ridge Transit down at CPI in Blacksburg, or Capital Transit up here in Northern Virginia—we are riding that community's public transit system as part of the visit."

## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 10:45 AM — "Nobody Crops My Powerchair Out of My Senate Portrait"

Fifteen minutes after our Blue Line train let us off at Capitol South and we rode the street elevator up to the sunny sidewalk at First and C Streets Southeast, Katie and I rolled through the bronze doors of the Russell Senate Office Building with Dad walking alongside us and headed up to the third-floor corner suite overlooking the Capitol grounds for Katie's official Senate photograph.

Inside the high-ceilinged room with its dark mahogany wainscot paneling and classical cream pilasters, the official Senate historical photographer had already positioned the United States Flag with its gold fringe to the left of the tall arched window and the deep blue Flag of the Commonwealth of Virginia to the right, framing the white dome of the Capitol in the morning light directly behind the center mark on the marble floor.

As Katie unbuttoned her heavy winter wool coat and handed it to Dad—revealing her tailored royal-blue Senatorial suit blazer with the small gold circular United States Senate pin gleaming on her left lapel—she steered her compact SwiftChair onto the tape mark between the two flags and rested her right hand naturally on her joystick controller.

Behind the studio umbrella lights, the photographer squinted through the viewfinder of his camera and immediately reached for the crank on his heavy aluminum tripod, raising the lens higher and zooming the barrel inward.

"Alright, Senator-Elect, let's bring the frame in nice and tight from the mid-chest up so we match the standard head-and-shoulders portraits down the corridor," the photographer said briskly. "If you could just fold your hands slightly higher near your lapel so the armrests don't—"

"Stop right there, please," Katie said—not unkindly, but with the unmistakable, quiet authority that had once silenced committee rooms in Richmond and bullies in the hallways of Northwood High School.

The photographer's hand froze on the zoom ring.

"Lower the tripod back down and step back six feet so the entire wheelchair is in the frame from my head down to the wheels and the footplate," Katie instructed him, sitting tall and poised between the two flags. "My twin brother Nate and I have rolled in these compact SwiftChairs from the moment we woke up until we went to bed since the third Friday of September in 2001 when we were sixteen years old. I didn't hide my wheelchair when I knocked on doors for Virginia Beach City Council, I didn't hide it in the House of Delegates or the Senate of Virginia, and nobody is cropping my powerchair out of my United States Senate portrait."

![Official full-seated U.S. Senate portrait of forty-one-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie of Virginia proudly seated in her compact SwiftChair power wheelchair in a royal-blue blazer between the United States and Virginia flags with the Capitol dome visible through the arched window behind her]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b005/senator_katie_prairie_age_41_us_senate_portrait.jpg)

From the doorway beside Dad, I watched the photographer blink once, nod with genuine respect, lower his tripod to waist height, and step back across the marble floor until every inch of my sister's silver-and-black SwiftChair was framed proudly between the Stars and Stripes and the Seal of Virginia.

When the studio strobes flashed against the mahogany walls and Katie smiled straight into the lens with her right hand resting on her joystick controller, my throat tightened with a pride so fierce I could barely swallow.

## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 12:00 PM — Swearing In on the Constitution of the United States of America

![Inside the historic Old Senate Chamber of the United States Capitol on January 3, 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie sits in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair affirming her secular constitutional oath of office before Vice President Julian Vance with her left hand resting on the family copy of the U.S. Constitution beside her twin brother Nate in his SwiftChair and their proud father David.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_old_senate_chamber_swearing_in_reenactment_katie_nate_david.jpg)

At two minutes before twelve o'clock noon—after riding the subterranean United States Capitol Subway together beneath Constitution Avenue—Dad and I took our places at the front brass railing of the Senate Family Gallery directly above the floor of the United States Senate Chamber.

Looking down from the gallery bay where my wheelchair brakes were locked beside Dad's polished dress shoes, I could see the entire semicircle of one hundred mahogany desks arranged across the blue carpet below us. On the far aisle of the Democratic side—three aisles over from Senator Tess Dunmore of Illinois in her ultralight titanium chair—the Sergeant at Arms carpenters had already modified Senator Katie Prairie's assigned mahogany desk, removing the fixed chair base and setting one-and-a-half-inch mahogany riser blocks beneath the legs so her compact SwiftChair and right-hand joystick controller rolled seamlessly beneath the desktop.

At precisely 12:00 PM, Vice President Julian Vance—serving in his constitutional capacity under Article I, Section 3, Clause 4 as President of the Senate—brought the heavy ivory gavel down on the marble rostrum, calling the One Hundred Twentieth Congress of the United States to order.

As the Reading Clerk began calling the Newly Elected Senators forward to the well in alphabetical groups of four—*"Ms. Prairie of Virginia..."*—and Senior Senator Thomas Keane stepped into the aisle to escort his new Virginia partner forward while Senator Tess Dunmore tapped her wheel rim in salute, Dad reached over and gripped my shoulder with a steady, calloused hand.

In the span of three heartbeats, forty-one years of our lives flashed behind my eyes: two nine-year-old twins whispering our *Journey to the Sunlit Valley* cartoon password before unlocking the front door after school; two twelve-year-olds wearing matching maroon shirts on Twins' Day when a sixth-grade boy broke my sister's heart; two sixteen-year-olds learning in Dr. Carter's exam room that the borrowed time in our legs was almost gone; two seventeen-year-olds wedging a fifty-pound power wheelchair against the inside of a locked bedroom door on New Year's Eve while we ate dry toasted oat cereal out of a cardboard box to stay safe from a house full of drunks; and two twenty-three-year-olds holding a camcorder on a CVTA Route 15 bus crossing the Elizabeth River as we exposed why working people couldn't even catch a bus home from a six o'clock City Council meeting.

Down on the blue carpet of the Senate Chamber, Katie didn't need stairs. She nudged her right thumb forward on her joystick, rolled her compact SwiftChair smoothly down the permanent ramp along the chamber aisle into the well of the Senate, looked straight up at Vice President Julian Vance on the rostrum, and raised her right hand.

Fifteen minutes later, as the applause from the galleries echoed through the Capitol corridors, the elevator doors brought Dad and me down to the historic Old Senate Chamber for the individual ceremonial swearing-in with Vice President Vance.

Katie had been looking forward to this exact five-minute appointment for two full months.

All through the autumn campaign—and even after the networks projected her victory on Election Night—President Garrett Thorne had sneered at her from the South Lawn as *"Broken Katie Prairie"* and *"a bitter little cripple on wheels,"* while Vice President Julian Vance had gone on the Liberty First Network to mock her wheelchair, her unmarried life, and her bond with me. Ten days before Christmas, hoping to whip up a culture-war pressure campaign that would force her to back down, Vance's ceremonial protocol office had leaked to Beacon News, LibertyMax, and Virginia Beach's own evangelical flagship broadcast *The 777 Circle* on the Covenant Broadcasting Network that Senator-Elect Prairie had formally notified the Vice President she would be affirming her oath on the Constitution of the United States instead of a Christian Bible and omitting the words "So help me God."

Instead of backing down an inch, Katie had worn their evangelical outrage as a badge of honor. Every time Katie had ever taken an oath of office back home in Virginia—first on the Virginia Beach City Council, then in the Virginia House of Delegates, and then in the Senate of Virginia in Richmond—she had refused to pretend to believe in a religion or a god that neither she nor I believed in. To my sister's clear, literal, fiercely principled mind, Article VI, Clause 3 of the United States Constitution explicitly guaranteed that Senators were bound by Oath or Affirmation and prohibited any religious test for public office—meaning her left hand belonged on the exact secular Constitution she was promising to defend, not a single word of religious theater belonged in her mouth, and Vice President Julian Vance had zero constitutional choice but to stand in front of her wheelchair and administer every word of it himself.

Now, as Dad and I positioned ourselves right beside her in the center of the crimson-and-gold Old Senate Chamber while Vice President Vance stepped up stiffly with the printed oath card in his hand, I held out the thick, gold-stamped navy leather volume Dad had bought for her at the National Archives gift shop that morning: *The Constitution of the United States of America*.

Looking down from his full height at the forty-one-year-old woman he and President Thorne had mocked as "Broken Katie Prairie"—and who had just helped strip him of his tie-breaking vote in a fifty-four-seat Democratic Senate—Julian Vance's jaw muscle twitched once as Katie placed her left palm squarely on the gold lettering of *We the People*, raised her right hand high, and locked her unblinking blue eyes on his face.

Reading from the secular affirmation card our transition counsel had required under Article VI, Clause 3 of the Constitution, Title 5, Section 3331, and Title 1, Section 1 of the United States Code—with the optional religious phrase conspicuously absent at the bottom—Vice President Vance had to speak every syllable aloud:

*"Do you solemnly affirm that you will support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic; that you will bear true faith and allegiance to the same; that you take this obligation freely, without any mental reservation or purpose of evasion; and that you will well and faithfully discharge the duties of the office on which you are about to enter?"*

Katie's voice rang out clear, steady, and unshakeable across the historic chamber, never breaking eye contact with the Vice President:

*"I do solemnly affirm."*

As the shutters of the Senate photographers clicked in a rapid burst of white light and Vice President Vance forced a tight, wooden handshake—"Congratulations, Senator Prairie"—Katie gave him a cool, victorious smile:

"Thank you, Mr. President of the Senate. Keep that gavel warm—Senator Dunmore already showed me the ramp up to your rostrum."

Before Vance's face could finish turning purple, Katie turned her head toward me first.

Right there on the floor of the Old Senate Chamber, with the Constitution of the United States resting between our two compact power wheelchairs and our father's hands resting proudly on both of our shoulders, United States Senator Katie Prairie slipped her left hand across our armrests and laced her fingers tightly through mine.

Twenty minutes later, when the three of us rolled out onto the East Front plaza of the Capitol toward the Capital South Metro entrance, a wall of national television cameras from Beacon News, LibertyMax, and Virginia Beach's own evangelical flagship program *The 777 Circle* on the Covenant Broadcasting Network came rushing across the flagstones—breathlessly demanding to know why the new junior Senator from Virginia had affirmed her oath on the Constitution instead of a Christian Bible and omitted "So help me God" on national television.

Katie didn't roll past them. She stopped her SwiftChair right in front of the live satellite microphones, rested her hand on the navy leather cover of the United States Constitution on my lap tray, and smiled brightly into the lenses.

"I'm glad you asked, because it sounds like Beacon News, LibertyMax, and the folks at *The 777 Circle* back home on Indian River Road in Virginia Beach still haven't read the document I'm holding in my hand," Katie said clearly into the live network feeds. "Article VI, Clause 3 of the Constitution of the United States says that all Senators shall be bound by Oath or Affirmation to support this Constitution, and that no religious test shall ever be required as a qualification to any office or public trust under the United States. Even the Presidential Oath written by the Founders in Article II, Section 1, Clause 8 says 'swear or affirm' and does not contain the words 'So help me God.' And before anyone on cable television repeats the myth that President George Washington added 'So help me God' in 1789—historians debunked that fable decades ago. Zero eyewitnesses at Federal Hall in 1789 recorded Washington saying it; a writer named Rufus Wilmot Griswold made it up sixty-five years later in an 1854 book, and no President is recorded saying it until Chester A. Arthur in 1881. I affirmed my oath on the Constitution of the United States because the Constitution is the supreme law of the land—and my brother, my dad, and I are going to spend every single day of this term defending it."

With the cable correspondents left speechless behind us on the Capitol plaza, Katie turned her SwiftChair toward the sidewalk curb cut, gave my hand a warm squeeze between our chairs, and rolled side by side with Dad and me toward the Metro elevator—proving to the entire country what the two of us had known since the day we were born: twin brothers and twin sisters never leave each other behind.
