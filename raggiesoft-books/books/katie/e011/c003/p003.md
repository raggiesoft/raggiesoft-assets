## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 4:30 PM — The First Invite to Cocktails & "A Known Side Effect of Death"

![Inside the gilded Mansfield Room of the United States Capitol on January 3, 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs with their father David standing proudly behind them, clinking three tall glasses of ice-cold ginger ale in a family toast with senior Senators.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_mansfield_room_ginger_ale_toast_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"There she is—the newest Senator from the Commonwealth of Virginia!" Senator Arthur Harrington, the silver-haired senior chairman of the Appropriations Committee, boomed warmly across the gilded chandeliers and marble fireplace of the Mansfield Room just off the Senate floor. "Katie, bring your brother and your father over here! Waiter—three flutes of the French champagne for Senator Prairie and her family, and then we're heading downstairs to my hideaway office for twenty-year single-malt scotch and celebratory cigars on the West Front terrace!"

A tuxedoed catering steward immediately stepped between our two compact SwiftChairs with a polished silver tray balanced on his palm, lowering three crystal flutes of bubbling gold champagne toward Katie, Dad, and me.

Across the crowded reception room, half a dozen senior Senators and committee chairs raised their wine glasses and crystal tumblers, waiting for the freshman Senator from Virginia to take a flute and join the oldest social ritual on Capitol Hill.

Sitting beside my sister in my powerchair, I didn't even have to glance at her to know what both of us were remembering beneath the polite clink of Capitol crystal: the sour stench of gin on our mother's breath on the boat deck of the *Sunward Tropics* in August 2001; the bass-heavy thump of our cousin Brittany's drunken New Year's Eve party rattling the walls of our brick ranch off Military Highway on December 31, 2002; and two seventeen-year-old twins wedging a fifty-pound wheelchair frame against the inside of a locked bedroom door while we ate dry toasted oat cereal out of a cardboard box, clinked two cold cans of ginger ale together at midnight, and swore we would never touch a drop of alcohol for the rest of our lives.

In a room full of powerful Washington politicians, however, a personal promise made at age seventeen was the kind of thing back-slapping colleagues always tried to talk you into breaking *"just this once."*

Fortunately for the three of us, biology and pharmacology had given Katie, Dad, and me an answer that no Senator in the United States Capitol could ever argue with.

Katie raised her left hand—calm, poised, and completely unbothered—stopping the waiter's silver tray six inches above her armrest.

"Thank you so much, Arthur, and thank you, sir, but please keep the champagne on the tray," Katie said clearly enough for the entire circle of Senators to hear. "My twin brother Nate, my father David, and I can't touch a single drop of alcohol—or go anywhere near cigar smoke or tobacco—for medical reasons."

Senator Harrington's genial smile paused mid-toast as he lowered his crystal tumbler half an inch. "Oh—nothing serious, I hope?"

"All three of us take daily prescription medication where mixing it with alcohol or tobacco has a known side effect of death," Katie replied with matter-of-fact serenity, her blue eyes steady and bright beneath the crystal chandelier.

Across the circle of velvet ropes and gilded mirrors, the silence was instantaneous.

Two committee chairs instinctively took a half-step back with their wine glasses, and Senator Harrington's eyes widened before he immediately gestured for the waiter to pull the champagne tray away.

"Good Lord—say no more, Katie!" Senator Harrington said quickly, waving a second steward over from the refreshment bar across the room. "No alcohol and no cigars anywhere near you three—I'll make sure the whip's office and the cloakroom staff know that permanently! What can we get for you, Nate, and David right now?"

"Three glasses of ice-cold ginger ale would be wonderful, Arthur," Dad said with a warm, easy smile, resting his hand on the back of Katie's powerchair. "That's been the Prairie family toast for twenty-four years."

Thirty seconds later, when the steward returned with three tall highball glasses filled with crushed ice and golden ginger ale, Senator Harrington and the surrounding Senators raised their glasses right alongside ours.

As Katie, Dad, and I clinked our three glasses of ginger ale together in the center of the United States Capitol, Katie caught my eye across our armrests with the smallest, most triumphant glint of amusement. In a single sentence, my twin sister had just permanently exempted all three of us from every late-night Washington cocktail hour, wine-soaked donor reception, and smoke-filled backroom on Capitol Hill for the next six years.

## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 6:15 PM — Across the Potomac & Through the King Street Tunnel to Sunset Drive

At quarter past six that evening, while the winter sky over the National Mall deepened to indigo and the other newly sworn-in Senators climbed into waiting black town cars bound for Georgetown townhouses or Capitol Hill condominiums, Katie and I rolled out of the Capitol South elevator with Dad walking right beside us, our CapitalPass cards in hand.

Riding the Blue Line two stops to L'Enfant Plaza and transferring up to the southbound Yellow Line platform took less than ten minutes. As our silver Yellow Line train glided back out onto the Charles R. Fenwick Bridge high above the dark, glass-smooth waters of the Potomac River, the illuminated white dome of the Capitol receded behind us and the lights of Alexandria rose ahead on the Virginia shore.

"How does it feel, Senator?" I asked quietly over the hum of the traction motors as the train banked south past Washington National Airport toward Old Town. "Leaving the District behind on your very first night?"

"Like I can finally breathe," Katie said, leaning her head back against her wheelchair headrest and letting out a long, contented sigh as the Yellow Line glided onto the elevated platform at King Street–Old Town Station. "Washington is where I work, Nate. Virginia is who we are."

Exiting the station street elevator into the crisp thirty-eight-degree night air, Katie and I rolled past the glowing destination signs of the King Street Station bus loop—where BOLT and Capital Transit buses were idling at the sawtooth curbs—and turned west along the wide, flat concrete sidewalk of King Street with Dad walking alongside us.

Immediately ahead of us stood the heavy dark-green steel girders and white-painted concrete retaining walls of the King Street railroad underpass, carrying the elevated tracks of both Alexandria Union Station and the King Street–Old Town Capital Transit station directly over the street.

As Katie and I guided our two SwiftChairs side by side along the sidewalk beneath the short railroad bridge with Dad walking at our side and emerged into the quiet residential glow on the west side of the tunnel, Dad's two-story Alexandria house rose right on the left where Sunset Drive met the hill.

It was a classic two-story red-brick Virginia house with black louvered shutters framing the multi-pane sash windows, two manicured round boxwood shrubs flanking a white columned front portico topped with a small white Chippendale railing, and a broad green lawn bordered by a shade tree just steps from the railroad overpass. Looking straight back over my shoulder from the front walkway, I could see the illuminated canopy of King Street–Old Town Capital Transit Station and the upper-level rail embankment of Alexandria Union Station right across the bridge.

Back home in Hampton Roads, Katie and I owned our primary residence fifty-fifty in the Kempsville section of Virginia Beach—the house where we claimed legal residency, where we were registered to vote, and where we paid our property taxes to the City of Virginia Beach. Dad, however, had owned this house on Sunset Drive long before Katie ever ran for the Virginia Beach City Council in 2010. The house was one hundred percent paid off and titled one hundred percent in Dad's name, giving Katie and me a permanent family roof in Northern Virginia whenever the Senate was in session so she could take the train into Washington for day trips and truthfully say she slept in the Commonwealth of Virginia every single night. Even better, we never had to publish a street number for reporters or curiosity-seekers—to everyone who knew us, it was simply Dad's house on Sunset Drive, tucked right on the other side of the tunnel with buses and trains at our doorstep.

## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 7:30 PM — The Vertical Platform Lift & Coding in Bedroom 4

![Inside the warm upstairs three-desk family office in Bedroom 4 of the Sunset Drive brick house in Alexandria on the night of January 3, 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs coding in Quantum Studio 2026 and PHP while their father David works at the third desk and an illuminated Capital Transit Yellow Line train and ARTS passenger train glide across the King Street bridge outside the window.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_sunset_drive_bedroom_4_coding_trains_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Home sweet Virginia," Dad said as he unlocked the white front door beneath the columned portico and held it open for Katie and me to roll inside over the flush threshold ramp.

When the three of us had worked with our Alexandria contractor that autumn to adapt the two-story Sunset Drive house for two full-time powerchair users, we had designed every square foot around both our accessibility needs and our privacy.

Here at the front of the first floor, directly behind the shuttered windows facing the sidewalk and the street, sat a traditional Colonial living room and dining room with a central hallway leading back to an accessible kitchen with roll-under countertops and a curbless roll-in bathroom. Anyone walking past the house on the street at night saw only warm table lamps, bookshelves, and a dining room table through the front windows.

Right alongside the central staircase in the hallway stood the engineering centerpiece that made a two-story historic brick house work for Katie and me: a quiet, enclosed through-floor residential vertical platform wheelchair lift linking the first floor directly to the second-floor landing.

After the three of us heated up bowls of chicken noodle soup and toasted sourdough bread in the kitchen for supper—and took our evening prescription tablets with three tall glasses of cold water—Katie rolled her SwiftChair to the hall lift gate and pressed the illuminated call button.

"Come on, Nate," Katie said, grinning over her shoulder even after sixteen hours awake since our 4:00 AM alarm in Virginia Beach. "Before we turn in, we still have to push the January 3rd schema update to our shared site network—and I want to test the new build in Quantum Studio 2026 while the night ARTS train rolls past the window."

While Dad walked up the central carpeted staircase to meet us on the upper landing, Katie and I rode the vertical platform lift one at a time up through the ceiling opening to the second-floor hallway and rolled past the three upstairs doors that completed our four-bedroom layout.

The first door on the left was Bedroom 2: officially designated as my bedroom whenever congressional staffers, plumbers, or visitors ever saw the upstairs floor plan, completely furnished with a neatly made full-sized bed, a cherrywood dresser, and a nightstand lamp—even though that bed went completely untouched every single night.

Across the hall was Bedroom 3, Dad's upstairs bedroom with his full-sized bed and reading chair.

And at the east end of the upstairs hallway, with two wide sash windows looking directly out over the shade tree and the steel railroad bridge toward the glowing platforms of King Street–Old Town and Alexandria Union Station, was Bedroom 4: our three-desk family office.

Having already changed out of her royal-blue Senate blazer into her favorite soft navy fleece pullover, the newly sworn-in United States Senator from Virginia rolled her powerchair right up to her dual-monitor workstation beside mine, while Dad settled into his ergonomic chair at the third desk in the corner to check his Monday morning remote-work queue.

For the next two hours, the only sounds in that warm upstairs room were the familiar, rhythmic click of three mechanical keyboards, the soft hum of our desktop towers, and the occasional distant rumble of a Capital Transit Yellow Line train or an ARTS locomotive gliding across the King Street bridge outside our window.

Side by side at our desks—just as we had coded together since learning HTML in seventh grade at Morrison Middle School in 1997, studying Dad's *Quantum Basic 6.0* textbooks with him in 2001, and copying the Web Applications Developer PHP and SQL curriculum onto our laptops at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in March 2007 to master it alongside Tina, Sheila, and Jim—Katie and I had Quantum Studio 2026, Quantum Code, and our PHP backend templates open across our monitors, maintaining and deploying updates to the network of websites and desktop applications that the two of us shared.

"Look at line two hundred fourteen in the router controller, Nate," Katie said happily, pointing at her screen as a northbound ARTS train whistled softly across the elevated embankment beyond the glass. "Five hours ago I was holding my hand on the Constitution of the United States in the Old Senate Chamber, and right now I'm fixing a trailing array comma in PHP with my twin brother. Tell me any other Senator on Capitol Hill is having a better Sunday night than this."

"Not a single one of them, Katie," I laughed, compiling the build in Quantum Studio 2026 and watching the green deployment checkmarks light up across our shared server dashboard. "Though wait until you see who just requested an appropriations meeting on your Class II Senate calendar for next month: the Virginia Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services and the leadership of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville. They're coming to Washington to ask Virginia's new United States Senator for federal discretionary funding."

Katie paused her fingers over her keyboard, and a slow, razor-sharp smile curved across her lips as twenty years of memory flashed in her blue eyes.

"Put them right on the calendar, Nate," Katie said softly. "I can't wait to ask DARS and the director of RRC whether they're still forcing twenty-one-year-old wheelchair users with associate degrees to tour the auto shop, the woodworking shop, and the nursing school before pitching them on a partnership with ValueMart to be a door greeter. And while they're sitting across from our wheelchairs asking for federal tax dollars, we're going to ask them under oath about aides grabbing clients' push-handles, locking twin siblings into separate Level 2 wings, accusing them of sneaking into a closet to have sex and then backing down the second they offer to go home because of the headcount funding formula I suspected back when we were twenty-one, and escorting the guy who attacked Natalie out to a van without calling the police."

## Sunday, January 3, 2027, 10:30 PM — The Private Ground-Floor Back Bedroom Sanctuary

![Inside the ground-floor master bedroom of the Sunset Drive house in Alexandria on the night of January 3, 2027, while the dark mahogany sleigh bed beneath the framed blue butterfly print sits untouched behind their two parked black SwiftChair power wheelchairs, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate cuddle closely in a soothing two-arm embrace inside their navy-blue sleeping bag on the floor of the walk-in closet beneath her hanging navy Senate blazer.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jan_2027_sunset_drive_senator_night_closet_full_cuddle_sanctuary_katie_nate.jpg)

At ten-thirty, Dad shut down his remote-work laptop at the third desk, stood up with a gentle stretch, and kissed the top of Katie's head before resting a warm hand on my shoulder.

"Goodnight, Senator. Goodnight, Nate," Dad said softly from the doorway of Bedroom 3 across the upstairs hall. "Sleep well, you two."

"Goodnight, Dad—love you," Katie and I said together.

One after the other, my sister and I took the interior vertical platform lift back down to the quiet first floor and rolled past the darkened front living room toward the rear of the house.

Tucked into the private back corner of the ground floor—looking out only onto our high-fenced backyard garden and tall evergreens behind drawn blackout curtains—was the Master Bedroom.

Officially, on paper, this ground-floor suite was Katie's bedroom, while Bedroom 2 upstairs was mine. We had deliberately chosen this room at the very back of the ground floor so that no passerby, reporter, or neighbor walking along the front sidewalk could ever peer through a street-facing window to see two forty-one-year-old opposite-gender twins sharing a bed.

Inside our quiet back-room sanctuary, however, there were no cameras, no Senate galleries, and no outsiders who needed an explanation for why the two halves of the same soul slept safest together. Even though the ground-floor suite had a queen-sized bed beneath the framed watercolor print of two blue butterflies we had carried with us from our bedroom off Military Highway, most nights—especially after a loud, bright, overstimulating day on Capitol Hill—we bypassed the open bed in favor of the deep, carpeted walk-in closet along the interior wall of the master suite, where we kept our single shared navy-blue sleeping bag and two pillows laid out on the floor.

Rolling our two compact SwiftChairs up to the threshold of the master walk-in closet, Katie and I plugged our magnetic charger leads into our joystick ports until both battery indicators glowed steady amber in the dim light.

Moving with the practiced upper-body coordination of twenty-five years in wheelchairs, we shed our clothes beside our chairs so our body heat could keep our lower-extremity circulation warm without tangled fabric, transferred down onto the soft carpet inside the walk-in closet, and zipped our shared sleeping bag up to our shoulders before pulling the closet door nearly shut to seal ourselves in quiet, velvet darkness.

Outside in the night, a late-night southbound train glided quietly over the King Street bridge just beyond the trees—heading south through Virginia toward Richmond and Norfolk along the exact steel rails that tied our whole life together.

Inside the warm cocoon of our shared sleeping bag in the dark closet, United States Senator Katie Prairie curled close against my chest, found my hand in the dark, and laced her fingers through mine.

"Goodnight, Nate," she whispered against my shoulder.

"Goodnight, Katie," I whispered back, holding my twin sister close as we drifted off to sleep in our sanctuary on Sunset Drive.
