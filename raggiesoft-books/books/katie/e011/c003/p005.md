## Tuesday, February 16, 2027, 2:00 PM — Making Them Squirm Behind 34 C.F.R. Section 361.38 in Dirksen Room 511

![Inside Room 511 of the Dirksen Senate Office Building in February 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding hands behind her mahogany desk while the squirming DARS Commissioner and RRC Director sit across from them beside their glossy funding binders as Katie reveals that she and Nate are the two March 2007 twelve-day RRC dropouts.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/feb_2027_dirksen_room_511_rrc_dars_appropriation_showdown_katie_nate.jpg)

"Senator Prairie, on behalf of the Virginia Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services and the entire executive leadership team down in Fishersville, it is such an honor to welcome a champion of the disability community to the Senate Appropriations Committee," Commissioner Marcus Sterling beamed across the polished mahogany desk of Room 511 in the Dirksen Senate Office Building, sliding a glossy spiral-bound presentation book toward Katie's wheelchair while the new director of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center, Dr. Evelyn Cross, nodded eagerly beside him.

Sitting in my compact black SwiftChair right beside my twin sister's left armrest with my MagnaBook Pro open on the corner of the desk and Katie's legislative director, Sarah, seated with her legal pad to our right, I watched the two state agency executives settle back into their leather visitor chairs.

Neither Commissioner Sterling nor Director Cross had worked in Fishersville twenty years earlier in March of 2007; both were newly appointed career administrators who had taken over their roles within the past three years. Better still, neither of them had the faintest idea that the forty-one-year-old United States Senator sitting across from them—or her twin brother beside her—had ever set foot on the campus of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center.

Why would they? On every campaign biography, on our software company's corporate profile, and in the official *Congressional Directory* of the One Hundred Twentieth Congress, both Katie and I listed the exact same two academic credentials we had actually earned: an Advanced Studies Diploma from Virginia Beach City Public Schools at Northwood High School in June 2003, and an Associate of Applied Science degree from the Community College of Hampton Roads in December 2006.

We never listed Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center on a resume because, technically, Katie and I were RRC dropouts. We had rolled out of Barton Hall after twelve days in March 2007 without completing the program, without sitting for an exit interview, and without earning a certificate—and not long after we had returned home to our house on Arvin Road in Virginia Beach and worked on a few of the copied classroom modules unofficially from home, we had decided to stop bothering with RRC's rigid exercises altogether and start doing our own thing.

"As you'll see on page four of our briefing packet, Senator," Director Cross said smoothly, tapping a color photograph of the flat-roofed red-brick dormitories along Roosevelt Avenue in Fishersville, "we are asking your office to sponsor an eighteen-point-five-million-dollar Congressionally Directed Spending request in this week's Labor, Health and Human Services, and Education Appropriations Subcommittee markup. The funds will support residential dormitory administration in Barton Hall and Cantor Hall, expand our two-week Vocational Evaluation intake division in the Bates-Harding Building, and fund our administrative compliance office."

Katie didn't even open the glossy spiral binder. Resting her hands calmly on the armrests of her SwiftChair, she looked straight across the mahogany desk at Director Cross and Commissioner Sterling.

"Before we talk about eighteen and a half million federal tax dollars, Director Cross, I have a few specific operational questions about how Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center treats the adults who check into Barton Hall," Katie said in a mild, conversational tone. "Let's say a twenty-one-year-old twin brother and sister—both full-time wheelchair users with progressive neuromuscular weakness, and both already holding accredited Associate of Applied Science degrees in information technology from the Community College of Hampton Roads—arrive at your center specifically to take the Web Applications Developer course in server-side PHP and SQL."

Beside me, I saw Director Cross blink once, her polite smile freezing slightly at the corners.

"First," Katie continued evenly, "when those two twenty-one-year-old wheelchair users roll up to the Barton Hall counter or walk down the hallway of the Bates-Harding Building, do your residential aides and vocational evaluation staff still walk up behind them and grab the rear push-handles of their wheelchairs to shove them around without asking permission—and then threaten to write the clients up as aggressive when they tell the staff to take their hands off their chairs?"

"Well—Senator, certainly our staff are trained to assist mobility-impaired clients—" Director Cross began.

"Second," Katie pressed on without letting her pivot, "why would two twenty-one-year-old wheelchair users who already hold accredited associate degrees in computer science be forced to spend two weeks in Vocational Evaluation touring the auto mechanics shop, the woodworking shop, and the nursing assistant lab—and then be told by an evaluator that if software engineering proves too taxing, the Center has a wonderful partnership with the Waynesboro ValueMart to train a college-degreed woman in a wheelchair to sit by the sliding doors as a ValueMart door greeter?"

Across the desk, Commissioner Sterling shifted uncomfortably in his leather chair, glancing sideways at Director Cross.

"Third," Katie said, her blue eyes sharp and unblinking, "let's say those twins test out of your two-week Vocational Evaluation in a single week so they can start the Web Applications Developer class—where every student has to bring a personal laptop because VITA has locked the state desktop computers down so tightly on a seven-year-old operating system that nobody can even start a local web server. Because moving to Cantor Hall is part of earning Level 3, and they haven't been on campus long enough to earn Level 3, they remain stuck in Barton Hall on Level 2—forced to beg for signed yellow paper Hall Passes just to catch the local bus, and forced onto separate men's and women's wings every night at eleven o'clock."

Director Cross's cheeks flushed pink, and she cleared her throat. "Senator Prairie, if someone has shared an anecdote with your office—"

"I'm not finished, Director," Katie said calmly. "Suppose on their ninth afternoon on campus, a male classmate assaults the twin sister in the therapeutic pool—splitting the skin across the bony bridge of her nose, causing a heavy nosebleed, and tearing her swimsuit top so that Student Health nurses have to pull the split bridge of her nose together with sterile MedStrip wound-closure strips and tape a white gauze bandage across her nose that she has to wear for seven straight days—and when her twin brother uses his hands on the pool deck to cover her bare chest until someone brings a towel, your poolside staffer writes him up for inappropriate contact. And when the traumatized sister—with fresh white tape and MedStrips across her swollen nose—doesn't want to be separated from her twin brother that night, so at ten-forty-five they slip down a darkened hallway from the Rec Hall in their street clothes and lock themselves inside a seasonal storage closet using lumpy bags of curtains as pillows, your night supervisor unlocks the door with a master key after the eleven-o'clock curfew check, automatically accuses a twin brother and sister in full street clothes of sneaking into a closet to have sex, and threatens to kick them out of the Center—until the twins call his bluff and dare him to call their father in Alexandria right then and there to come pick them up, at which point the supervisor backs down because losing two full-time residential students would hurt the Center's headcount funding, while the counseling staff prepares to slap them with a Level 1 restriction and a mandatory sibling separation contract."

In the quiet mahogany-paneled Senate office, you could have heard a pin drop.

Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross squirmed visibly in their chairs, exchanging a bewildered, increasingly uneasy look across the corner of the desk.

"Senator Prairie," Director Cross said slowly, her brow furrowing as she looked from Katie's face to mine and back again, "with all due respect... why are you being so oddly specific about a twenty-year-old incident? You're reciting the exact color of our Level 2 Hall Passes in Barton Hall, VITA's old desktop permissions in the computer lab, a Waynesboro ValueMart door-greeter pitch, MedStrip wound-closure strips under a seven-day nasal bandage from Student Health, and even plastic bags of curtains inside a seasonal storage closet at ten-forty-five at night. None of that would ever appear in a Senate briefing book—or even on a standard state incident form. How could you possibly know all of that?"

"And frankly, Senator," Commissioner Sterling jumped in, his voice tightening with a mixture of confusion and alarm as he tried to figure out who could have briefed her, "I don't understand why we are even talking about an oddly hyperspecific anecdote from March of 2007 when neither Dr. Cross nor I was employed at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center twenty years ago, and when our administration has no knowledge whatsoever of any twin siblings ever being treated in the manner you just described. Furthermore, even if someone claiming to be a former client from 2007 contacted your office with those allegations, you know our hands are tied by federal law. Under Title 34, Section 361.38 of the Code of Federal Regulations governing the State Vocational Rehabilitation Services Program, all personal client case files, disciplinary logs, and vocational evaluation records are strictly confidential. We are legally prohibited from confirming, denying, or discussing any individual client's personal file—even with a United States Senator—without an explicit written privacy release signed by the former clients themselves."

Katie let the silence hang across the mahogany desk for five full seconds, watching the two executives squirm behind their federal privacy statute while they thought they had successfully walled off the question.

Then Katie reached across our wheelchair armrests, laced her fingers through mine, and looked Director Cross and Commissioner Sterling dead in the eye.

"You don't need to worry about violating Title 34, Section 361.38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Commissioner Sterling," Katie said softly. "Because the twenty-one-year-old twin brother and sister in those March 2007 Barton Hall files are sitting right across this desk from you."

Every drop of color drained out of Director Cross's face. Commissioner Sterling's jaw literally dropped open.

"My twin brother Nathaniel and I checked into Barton Hall on Sunday, March 4, 2007, and we rolled out twelve days later after watching how your center operated," Katie said, her voice steady and cold as steel. "We were never interested in the rehabilitation part of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center—we only came to Fishersville for the Web Applications Developer class, and we fully intended to treat your center like college: go to class, sleep in the dorm, and be done with it. We were not prepared for the juvenile rules, or the mandatory Vocational Evaluation, or being talked down to like children. In your DARS computer database in Richmond, the junior United States Senator from Virginia and her twin brother are officially coded as Status 28 unsuccessful rehabilitation dropouts—because after we rolled out of Barton Hall and spent a few days working on the copied PHP modules from our house in Virginia Beach, we decided to stop wasting our time on your modules and build our own software company instead of becoming a ValueMart door greeter. And right here, across this desk, Nathaniel and I waive our confidentiality."

"S-Senator Prairie," Commissioner Sterling stammered, his hand trembling against his briefing binder, "we had no idea—your official biography only lists the Community College of Hampton Roads—"

"Because we didn't graduate from Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center—we dropped out after twelve days when we saw what a bureaucratic circus it was," I said evenly from my wheelchair beside Katie. "And wait until you hear why our Thursday morning Level 1 restriction meeting with our counselor never happened on March 15, 2007."

Katie reached to her right, picked up the thick manila constituent casework folder that had arrived by courier a month earlier after [our January phone call with Evelyn Brooks](/katie/e011/c003/p004), and laid it open directly on top of Director Cross's glossy funding brochure.

"On Wednesday night, March 14, 2007," Katie said, tapping the sworn affidavit clipped inside the folder, "your dorm database was scrambled, and your front-desk staff blindly followed the computer printout and handed out room keycards that put men into women's rooms. The exact same male student who broke my nose in your therapeutic pool on Tuesday afternoon used a reassigned keycard to enter the women's wing, where he raped our classmate Natalie—a chronologically adult woman who has the cognitive capacity of a young child. And on Thursday, March 15, 2007, while your counseling wing canceled our ten-o'clock closet write-up meeting because the housing roster was in chaos, your administration put Natalie's rapist into a passenger van at seven-fifty in the morning and escorted him off the property without calling the police, held a bleeding, traumatized young woman inside your campus infirmary all day without a forensic exam while washing her dormitory bedsheets in the campus laundry, and lied to her mother that she was merely having a homesickness episode—sweeping a violent campus rape under the rug so it wouldn't appear on a state incident report."

"Senator," Director Cross whispered, her voice shaking, "again, under federal confidentiality rules, we could never discuss another client's—"

"Read the top page of that folder, Director Cross," Katie said flatly. "Natalie's mother, Evelyn Brooks, holds full legal Guardianship and Conservatorship over Natalie under the laws of the Commonwealth of Virginia. Last month, after Natalie recognized my brother and me on television from our week and a half in Fishersville and watched my swearing-in ceremony, Mrs. Brooks called this office directly and filed an official constituent complaint against Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center and DARS with her United States Senator—complete with the hospital emergency room records from March 16, 2007 and a notarized HIPAA and 34 C.F.R. Section 361.38 privacy waiver signed by Natalie's mother as her legal guardian and conservator. And both Natalie and her mother will be sitting in the front row of Room 124 on Wednesday morning when the two of you testify under oath before the Appropriations Subcommittee."

Neither Commissioner Sterling nor Director Cross said another word. Gathering up their briefcases with visibly shaking hands—and forgetting their glossy spiral-bound funding book on the corner of Katie's desk—the two executives practically stumbled backward out of Room 511. Through the open doorway of our outer reception suite, Sarah, Katie, and I heard both of them frantically pulling out their phones before they even reached the fifth-floor elevators, speed-dialing their superiors in Richmond and the records office in Fishersville to beg for guidance.

Watching the heavy oak door click shut behind them, I looked over at Katie and Sarah. "Ten bucks says the two of them are already trying to figure out how to bail on Wednesday morning's Subcommittee hearing and drive straight back to Richmond."

Less than an hour later—while Katie, Sarah, and I were sitting around the mahogany desk finalizing our five-page statutory appropriations rider—the red light on Line One of Katie's desk phone flashed.

Having served across Capitol Square from the Governor's Mansion during her years in the General Assembly, the Governor's Chief of Staff, Arthur Lawrence, knew exactly who Katie Prairie was, knew that I was always right beside her, and knew that she almost always answered her direct office line herself.

Katie reached over and pressed the speakerphone button. "Senator Katie Prairie."

"Katie—Senator Prairie—it's Arthur Lawrence in the Governor's Office in Richmond," the Chief of Staff's voice came through the speaker grille in full, breathless damage-control mode. "Is Nate there at the desk with you?"

"I'm right here, Arthur," I answered.

"Good," Lawrence said grimly. "I have Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross conferenced in on this line from their parked car in Washington because I want them to hear every word of this directly. Forty minutes ago, after they walked out of your office and called the Secretary of Health and Human Resources in a panic, I personally ordered DARS General Counsel in Richmond and the records custodian in Fishersville to pull the unredacted March 2007 paper archive from the vault. Everything you and Nate told them—every single word about Barton Hall, the Tuesday afternoon pool assault, the two blacked-out Status 28 dropout lines on the April 2007 summary, and the Thursday morning van to the Staunton bus station—is sitting on my desk right now in black and white. The Governor is livid, and on behalf of the Commonwealth, I cannot tell you how sorry we are for what happened to Natalie Brooks and for how the two of you were treated twenty years ago."

"Save the apology for Natalie and her mother in Room 124 tomorrow morning, Arthur," Katie said evenly. "Why are Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross sitting in a parked car instead of reading our statutory reform checklist?"

"Because ten minutes ago, Senator, both of them asked the Secretary and me for permission to pull out of tomorrow morning's Subcommittee witness panel and drive straight back to Richmond tonight," Lawrence admitted flatly—before his voice hardened into a thunderclap aimed straight at the two state executives on the conference line. "And as I just told both of you before we dialed the Senator's direct line—and as I am repeating right now in front of Senator Prairie and Nate—you are out of your minds if you think the Governor of Virginia is letting either of you run back to Richmond! Do you have any idea what happens if the Commissioner of DARS and the Director of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center pull a no-show in front of Virginia's own United States Senator on the Appropriations Subcommittee?! You won't just cost Fishersville eighteen and a half million dollars—you will jeopardize the Commonwealth's entire ninety-two-million-dollar share of federal Title I Rehabilitation Act matching funds! Neither of you is leaving Washington. I am ordering both of you right now: check into your hotel, walk into Dirksen Room 124 at ten o'clock on Wednesday morning, sit behind those microphones while Senator Prairie enters every page of the 2007 record into the Congressional Record, and agree under oath to every single reform her office puts in front of you without one word of pushback. Do both of you hear me?!"

Over the speakerphone, two small, hollow voices answered from the parked car:

"Yes, Mr. Lawrence," Commissioner Sterling said quietly.

"Yes, sir," Director Cross whispered.

Katie leaned forward over the armrests of her SwiftChair, looking straight down at the speakerphone grille.

"And let me make one promise to all three of you right now, Arthur," Katie said, her voice calm and cold as ice. "If Director Cross and Commissioner Sterling do not show up at that witness table for the Subcommittee hearing on Wednesday morning, they won't be hiding in Richmond or Fishersville—they will be facing compulsory subpoenas from the United States Senate."

She had chosen every word with surgical precision. An individual Senator couldn't unilaterally conjure a subpoena into existence on her own say-so—Senate committees and subcommittees operated under strict chamber and committee rules governing how compulsory process was authorized and issued—and Katie wasn't pretending otherwise. She was warning the Governor's Chief of Staff that the moment voluntary cooperation ended, she was prepared to walk straight to the Subcommittee Chair and the full Committee leadership to initiate formal compulsory congressional process.

And Arthur Lawrence, who knew Capitol politics inside and out, heard the unspoken final sentence behind her words loud and clear: *Arthur, get them into those chairs voluntarily, because neither you nor the Governor wants to find out what happens next.*

"They will be in their seats at nine-forty-five sharp, Senator," Lawrence said quickly. "You have my word."

## Wednesday, February 17, 2027, 10:00 AM — The Subcommittee Hearing & Strings Attached on Every Federal Dollar

Less than twenty hours later, at ten o'clock on Wednesday morning inside Room 124 of the Dirksen Senate Office Building, the crystal chandeliers gleamed above the curved mahogany dais of the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee on Labor, Health and Human Services, Education, and Related Agencies.

Sitting in my SwiftChair in the staff bay immediately behind Katie's custom-ramped place on the Subcommittee dais, I looked out past the green-baize witness table—where Commissioner Marcus Sterling and Director Evelyn Cross sat stiffly behind their microphones—to the very first row of the public seating gallery.

Sitting side by side in the front row were forty-one-year-old Natalie and her sixty-eight-year-old mother Evelyn, holding hands as they looked up at Katie on the dais.

"The Chair recognizes the Senator from Virginia, Senator Prairie, for five minutes of questioning," the Subcommittee Chairman announced, tapping his gavel gently.

Katie leaned slightly toward her microphone from her SwiftChair, her royal-blue blazer crisp beneath the television lights.

"Thank you, Mr. Chairman," Katie said clearly. "Commissioner Sterling, Director Cross, before we begin, I ask unanimous consent to enter into the permanent hearing record the sworn constituent complaint, March 2007 hospital emergency room documentation, and signed guardianship privacy release submitted by Evelyn Brooks—a Virginia mother who holds legal Guardianship and Conservatorship over her daughter, Natalie—detailing the events of March 14 through March 16, 2007 at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville, Virginia. And for the public record, I will state in open session what I shared with both of you in my office on Tuesday: in March of 2007, my twin brother Nathaniel and I spent twelve days as residential Level 2 clients in Barton Hall at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center before dropping out."

A quiet murmur rippled across the press table and the staff benches along the wall as reporters scribbled furiously in their notebooks.

"During those twelve days in March 2007," Katie continued, her voice ringing with calm, forensic precision across the marble hearing room, "my brother and I—already holding Associate of Applied Science degrees from the Community College of Hampton Roads—had our wheelchair push-handles grabbed without our consent by Center staff, and were forced to tour auto mechanics, woodworking, and nursing shops before being pitched on a partnership with ValueMart to work as a door greeter. Then, on Tuesday, March 13, 2007, a male student assaulted me in the therapeutic pool—breaking my nose open and ripping my swimsuit top off, which forced my twin brother to use his hands to cover my exposed chest until someone brought a towel. Instead of expelling the attacker, RRC staff wrote my brother up for covering my chest, and when my brother and I locked ourselves in a storage closet together that night in our street clothes because I was too traumatized to be separated onto different wings, staff accused us of having sex and threatened us with expulsion and a Level 1 dorm-grounding and sibling-separation contract—while leaving my attacker right there on campus, handing him a scrambled keycard to the women's wing the very next night so that the exact same perpetrator who broke my nose and ripped my swimsuit top off was able to unlock Natalie's door and rape her in the dormitories, before Center administration escorted him into a van on the morning of March 15, 2007 and sent him off campus without calling the police."

At the witness table, Commissioner Sterling swallowed hard into his microphone. "Senator Prairie, as we expressed to you on Tuesday, neither Dr. Cross nor I was with the agency in 2007, and we are horrified by what Natalie and her family—and you and your brother—experienced twenty years ago."

"Words don't reform institutions, Commissioner—appropriations language does," Katie replied evenly. "You have come to Washington asking this Subcommittee for eighteen point five million dollars in federal funding. As a United States Senator from Virginia sitting on this Committee, let me make my position crystal clear: I refuse to entertain a single federal dollar for Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center or the Virginia Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services until you carry out the specific institutional reforms my office has placed on this table."

Holding up the five-page statutory rider I had drafted with Sarah the night before, Katie read the strings attached into the *Congressional Record* one by one:

"First, DARS and Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center will issue a formal written institutional apology to Natalie and her mother, open their 2007 administrative files to an independent audit by the Virginia Office of the State Inspector General, and adopt an ironclad, zero-tolerance mandatory reporting policy requiring Center staff to notify the Virginia State Police and local law enforcement within one hour of any report of sexual or physical assault on campus—with immediate termination and criminal referral for any administrator who attempts to escort a suspect off property, tamper with physical evidence, or conceal a crime to protect campus statistics.

"Second, the Center handbook and staff training manual will be permanently amended to recognize that a wheelchair is an extension of the person sitting in it, prohibiting any staff member from grabbing or pushing a client's wheelchair or push-handles without explicit consent.

"Third, DARS will end the practice of forcing clients who already hold accredited college degrees or documented technical proficiencies through two-week manual-trade Vocational Evaluation tours or pitching them low-wage retail greeter placements just to pad evaluation rosters, and will ensure that vocational technology classrooms are equipped with modern, unlocked developer workstations rather than outdated VITA-locked desktops.

"Fourth, Residential Services will abolish forced daytime separation directives and punitive Level 1 isolation contracts against adult family members and siblings who rely on one another for disability and sensory support.

"And fifth, federal disbursement will be tied to independent audits of real educational and career outcomes—never to 'butts-in-seats' residential headcount quotas."

Katie lowered the statutory rider onto her desk and looked squarely down from the dais at the two administrators.

"Until those five reforms are signed, implemented, and verified in Fishersville, Commissioner Sterling, my hold on your federal funding remains in place. Do we understand each other?"

At the witness table, Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross sat frozen under the television lights, the tiny red recording lamps glowing on the necks of their microphones while the stenographer's fingers hovered over her stenotype machine.

"Yes, Senator Prairie," Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross answered into their microphones in unison. "We will implement every single one of them."

If Sterling and Cross had been hoping that a quick verbal surrender would run out the five-minute clock and allow them to slip out of Dirksen Room 124 before the national cameras zoomed in any closer, they had badly misjudged the United States Senate.

At the center of the curved mahogany dais, Senator Arthur Harrington—the silver-haired Chairman of the full Appropriations Committee—leaned forward and clicked on his microphone, exchanging a single grim nod with the Subcommittee's Ranking Republican Member beside him.

"Before the Senator from Virginia's initial five minutes expire, let the Chair state the position of this Committee so there is zero ambiguity down in Richmond or Fishersville," Chairman Harrington said, his deep voice booming off the marble walls. "On the Senate Appropriations Committee, no home-state Congressionally Directed Spending request moves a single inch without the affirmative support of the home-state Senator. Senator Prairie's hold on this eighteen-point-five-million-dollar line item is absolute. Furthermore, the Ranking Member and I have just instructed the Committee clerk to add both of our names as original bipartisan cosponsors to Senator Prairie's five-point statutory rider—and this Subcommittee is transmitting today's hearing transcript and exhibits directly to the Rehabilitation Services Administration at the Department of Education and the Office for Civil Rights at the Department of Health and Human Services for an immediate federal Title I compliance audit of the Virginia Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services. Without objection, the Chair yields an additional five minutes to Senator Prairie so these witnesses can answer her remaining questions under oath."

At the green-baize witness table, Director Cross closed her eyes for a fraction of a second as the last of the color drained from her cheeks. A home-state Senator's hold had just become a bipartisan Committee mandate backed by two federal oversight investigations.

"Thank you, Mr. Chairman," Katie said, picking up the second folder I had set beside her armrest. "Commissioner Sterling, let's talk about what happens between now and five o'clock this Friday afternoon. Under Section 19.2-8 of the Code of Virginia, there is no statute of limitations on a felony in the Commonwealth of Virginia. Felony forcible rape under Section 18.2-61 can be indicted and prosecuted twenty days after the crime, or twenty years after the crime."

Along the press table, every reporter's pen stopped in midair as they looked up at the dais.

"For twenty years," Katie continued, her voice steady and unflinching, "Evelyn and Natalie Brooks have kept the March 16, 2007 hospital emergency room forensic examination report, the triage records, and the photographs of Natalie's injuries safe in their files—while Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center locked the perpetrator's full legal identity, counselor intake file, electronic keycard door-lock audit log from the night of Wednesday, March 14, 2007, campus infirmary admission chart, laundry bleaching work order, and Thursday morning van dispatch log inside your vault in Fishersville behind Title 34, Section 361.38 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Yesterday afternoon, after you left my office, your archives staff in Richmond and Fishersville pulled those unredacted March 2007 records out of storage. And as your General Counsel knows full well, Section 361.38(e)(5) explicitly authorizes the release of vocational rehabilitation records in connection with a law enforcement investigation of a criminal offense."

Katie leaned an inch closer to her microphone, locking her blue eyes on the Commissioner.

"Commissioner Sterling, Director Cross—will you commit on the record right now to turning over the complete, unredacted March 2007 administrative, security, keycard, infirmary, and transport records for the perpetrator who assaulted me in the therapeutic pool on March 13 and raped Natalie Brooks on March 14 to the Virginia State Police Bureau of Criminal Investigation and the Augusta County Commonwealth's Attorney before five o'clock this Friday afternoon?"

"Yes, Senator Prairie," Commissioner Sterling said hoarsely into his microphone. "I have already instructed our General Counsel to assemble the complete unredacted 2007 files. They will be hand-delivered to the Virginia State Police Bureau of Criminal Investigation and the Augusta County Commonwealth's Attorney before five o'clock this Friday."

"Good," Katie said. "Now let's address the falsified administrative coding in your agency's mainframe. When Evelyn Brooks drove to Fishersville on Friday, March 16, 2007 and rescued her traumatized, bleeding daughter from your campus infirmary, Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center didn't log a campus violent felony. Instead, your predecessors coded Natalie Brooks in the DARS mainframe as a voluntary 'homesickness' withdrawal and a Status 28 'Unsuccessful Rehabilitation' dropout—the exact same Status 28 failure code your system placed next to my name and my twin brother's name when we rolled out of Barton Hall that very same afternoon."

Behind Katie's wheelchair on the dais, I saw two Senate aides along the back wall shake their heads in disbelief at the sheer audacity of a state agency coding a sitting United States Senator—and a sexual assault survivor—as "unsuccessful rehabilitations."

"My brother and I don't care what code your computer in Richmond has next to our names, Dr. Cross—Nate and I wear being twelve-day RRC dropouts as a badge of honor," Katie said evenly. "But Natalie Brooks did not drop out of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center because she was homesick, and she did not fail rehabilitation. Your center failed her. Before this Subcommittee marks up your budget next week, you will formally expunge every falsified 'homesickness' and 'Status 28' entry from Natalie Brooks's permanent record, replace them with an official administrative finding documenting the true reason she left Fishersville on March 16, 2007, and reimburse Evelyn Brooks in full for every dollar of out-of-pocket medical and trauma counseling care your agency refused to cover twenty years ago. Do I have your commitment?"

"Yes, Senator Prairie," Director Cross answered, her voice trembling slightly into the microphone. "Her record will be expunged and corrected immediately, and our agency will provide full restitution for her medical and counseling care."

Katie glanced at the amber digital countdown clock on the edge of the dais—ninety seconds remaining—and then looked out past the green-baize witness table toward the first row of the public seating gallery.

"I have one final requirement before I yield back to the Chairman," Katie said softly, though the acoustics of Room 124 carried every syllable to the back corners of the chamber. "Twenty years ago on Roosevelt Avenue in Fishersville, the administrators sitting in your chairs kept a twenty-one-year-old woman with the sweet, trusting heart of a seven-year-old child locked inside your campus infirmary for thirty-two hours while housekeeping bleached her bedsheets, and when her mother arrived to take her home, they wouldn't even look Evelyn Brooks in the eye when they lied to her face. Evelyn and Natalie Brooks took the early morning train up to Washington today, and they are sitting ten feet behind your backs in the very first row of this hearing room."

Every camera lens in the room pivoted toward the front row of the gallery, where sixty-eight-year-old Evelyn Brooks sat with her arm around forty-one-year-old Natalie.

"Commissioner Sterling, Director Cross," Katie said, "before you leave that table, I want both of you to turn around in your chairs, look Natalie and Evelyn Brooks in the eye, and give them the apology the Commonwealth of Virginia has owed them since March 15, 2007."

For three long seconds, neither administrator moved. Then, slowly, under the silent, unblinking gaze of every Senator on the dais, Marcus Sterling and Evelyn Cross pushed their leather witness chairs back from the green-baize table, stood up, and turned completely around to face the front row of the public gallery.

Standing ten feet in front of Natalie and her mother with the press cameras clicking softly along the wall, both executives bowed their heads.

"Mrs. Brooks, Natalie," Commissioner Sterling said, his voice carrying clearly across the quiet aisle without a microphone, "on behalf of the Commonwealth of Virginia and the Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services, I am deeply, profoundly sorry for the horrific harm done to you at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in March of 2007, and for the twenty years our agency spent hiding the truth instead of standing beside you."

"We failed to protect you when you were in our care, Natalie, and what was done to you and your mother was inexcusable," Director Cross added quietly, looking straight into Mrs. Brooks's eyes. "We are so sorry."

In the front row, Evelyn Brooks held her daughter's hand tightly against her coat, tears spilling down her cheeks, and gave a single, solemn nod—not of easy forgiveness for an institution that still had to prove itself in action, but of a mother who had finally forced the state to speak the truth in the light of day.

"Thank you, Mr. Chairman," Katie said into her microphone. "I yield back."

## Wednesday, February 17, 2027, 11:15 AM — Twenty Years Since the Barton Hall Cafeteria

The moment Chairman Harrington rapped his gavel to recess the morning witness panel, Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross hastily gathered their folders and slipped out the side corridor with their legal counsel to begin placing calls to the Virginia State Police and Richmond.

Up on the dais, Katie and I unlocked the brakes of our SwiftChairs at the exact same second. Steering down the custom carpeted side ramp off the Senate rostrum in tight parallel formation, we rolled out through the brass gate into the front aisle of Dirksen Room 124, stopping right in front of the first row of the gallery.

For the first time in twenty years—not since that grey March week in 2007 outside the noisy double doors of the Barton Hall cafeteria—we were looking at Natalie face-to-face.

At forty-one years old, Natalie wore a neat lavender cardigan over a floral dress, with a silver heart locket around her neck and a small canvas tote bag in her lap. The instant our two black motorized SwiftChairs glided to a stop in front of her row, Natalie's eyes went wide with pure, radiant recognition.

"Katie! Nate!" Natalie gasped happily, standing up beside her mother and reaching both hands straight out toward our wheelchairs—the exact pose she had drawn with her purple and blue crayons on the piece of white construction paper that was now framed in brushed gold behind Katie's desk upstairs in Room 511.

Katie and I both leaned forward over our armrests, Katie taking Natalie's right hand in hers while I took Natalie's left hand in mine.

"Hi, Natalie," Katie said, her voice thick with emotion as a warm tear slipped down her cheek and she smiled up at her old classmate. "It is so good to see you again."

"It's really good to see you, Natalie," I said warmly, giving her fingers a gentle squeeze. "We loved the picture you drew for us so much that Katie hung it up in a shiny gold frame right on the wall of her Senate office."

Natalie beamed from ear to ear, looking back and forth between my wheelchair and Katie's. Then she tilted her head, peering down curiously at the black motorized drive wheels and the compact joystick controllers mounted on our armrests.

And Katie, you talked so loud and brave on the big microphone up there! You told those people they aren't allowed to let mean boys have keys to the girls' rooms ever again!"

"For twenty years, every door I knocked on in Virginia was slammed in my face with a federal code number," Mrs. Brooks whispered. "It took the two kids who walked out of that place after twelve days to finally tear the wall down."

Beside Katie's wheelchair in the middle of the Senate hearing room, I looked at Natalie smiling brightly down at us, and then across at my twin sister—two twelve-day RRC dropouts who had gone home to do their own thing, and had just rewritten the rules for every disabled student who would ever roll through the doors of Fishersville again.
