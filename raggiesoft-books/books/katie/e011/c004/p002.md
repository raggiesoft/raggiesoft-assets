## Saturday, April 3, 2027, 12:05 PM — Riding CVTA Route 36 to Timberline Shopping Center & Family Lunch at Don Miller Subs

![Inside Don Miller Subs at Timberline Shopping Center on Holland Road in Virginia Beach on April 3, 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs clinking tall cups of ice-cold ginger ale in an early 42nd birthday toast with their 68-year-old father David over baskets of grilled steak-and-cheese subs and french fries while counter manager Marcus smiles behind the grill line.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/apr_2027_timberline_don_miller_subs_birthday_lunch_katie_nate_david.jpg)

"Approaching State Route four-ten, Holland Road and Timberlake Road—Timberline Shopping Center," the automated chime announced over the interior speakers of our forty-foot Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Route 36 bus as the driver signaled right and eased into the curb lane just east of where State Route 225 split off toward South Independence Boulevard and the Silverleaf commuter lot.

Up near the front wheel well, my twin brother Nate reached up from his compact SwiftChair and pressed the yellow request-to-stop strip along the wall, while our sixty-eight-year-old dad stood up from the forward-facing bench seat across the aisle and tucked his sunglasses into the breast pocket of his polo shirt.

Unlike the part-time Virginia General Assembly up in Richmond—which met for forty-five or sixty winter days and then sent us home for the rest of the year—the United States Senate stayed in session almost year-round from January 3rd right up to Christmas. Even so, the Senate's weekly floor voting calendar almost always wrapped up around two o'clock on Thursday afternoon, allowing Nate, Dad, and me to catch the afternoon ARTS Northeast Regional train south from Alexandria to Norfolk and spend every Friday, Saturday, and Sunday at home in Kempsville. Better yet, this first full week of April fell during the Senate's scheduled Spring State Work Period, giving the House impeachment managers and the White House defense counsel ten days to file their pre-trial briefs before the Senate impeachment trials of Secretary of Defense Bret Halverson, Vice President Conrad Drayton, and President Garrett Thorne gaveled in on Wednesday, April 14th.

"Have a great Saturday, Senator Prairie, Nate, Mr. Prairie!" the CVTA driver called out warmly as he kneeled the front air suspension, flipped the hydraulic fold-out ramp onto the concrete sidewalk of Holland Road, and waved as all three of us—having tapped our regular fare cards when we boarded—thanked him and rolled out into the bright sixty-eight-degree April sunshine.

Across the six lanes of State Route 410 on the south side of the highway stood Holland Square, anchored by the green signage of the Market Square grocery store. Here on the north side of Holland Road, however, we had just rolled into the parking lot of Timberline Shopping Center—a weathered, familiar 1980s neighborhood strip center anchored at its far end by a General Dollar discount store.

For Nate, Dad, and me, Timberline Shopping Center was one of our favorite Saturday afternoon stops along the Route 36 bus line between Buckner Boulevard and the Silverleaf transfer hub—and today was a special occasion. In five days, on Thursday, April 8th, Nate and I would turn forty-two years old. Every spring on the Saturday before our April 8th birthday, Dad rode the bus with us to Timberline Shopping Center to treat us to our traditional birthday lunch at Don Miller Subs and buy us a pair of vintage video game cartridges next door at Neat Stuff.

Steering our two SwiftChairs side by side along the covered concrete walkway toward the far-left bay of the five-storefront row, we rolled straight through the propped-open glass door of Don Miller Subs, where the warm, savory aroma of grilled ribeye steak, caramelized onions, and freshly baked sub rolls greeted us like an old friend.

"Well, look who's back from Washington—and happy early birthday to the April eighth twins!" Marcus, the counter manager who had been grilling subs for the three of us since my years in the Virginia House of Delegates, beamed from behind the stainless-steel prep line. "The birthday usual for the Prairie crew? Two six-inch grilled steak-and-cheese subs with everything, one turkey-and-provolone, a large basket of hot french fries to share, and three tall cups of ginger ale?"

"You know us by heart, Marcus—and the birthday lunch is on Dad today," Dad laughed, stepping up to the register to tap his debit card before I could reach for my wallet.

For the next thirty-five minutes, sitting at our usual accessible corner table where Nate and I could pull our wheelchairs right up to the laminate top without blocking the aisle, the three of us ate piping-hot subs and crispy french fries out of wax-paper baskets and clinked our three plastic cups of ice-cold ginger ale together in an early forty-second birthday toast. Nobody in the sub shop bothered us for speeches or talking points; a retired Navy chief at the next booth simply nodded with a quiet *"Proud of you up there on Capitol Hill, Senator,"* before letting us enjoy our Saturday lunch in peace.

## Saturday, April 3, 2027, 12:50 PM — Hunting for Forty-Second Birthday Retro Cartridges at Neat Stuff in Suite 732

![Inside Neat Stuff retro video game store in Suite 732 of Timberline Shopping Center on April 3, 2027, U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs with their father David standing beside them as store clerk Devon presents vintage PlayBrick and 16-bit Super Kinoka cartridges for their upcoming forty-second birthday.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/apr_2027_timberline_neat_stuff_suite_732_birthday_retro_games.jpg)

At ten minutes to one, after wiping our hands with napkins and tossing our paper baskets into the trash bin by the door, we rolled out of Don Miller Subs and turned one door to the right into Suite 732: Neat Stuff.

While everyone in Hampton Roads knew Pixel Haven as our region's premier retro and modern video game store, Neat Stuff here in Suite 732 of Timberline Shopping Center was Pixel Haven's sister store—a floor-to-ceiling treasure cavern packed with vintage console cartridges, classic handheld games, eight-bit and sixteen-bit hardware, comic books, and retro sci-fi collectibles. Ever since we were kids playing our Super Kinoka and Nova 64 consoles in our bedroom on Delaney Street—and trading PlayBrick cartridges in the back of Dad's car—Nate and I had never outgrown our love for retro video games.

"Katie! Nate! Mr. Prairie—happy early birthday!" Devon, the bearded thirty-something clerk behind the glass cartridge counter, lit up the moment our two SwiftChairs rolled down the wide center aisle between the glass display cases. "You three picked the exact right Saturday to ride the 36 over here. Hold on—I put two cartridges in the lockbox under the register yesterday afternoon because I knew Mr. Prairie would be bringing you two in for your April eighth birthday!"

Devon unlocked the sliding glass door at the back of the counter and set two pristine, gray plastic cartridges on the felt mat in front of our wheelchairs: an original 1994 *Gorilla King '94* cartridge for the PlayBrick with an unblemished holographic label, and a complete-in-box sixteen-bit *Super Kinoka* strategy role-playing game we had been hunting for since 2024.

Nate's blue eyes lit up like he was twelve years old again. Picking up the sixteen-bit cartridge, he tilted the bottom edge toward the overhead fluorescent light to inspect the gold copper contact pins.

"Look at the board pins, Katie—not a single trace of oxidation, and the battery-backed save chip was just soldered fresh," Nate said happily, handing me the cardboard box so I could admire the manual. "We can dump the ROM onto our local emulator in Bedroom 4 to preserve the save states and play the original cartridge on the CRT television in the living room tonight."

"Happy forty-second birthday, you two," Dad smiled, stepping up to the counter to pay for both games while Devon slipped them into a sturdy paper bag and hung the handles over my right armrest.

## Saturday, April 3, 2027, 1:15 PM — The Banners at Suite 756 & Senatorial Curiosity

![Outside along the covered concrete sidewalk of Timberline Shopping Center on Holland Road in Virginia Beach on April 3, 2027, 41-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie (with her Neat Stuff birthday gift bag hanging from her right armrest) and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs with their 68-year-old father David standing beside them in front of Pho 79 Vietnamese Kitchen, staring in astonished curiosity at the newly opened Tidewater Thorne & RTR Superstore in Suite 756 next door.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/apr_2027_timberline_suite_756_exterior_banners_pho_79_katie_nate_david.jpg)

Rolling back out of Suite 732 onto the covered concrete sidewalk of Timberline Shopping Center with our paper bag of retro games tucked beside my armrest, Nate, Dad, and I turned right along the storefront walkway toward the Route 36 bus stop out on Holland Road.

For years, the five-bay strip along this end of Timberline Shopping Center had followed the exact same left-to-right rhythm: Don Miller Subs on the left, Neat Stuff in Suite 732, the quiet pink neon sign of Halo Nails & Spa in the middle, the busy tables of Pho 79 Vietnamese Kitchen in the fourth bay, and then Suite 756 on the far right—a narrow eleven-hundred-square-foot storefront that had sat completely empty behind dusty brown butcher paper since the spring of 2023.

As we rolled past Halo Nails & Spa and reached the front windows of Pho 79 Vietnamese Kitchen—where the rich, mouthwatering scent of simmering star anise, charred ginger, cinnamon bark, and slow-cooked beef pho broth drifted out through the exhaust vent into the April air—all three of us stopped in our tracks.

Suite 756 right next door to the Vietnamese restaurant wasn't empty anymore.

Every square inch of its plate-glass facade had been plastered floor-to-ceiling with glaring crimson, white, and gold vinyl banners that read:

*THE TIDEWATER THORNE & RTR SUPERSTORE — OFFICIAL RESTORE THE REPUBLIC CAMPAIGN HEADQUARTERS!*

Behind the glass stood a six-foot cardboard cutout of President Garrett Thorne giving a grinning double thumbs-up, flanked by neon-red posters reading *THORNE: 3-0 UNDEFEATED — IMPEACH THIS!* and *THORNE 2028: THE REVENGE TOUR!*

"Good grief," Dad muttered, staring at the giant vinyl banner taped right up against the brick pillar separating Suite 756 from Pho 79. "An 'America First' Garrett Thorne shrine sharing a party wall with a Vietnamese pho restaurant. You couldn't make that up if you tried."

Sitting in my SwiftChair on the sidewalk twenty feet from the door of Suite 756, I frowned thoughtfully at the words *OFFICIAL RESTORE THE REPUBLIC CAMPAIGN HEADQUARTERS* printed across the banner.

Over the past six weeks, our constituent caseworkers in Virginia Beach and Norfolk—along with my colleagues on the Senate Commerce Committee's Consumer Protection Subcommittee—had fielded dozens of inquiries about a sudden rash of brick-and-mortar "Thorne Stores" popping up in strip malls across Virginia. Every constituent who called our office believed the exact same rumor: that these storefronts were officially vetted, licensed, and authorized by President Garrett Thorne, Thorne Global Holdings, or his national campaign committee, and that buying forty-dollar hats and sixty-dollar flags inside them went directly to fund the President's impeachment defense.

Neither Nate, nor Dad, nor I had any idea who had leased Suite 756. As far as we knew, it was just another out-of-state novelty vendor looking to make a quick buck off Holland Road traffic before the next Route 36 bus arrived at one-thirty-five.

Curiosity got the better of the Senator in me.

"Wait a second, Nate, Dad," I said, resting my right hand on my joystick. "We still have twenty minutes before the one-thirty-five bus pulls up. People keep asking our Commerce Committee staff whether these 'Thorne Superstores' are actually vetted and licensed by Garrett Thorne, his company, or his campaign—or if they're unlicensed bootleggers tricking voters into thinking retail purchases are campaign contributions. Let's roll inside for sixty seconds and look at the merchandise tags and the business license by the register."

"Sixty seconds of consumer protection fieldwork," Nate agreed, glancing at his watch with a dry smile. "Lead the way, Senator."

## Saturday, April 3, 2027, 1:18 PM — Inside Suite 756: Sharron Connects the Dots & Twenty-Four Years of Truth

![Inside The Tidewater Thorne & RTR Superstore in Suite 756 of Timberline Shopping Center on April 3, 2027, U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs beside their father David as former Delaney Street neighbor Sharron confronts a stunned Brenda Walsh behind the cash register and drops her Restore the Republic chapter badge onto the glass counter.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/apr_2027_timberline_suite_756_thorne_store_brenda_sharron_confrontation.jpg)

When Dad pulled open the aluminum-framed glass door of Suite 756 and held it for Nate and me to roll our two SwiftChairs across the threshold, a cheap brass bell taped above the door frame jangled loudly.

The first thing that hit us inside the narrow storefront was the sensory clash: the sharp chemical smell of fresh overseas vinyl ink mixing with the unmistakable, delicious aroma of Vietnamese beef pho broth and star anise seeping straight through the thin drywall partition from Pho 79 next door.

Every wall was covered in pegboard hooks and wire racks overflowing with crimson Restore the Republic baseball caps, polyester three-by-five flags showing Garrett Thorne's head superimposed onto a bodybuilder holding an eagle, gold-foil novelty *"One Thousand Dollar Thorne Notes,"* and freshly unpacked shipping cartons stenciled *THORNE: 3-0 UNDEFEATED — ACQUITTED IN THE SENATE!*

Up at the glass checkout counter near the back wall—where a small tabletop television was tuned to a shouting panel on LibertyMax—an older husband and wife in matching red RTR windbreakers were handing a crisp hundred-dollar bill across the counter, while three sixty-something women wearing crimson Restore the Republic lapel pins stood near the back flag rack sipping iced coffees and browsing the shelves.

"We drove over from Kempsville as soon as we saw the sign on Holland Road," the husband was saying earnestly to the woman behind the cash register, whose back was half-turned as she priced a box of white ceramic *THORNE 2028* coffee mugs with a handheld sticker gun. "We bought two flags and two of the '3-0 Undefeated' hats because we want every dollar going straight to President Thorne's Senate trial defense fund!"

"Oh, bless your hearts, patriots—President Thorne appreciates every single bit of your support, and those Senate Democrats don't stand a prayer of getting sixty-seven votes next week!" the woman behind the register replied in a syrupy, familiar voice, never once correcting the couple's false belief that her store was connected to the President's defense fund.

While the couple zipped their new hats into a plastic shopping bag and walked past us out the front door, I reached out from my wheelchair and flipped over the neck tag of a *THORNE: 3-0 UNDEFEATED* t-shirt hanging on the nearest circular rack.

"Look at the collar tag, Nate," I murmured quietly. "Manufactured in Guangdong Province for Wholesale Novelty Imports LLC. Zero union bug, zero Federal Election Commission disclaimer, and zero licensing mark from Thorne Global Holdings."

Nate rolled his SwiftChair three feet closer to the glass checkout counter and pointed past a large plastic jar of five-dollar buttons that had been deliberately positioned at the corner of the register to block the counter mat. Taped flat beneath the jar in tiny eight-point font was a two-sentence notice:

*Privately owned and operated retail establishment. Not authorized by, vetted by, licensed by, or affiliated with Garrett Thorne, Thorne Global Holdings, or any candidate or political committee.*

"Every dime goes straight into the store owner's pocket," Nate said softly, his eyes lifting from the hidden disclaimer to the framed City of Virginia Beach Commissioner of the Revenue Business License hanging on the pegboard wall behind the cash register.

I followed my brother's gaze up to the typed name on the municipal business license.

*PROPRIETOR: BRENDA WALSH.*

Across the narrow aisle beside my right wheel, I heard Dad draw in a sharp, silent breath.

Behind the counter, the sixty-five-year-old woman with dyed blonde hair finished sticking a twenty-four-ninety-nine price tag onto the bottom of a *THORNE 2028* mug, set her pricing gun down on the glass, and turned around with a practiced retail smile on her face.

"Welcome to the Tidewater Thorne Superstore!" she began brightly. "Everything in the store goes to—"

Her voice died in her throat.

The ceramic coffee mug slipped halfway out of her fingers and clattered upright onto the glass countertop.

Standing—and sitting—six feet in front of her cash register were her ex-husband David Prairie, her forty-one-year-old son Nathaniel Prairie, and her forty-one-year-old daughter, United States Senator Katie Prairie.

None of the three of us had laid eyes on our mother since the afternoon of Saturday, July 12, 2003—twenty-four years ago—when eighteen-year-old Nate and I had rolled our wheelchairs out the front door of the brick house on Delaney Street with Dad to move into Greenway Court Apartment A6.

For five full seconds, the only sound inside Suite 756 was the drone of the LibertyMax anchor on the corner television and the muffled clatter of soup spoons coming through the drywall from Pho 79 next door.

Brenda's face drained to chalk white, and then flushed a deep, blotchy crimson from her collar up to her roots. Her eyes darted in sheer panic toward the three women from her Restore the Republic chapter standing ten feet away by the back flag rack.

"What—what are you three doing in here?!" Brenda hissed, her voice dropping from its syrupy customer-service lilt to the sharp, defensive snap Nate and I still remembered from the hallways of our childhood. "Are you following me?! Did David tell you to come in here to harass me?!"

"Nobody knew you worked here, Brenda," Dad said, his voice completely calm, level, and devoid of anger as he stood with one hand resting steadily on the back push-handle of my wheelchair and the other near Nate's shoulder. "Your children turn forty-two on Thursday, April eighth. Just like we've done every spring for the past fifteen years, I rode the Route 36 bus over here with Katie and Nate to buy them their birthday lunch at Don Miller Subs and their birthday video games at Neat Stuff three doors down. We didn't even know Suite 756 had been leased until we rolled out onto the sidewalk two minutes ago."

At the mention of April 8th, Brenda's eyes flickered toward the wall calendar hanging beside her cash register—making it unmistakably clear that until Dad said the date out loud, the woman who had given birth to us forty-two years ago hadn't even remembered that our birthday was five days away.

Before Brenda could utter another word, one of the three women standing by the flag rack stepped forward into the center aisle, lowering her iced coffee cup as she stared in stunned recognition at Dad, then at Nate, and then at me.

It was Sharron—a former neighbor from two streets over in our old Kempsville subdivision who had known our family back in the nineties when Nate and I were growing up on Delaney Street, long before our neuromuscular diagnosis in 2001.

"David?!" Sharron gasped, her eyes widening as she looked from Dad's silver hair down to our two wheelchairs, and then straight across the counter at Brenda. "David Prairie?! Wait a minute... Katie and Nathaniel Prairie! I remember watching you two ride your bikes down Delaney Street when you were kids!"

Sharron spun around toward Brenda, her voice echoing across the narrow storefront as the other two Restore the Republic women—Donna and Carol—froze beside her.

"Brenda, wait a second!" Sharron demanded, pointing from the framed *Brenda Walsh* business license on the wall to my wheelchair. "When you joined our Restore the Republic chapter under the name Brenda Walsh, you told us you didn't even know the Prairie family! For two straight years you've sat at our kitchen tables trashing 'that Prairie woman' on television like she was a total stranger! Wait—your daughter is *our* United States Senator?! *And* their birthdays are coming up on Thursday, and you didn't even remember?! Brenda, it's time for you to come clean!"

Beside Sharron, Donna and Carol stared at Brenda as if the floor had just opened beneath their feet.

"What is she talking about, Brenda?!" Donna asked, her voice rising in disbelief. "United States Senator Katie Prairie is your biological daughter?! You looked us dead in the eye at church coffee hour on Tuesday and swore you weren't related to her!"

Behind the glass counter, Brenda's hands trembled violently as her carefully constructed double life collapsed in front of her own friends.

"I—I went back to my maiden name Walsh because David and the woke universities brainwashed them against their own mother!" Brenda stammered, her face turning a blotchy, furious purple as she tried to fall back on the talking points that usually worked in her chapter meetings. "They turned their backs on me and became godless Washington liberals!"

Sharron didn't buy a single word of it. She took a step closer to the counter, her face tightening with maternal disgust as she did the math out loud in front of Donna and Carol.

"Woke universities?!" Sharron shot back. "Brenda, I lived two streets over from you on Delaney Street in two thousand and one! Katie and Nathaniel weren't at a university when you and David divorced—they were sixteen years old at Northwood High School, right after the doctors diagnosed their muscle condition and put both of them in wheelchairs! Every mother in the neighborhood heard how you threw a fit because you were embarrassed to be seen pushing wheelchairs in public!"

Beside my left wheel, Nate's voice was quiet, steady, and devastatingly clear.

"Dad didn't turn us against you, Brenda," Nate said, looking straight across the counter. "You did that yourself in August of 2001 when we were sixteen years old and our legs were failing—when you maxed out the credit card to drag us onto a cruise ship against our will, screamed at us that we were ruining your vacation because we couldn't walk up the gangway, and tried to force two high school juniors into a toddler character meet-and-greet line so you could take a fake photo for the neighbors. And when the doctors put us in wheelchairs that September, Dad built ramps and carried us up the stairs, while you locked yourself in the kitchen with a bottle of gin."

"And the reason your name is Brenda Walsh on that business license behind you," Dad added calmly, "is because in October of 2001, sixteen-year-old Katie and Nate asked me and my attorney to make restoring your maiden name a condition of paying off your cruise debt in the divorce decree—because neither of your children wanted you carrying the Prairie name ever again."

Sitting in my SwiftChair with Dad's forty-second-birthday gift bag from Neat Stuff hanging beside my armrest, I looked from the six-foot cardboard cutout of Garrett Thorne grinning beside the register to Brenda, and then to Sharron, Donna, and Carol.

"Sixteen days ago, on the morning of March eighteenth," I said evenly, "the man on that cardboard cutout stood on the South Lawn of the White House on live national television and called me a 'nasty, bitter little cripple,' said I was 'slow' and had 'no stamina,' called us 'defective,' and mocked your son Nate as my 'crippled twin brother.' Not only did you build a shrine to the man who hurled derogatory slurs at your own children's wheelchairs on national television, Brenda—you're also deceiving your own friends and customers right here in Suite 756."

I pointed past the plastic jar of five-dollar buttons on the counter to the hidden eight-point disclaimer taped flat against the glass.

"Constituents have been calling my Senate Commerce Committee office all month asking if the rumors are true that these 'Thorne Superstores'—or the local Restore the Republic chapters—are officially vetted, endorsed, and licensed by Garrett Thorne, his company, or his campaign," I said, looking directly at Sharron, Donna, and Carol. "Look behind that button jar on the counter, ladies, and look at the Guangdong wholesale tags inside the collars. Just like the local chapters, this store has zero authorization or endorsement from Garrett Thorne or his campaign. When that couple from Kempsville just handed Brenda a hundred dollars thinking it was going to the President's Senate trial defense fund, every single dime went straight into Brenda's personal bank account."

Donna and Carol stepped up to the glass counter, peered behind the button jar at the tiny eight-point disclaimer, and looked from Brenda's blotchy face to the grinning cardboard cutout of Garrett Thorne.

As mothers and grandmothers themselves, the three women looked at what Brenda had done to make her own two children cut her off—forcing two sixteen-year-old teenagers with failing legs onto a cruise ship, screaming at them when they couldn't walk, retreating to a bottle of gin when they went into wheelchairs, forgetting their forty-second birthday, and then cheering on a man who mocked her own son and daughter on national television while she fleeced her own chapter friends behind a button jar.

In that quiet strip-mall storefront, the entire illusion of the movement they had been pouring their lives into shattered at once.

Sharron stared at the cardboard cutout of Garrett Thorne, and then looked straight across the counter at Brenda in horror.

"Wait—we worshipped a man who has said derogatory things about your own daughter?!" Sharron said, her voice trembling with maternal outrage. "Calling her a cripple, and slow, and saying she has no stamina?! No, Brenda, we can't support this anymore!"

Sharron set the two flags and the coffee mug she had been holding flat down onto the glass counter with a sharp thud—and then reached up to the collar of her cardigan, unpinned her gold-bordered crimson *Restore the Republic — Chapter President* badge, and dropped it onto the glass right in front of Brenda.

"I started our Virginia Beach Restore the Republic chapter three years ago—and just like your phony store, Brenda, President Thorne never endorsed or cared about a single one of us either," Sharron said, her voice shaking with disgust. "I am a mother and a grandmother. Hearing what you did to your own sixteen-year-old twins when they went into wheelchairs—and watching you stand here worshipping a man who calls your own daughter a cripple and slow on national television while you use our members as your personal piggy bank—makes me sick to my stomach."

Beside her, Donna and Carol reached up to their own lapels, unpinned their crimson Restore the Republic pins, and dropped them onto the glass countertop with two sharp metallic clicks.

"We're done with you, Brenda, and we're done with Restore the Republic," Donna said firmly.

"I'm shutting the chapter down tonight," Sharron told Brenda flatly, before turning around in the narrow aisle with Donna and Carol to face Dad, Nate, and me. All three women had tears shining in their eyes.

"David... Katie... Nathaniel," Sharron said softly, her voice thick with genuine remorse as she looked down at our two wheelchairs. "We owe the three of you an apology. We bought into ugly lies on television about a family we should have been proud of all along. What your father did for you two—and what the two of you have built with your lives after what she put you through—is extraordinary."

"We're so sorry, Senator Prairie," Carol said, wiping her cheek with the back of her hand. "Every single one of us—and our husbands—are voting Democrat in the next election, and you have our votes for as long as you run."

"And happy forty-second birthday on Thursday, Katie and Nathaniel," Donna added warmly as the three women stepped to the front door and held it open for us.

"Thank you, Sharron. Thank you, Donna, Carol," I said, genuinely touched as Nate and Dad nodded warmly to all three of them.

Behind the glass counter, Brenda stood frozen in total, trembling silence—abandoned by the family she had mistreated twenty-six years ago and just walked out on by the friends and movement she had traded us for—inside an empty storefront that smelled of cheap vinyl ink and Pho 79's simmering beef broth.

Out on Holland Road beyond the plate-glass window, the amber LED destination sign of the one-thirty-five CVTA Route 36 bus glided into view as it approached the Timberline Shopping Center stop—a bus route that only ran on Saturday afternoons because Nate, Dad, and I had fought to save and expand it sixteen years ago when I first took my seat on the Virginia Beach City Council.

I glanced once more at the stacks of newly delivered cardboard shipping cartons piled behind Brenda's register—every single box stenciled *THORNE: 3-0 UNDEFEATED* and *THORNE 2028*.

"Goodbye, Brenda," I said quietly, turning my SwiftChair smoothly in the narrow aisle alongside Nate's chair as Dad walked beside us toward the open doorway. "And as your United States Senator—I hope you kept the wholesale receipts for those '3-0 Undefeated' and '2028' boxes before the Senate reconvenes on April fourteenth."

The cheap brass bell above the door jangled once more behind us as Nate, Dad, and I rolled out of Suite 756 into the warm, clean Virginia Beach sunshine—waving goodbye to Sharron, Donna, and Carol on the sidewalk while the Route 36 bus kneeled at the curb to take the three of us home to Kempsville.
