## Wednesday & Thursday, April 14–15, 2027 — Convicting Secretary Halverson and Vice President Drayton

![Inside the United States Senate Chamber on April 16, 2027, 42-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie sits at her custom-height mahogany floor desk in her black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchair casting her historic Guilty vote in the impeachment trial while her twin brother Nate (in his SwiftChair) and their father David watch proudly from the Senate Family Gallery.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/apr_2027_us_senate_impeachment_guilty_vote_katie_nate_david.jpg)

Two years earlier, in January of 2025, I had been sitting at my desk in the Senate of Virginia in Richmond when President Garrett Thorne nominated Bret Halverson—the slick, hair-sprayed weekend host of *Patriot Watch Weekend* on the Liberty First Network—to serve as United States Secretary of Defense, and when Vice President Conrad Drayton cast the tie-breaking fiftieth vote to put him in charge of the Pentagon over the objections of every military family in Hampton Roads.

That weekend in 2025, Halverson had gone back on his old cable network for a victory lap, laughing with his former co-hosts about *"that little wheelchair state senator down in Richmond who thought she could stop the Pentagon."*

He wasn't laughing anymore.

On Wednesday afternoon, April 14, 2027, Bret Halverson sat pale and sweating at the respondent's table in the well of the United States Senate Chamber while Chief Justice Jonathan Sterling presided from the marble rostrum and the Reading Clerk called the final roll on Article One and Article Two of his impeachment.

Up on the top tier of the Senate Floor beside the accessible upper doorway, I sat alone at my custom-height mahogany Senator's desk in my compact SwiftChair with my electromagnetic drive brakes locked firm and my red-tabbed impeachment evidence binder resting flat across my lap. Because Senate rules strictly limit the chamber floor during an impeachment trial to sworn United States Senators and officers of the court, my twin brother Nate sat in his matching SwiftChair beside our dad, Brittany, and Chelsea at the brass railing of the Senate Family Gallery directly across the chamber, his blue eyes locked steadily on mine while Brittany kept a quiet, reassuring arm around Chelsea's shoulders.

Down in the well, Halverson's defense attorneys didn't even look up toward my desk. After the House managers had displayed the screenshots of Halverson texting classified Naval Station Norfolk carrier strike group deployment schedules over a commercial phone app alongside the four-hundred-million-dollar Thorne Global Holdings kickback ledger Nate's scripts had uncovered, seventeen institutional Republican Senators from defense and coastal states had announced they were crossing the aisle.

"Mr. Mendoza," the Reading Clerk called out.

"Guilty," Senator Mendoza answered from across the aisle.

The clerk's eyes moved down the alphabetical tally sheet and looked up toward my mahogany desk at the top of the chamber.

"Ms. Prairie."

For half a second, the entire Senate Chamber went dead silent. Down at the respondent's table, Bret Halverson finally raised his head, looking up the tiered steps of the chamber straight at the wheelchair he had mocked on national television two years ago.

I didn't raise my voice, and I didn't take my hands off my armrests—even though beneath the calm wool lapel of my navy blazer, my blood was boiling hot enough to blister paint.

Looking down at Bret Halverson in the well, I wasn't only seeing the cable-television fraud who had texted classified Fifth Fleet deployment coordinates for six thousand Naval Station Norfolk sailors over a commercial phone app and buried four hundred million dollars in offshore kickbacks. I was looking two miles across the Potomac River to the quiet green slopes of Arlington National Cemetery, where my grandfather—Grandpa Walter Prairie, Grandma Ruth's husband, who had served aboard a United States Navy destroyer in the North Atlantic during World War II—slept beneath a plain white marble headstone that Dad, Grandma Ruth, Nate, and I had stood beside in silence since we were little kids.

I was remembering Veterans Day five months ago, watching Halverson and his grinning entourage treat the sacred marble steps at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier like a television green room—laughing and carrying on so loudly over hallowed ground that young Specialist Caleb Navarro of The Old Guard had been forced to halt his twenty-one-step march on the mat, snap to attention, and bark across the plaza at the Secretary of Defense's own party to maintain an atmosphere of silence and respect at all times. *You do not do that.* You do not disrespect the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier—not if you are a tourist off a tour bus, and ten thousand times less if you hold the seal of the Department of Defense. And then, when cell-phone video of an enlisted sentinel reprimanding his group went viral, watching Halverson stage that stomach-turning Pentagon photo-op the very next afternoon—hauling Specialist Navarro across the river in front of press cameras to pin a Joint Service Commendation Medal on his chest and post smug, chest-thumping slogans online—hadn't fooled me for half a second. That medal wasn't honor; it was pure, cynical damage control, turning a disciplined young soldier who had simply defended the dignity of the fallen into a human shield for his own disgrace.

Looking straight down into Halverson's eyes on behalf of Naval Station Norfolk, The Old Guard, and Grandpa Walter resting across the river, I gave him the vote I had promised from Richmond in 2025.

"Guilty," I said clearly across the quiet chamber.

By a vote of seventy-one to twenty-nine—four votes above the two-thirds constitutional threshold required under Article I, Section 3 of the Constitution—the Senate convicted and removed Bret Halverson from office. Ten minutes later, when Majority Leader Charles Schuman moved the second resolution permanently barring Bret Halverson from ever holding any office of honor, trust, or profit under the United States again—ensuring he would never again stand in a chain of command over Arlington National Cemetery or use an American soldier as a damage-control prop—the Reading Clerk called *"Ms. Prairie"* a second time, and with my pulse still blazing behind my ribs, I answered just as clearly:

"Aye."

Twenty-four hours later, on Thursday afternoon, April 15, 2027, the second domino fell.

Confronted with the unredacted Treasury wire tables proving that Vice President Conrad Drayton had personally co-signed the offshore routing waivers for the Naval Station Norfolk logistics kickbacks and sat on the exact same unsecured messaging thread while our Fifth Fleet sailors' movements were compromised, sixty-eight Senators—including me—voted Guilty at four-thirty on Thursday afternoon to convict and remove Conrad Drayton from the Vice Presidency, followed immediately by a second vote permanently disqualifying him from future federal office.

With the Democratic majority in the House of Representatives and our fifty-four-seat majority in the Senate refusing to entertain any Twenty-Fifth Amendment replacement nominee from a disgraced White House, the office of Vice President of the United States stood completely vacant as Thursday night fell over Washington.

## Friday, April 16, 2027, 1:30 PM — The Words of George Mason, James Madison, and Abraham Lincoln

At three o'clock on Friday morning—less than eleven hours before the final Senate deliberation and roll-call vote on the Articles of Impeachment against President Garrett Thorne—the President had gone online from the residence of the White House to unleash one final, desperate tirade against Nate and me.

In all-capital letters, Thorne had called me a *"defective, bitter little cripple on wheels"* and a *"spinster who couldn't keep a man in her twenties and is trying to steal the Presidency from her wheelchair with her shut-in twin brother."*

Even at the very end, Garrett Thorne still didn't understand that every ugly, ableist insult he hurled at my wheelchair and my brother only disgusted more institutional Senators in his own party and cemented the two-thirds coalition against him.

At one-thirty on Friday afternoon, April 16, 2027, inside the closed marble chamber of the United States Senate during the final hour of deliberations before the roll call, I engaged the drive lock on my SwiftChair beside my top-tier mahogany desk, pressed my microphone button, and rested my hands on the edge of my lectern as all ninety-nine of my fellow Senators turned toward my seat.

"Mr. Chief Justice, and my colleagues of the Senate," I said evenly across the silent chamber. "Two hundred and forty years ago, right down the road from where my brother Nate and I grew up in Virginia, George Mason stood at the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia and warned that no man who holds the executive power should ever be placed above the reach of the law when he corrupts his office, asking the delegates: *Shall any man be above justice? Above all, shall that man be above it, who can commit the most extensive injustice?*"

Up in the Family Gallery above the marble columns, Dad rested his hand on Nate's shoulder while Brittany squeezed Chelsea's hand beside them as Nate looked down at me from his SwiftChair.

"And when another Virginian, James Madison, wrote *Federalist Number 51*," I continued, my voice calm and unhurried, "he didn't tell us to trust the personal goodwill of a President or a Cabinet Secretary. Madison wrote: *If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary. In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself.* Madison and the framers gave this Senate the sole power to try all impeachments specifically to guard the republic against men who treat a public trust as a private business."

I looked down into the well toward the respondent's table where President Garrett Thorne's defense lawyers sat staring down at their legal pads.

"Abraham Lincoln once said that nearly all men can stand adversity, but if you want to test a man's character, give him power—and at Gettysburg, he charged every generation of Americans to ensure that government of the people, by the people, for the people, shall not perish from the earth," I said. "We gave Garrett Thorne, Conrad Drayton, and Bret Halverson the highest powers under this Constitution. They used that power to trade tariff waivers and Naval Station Norfolk fleet contracts for four hundred million dollars in offshore kickbacks, to leak the movements of six thousand Virginia sailors on commercial phones, and to mock the disabled citizens they swore an oath to serve. Mason, Madison, and Lincoln left us an exact constitutional remedy for men who fail that test—and when the Clerk calls the roll, I will vote Guilty."

## Friday, April 16, 2027, 2:15 PM — Sixty-Eight Guilty Votes, Permanent Disqualification, & President Clara Morales

At two o'clock, the gallery doors were unlocked to the public and the press corps, and Chief Justice Jonathan Sterling rose in his black judicial robe at the marble rostrum.

"The Secretary will call the roll on Article One of the Articles of Impeachment exhibited by the House of Representatives against Garrett Thorne, President of the United States," Chief Justice Sterling announced.

This was the third time in American history that the House of Representatives had impeached Garrett Thorne—and during his first two impeachments back in his first term, he had escaped removal only because the Senate tally had fallen short of the two-thirds constitutional wall.

This third time, the wall broke.

One by one, as the Reading Clerk called the alphabetical roll, fourteen Republican Senators who had voted to acquit Thorne in his earlier impeachment trials rose from their mahogany desks and answered *"Guilty."*

"Mr. Mendoza," the Reading Clerk called out.

"Guilty," Senator Mendoza answered.

"Ms. Prairie."

I kept my hands resting calmly on the armrests of my SwiftChair, my electromagnetic drive brakes locked tight against the blue Senate carpet. Twenty-one years after Brandon Mercer had switched off my powerchair outside the Williamsburg train station in the pouring November rain and told me I was powerless—and thirteen days after rolling into Suite 756 at Timberline Shopping Center on Holland Road and seeing the shrines and unopened crates of *"3-0 UNDEFEATED"* and *"THORNE 2028"* merchandise my own estranged mother had chosen over her children—I looked straight down into the well of the United States Senate.

"Guilty," I said clearly.

Twenty minutes later, when the Reading Clerk handed the certified tally sheet up to the rostrum showing that sixty-eight Senators had voted Guilty on both Articles of Impeachment—exceeding the sixty-seven-vote two-thirds constitutional threshold required under Article I, Section 3—Chief Justice Sterling struck the marble rostrum once with his ivory gavel.

"The Senate having tried Garrett Thorne, President of the United States, upon two Articles of Impeachment exhibited against him by the House of Representatives, and two-thirds of the Senators present having found him guilty of high crimes and misdemeanors," Chief Justice Sterling declared across the hushed chamber, "it is therefore ordered and adjudged that the said Garrett Thorne be, and he is hereby, removed from office."

Before the defense table could even close their briefcases, Senate Majority Leader Charles Schuman rose and moved the second resolution under Article I, Section 3, Clause 7: permanent disqualification from ever holding any federal office again, which under settled Senate precedent required only a simple majority of fifty-one votes. When the clerk called *"Ms. Prairie"* on the permanent disqualification roll call, I delivered my second vote of the hour just as evenly as the first:

"Aye."

The disqualification resolution passed sixty-eight to thirty-two. With that gavel strike, Garrett Thorne, Conrad Drayton, and Bret Halverson were not only stripped of their offices—they were barred for the rest of their natural lives from ever putting their names on a federal ballot or holding any office of honor, trust, or profit under the United States again.

Looking up across the marble columns at Nate in the Family Gallery as he smiled down at me from his SwiftChair, both of us were thinking the exact same thing: if Garrett Thorne had wanted Mana Canvas's *The Grand Decree* as his unauthorized 2024 theme song so badly before Mana Canvas and Engine Room Records hit his campaign with copyright claims and a cease-and-desist order, the Senate had just given him the broken-music-box reprise from the end of the *Runebreaker* soundtrack.

And because we had convicted and removed Vice President Conrad Drayton on Thursday afternoon and left the Vice Presidency vacant, the Presidential Succession Act of 1947—Title 3, Section 19 of the United States Code—took immediate constitutional effect.

Fifteen minutes after the Senate doors swung open, Nate, Dad, Brittany, and Chelsea took the elevator down from the Family Gallery and rolled or walked down the marble corridor beside my wheelchair into the Mansfield Room just off the Senate Floor.

Nate locked his SwiftChair brakes right alongside mine at the front of the room and reached across the two-inch gap between our armrests to squeeze my hand while Dad rested his hands proudly on our shoulders.

"Clean sweep, Senator," Nate whispered against my ear as the room erupted into applause for President Clara Morales. "All three of them gone for good."

"Every single one, Nate," I whispered back, squeezing my twin brother's hand just as tight.

Twenty minutes later, when Nate, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and I rode the subway car back under Constitution Avenue and rolled into Dirksen Room 511, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins were standing side by side around the reception television with tears in their eyes and smiles from ear to ear.

"When Senator Prairie voted Guilty to convict Garrett Thorne, Conrad Drayton, and Bret Halverson for selling out our Naval Station Norfolk sailors, and when she voted Aye to bar all three of those men from ever holding federal office again, she didn't cast a partisan vote. Broken Katie Prairie is the reason I was removed from office—she destroyed our movement, and no real patriot in Restore the Republic should ever give Broken Katie Prairie a single moment of peace!"*

"Senator Prairie, we monitored former President Thorne's broadcast from Florida in real time," the Capitol Police Inspector said. "Because he singled you out by name and blamed you personally for his conviction and disqualification, our Threat Assessment Section has elevated your protective posture to Tier One. Uniformed Capitol Police officers are now posted outside Room 511 around the clock, and we've already coordinated twenty-four-hour residential patrols with the Alexandria Police Department on Sunset Drive and the Virginia Beach Police Department at your Virginia Beach residences. In addition, the Capitol Police Board has assigned a full-time plainclothes Dignitary Protection detail to accompany you and your household everywhere you travel—and we have an armored Capitol Police van waiting downstairs in the Dirksen courtyard to drive you, Nate, Mr. Prairie, Ms. Fletcher, and Ms. Chelsea home to Alexandria right now."

Dad looked down at Nate and me, a knowing smile tugging at the corner of his mouth, while Brittany kept her arm gently around Chelsea on the sofa and Sam, Mandy, and Sarah watched my wheelchair.

Even smarter, rather than forcing forty-foot transit coaches and through-riders on a five-mile detour off the highway into historic downtown Cape Charles, the planners terminated Route 913 right on U.S. Route 13 and State Route 184 at the Cape Center Market Square plaza—timed to arrive at forty minutes past every hour so riders heading north to Eastville, Exmore, or Onley could step right across the plaza curb onto ShoreLine Transit's northbound Red Route cutaway van at forty-five minutes past the hour as it came out of downtown Cape Charles, while the Purple and Red cutaway loops handled the short shuttle into town.
