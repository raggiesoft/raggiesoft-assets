## Thursday, May 20, 2027, 10:15 AM — The Dirksen Subway, Whispering Across Statuary Hall, & Why Katie Still Isn't Home in May

"Hold onto the brass stanchion pole, Wes—this little electric train runs straight through a tunnel underneath Constitution Avenue!" forty-two-year-old Katie smiled up from her compact black-and-silver SwiftChair as the pneumatic platform doors slid open in the basement of the Dirksen Senate Office Building.

Forty-year-old Wesley Collins blinked twice at the sleek, open-air stainless-steel Senate Subway monorail car waiting flush against the terrazzo curb, tapped his smooth solid-blue silicone ring twice against his thigh in delighted astonishment, and broke into the exact same boyish grin he used to wear at fifteen whenever a kneeling blue-and-yellow bus hissed open outside Emerald Creek Mall.

Right beside him, forty-five-year-old Chelsea giggled happily in her soft lavender cardigan, holding tight to Wesley's right hand as Katie and I rolled our two SwiftChairs side by side onto the accessible center car ahead of sixty-eight-year-old Dad, forty-two-year-old Brittany in her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses, forty-two-year-old Heather and Dan Lindstrom, twenty-one-year-old fraternal twins Jonas and Nora—sitting shoulder to shoulder holding inner hands—and eighteen-year-old Molly, who had finished her spring finals in Saint Paul two days earlier so all six of them could fly east from Minneapolis–Saint Paul to Reagan National and ride the Yellow and Blue Lines up to Capitol Hill.

Five weeks had passed since Chief Justice Jonathan Sterling had sworn former Speaker of the House Clara Morales in as President of the United States inside the Mansfield Room—and eight days had passed since a federal district judge had slapped private citizen Garrett Thorne with an ironclad pre-trial bail gag order. Up in Dirksen Room 511, the switchboard was blissfully quiet again. Before we rode the elevator down to the basement subway platform, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins had warmly welcomed Heather's whole family and locked everyone's cell phones, cameras, canvas backpacks, Nora's tablet bag, and Chelsea's plush green Shelly Turtle safely inside Katie's private inner office—with Brittany promising Chelsea that Shelly Turtle was taking a VIP morning nap right on Katie's mahogany Senate desk chair because strict Senate Gallery rules barred bags, electronics, and stuffed animals upstairs.

Two polite plainclothes officers from Inspector Vance's Capitol Police Dignitary Protection detail—each carrying a loaded blue CapitalPass card in his suit pocket—rode quietly in the trailing subway compartment twenty-five feet behind us without crowding a single member of our family as our electric car glided smoothly down the brightly lit white tunnel into the basement of the United States Capitol.

"First stop on Senator Prairie's official family tour," Katie announced warmly as we rode the wide stone-walled Capitol elevator up to the first floor and rolled into the cool, vaulted sandstone columns of the Capitol Crypt. Guiding her wheelchair up to the inlaid white marble compass star set flush into the center of the floor, Katie invited Wesley, Chelsea, Jonas, Nora, and Molly to touch the brass center point with the toes of their sneakers. "Every street address in Washington—Northwest, Northeast, Southwest, and Southeast—measures right from this single star directly underneath the Capitol Dome. Back in the seventeen-nineties, they originally built a chamber right below this floor for George Washington's tomb before his family decided to keep him home at Mount Vernon in Virginia."

From the Crypt, Katie led all eleven of us up the accessible elevator into the soaring hundred-and-eighty-foot Capitol Rotunda.

Even Dan—who could identify forty species of North American colubrid snakes in the dark without blinking—tilted his bearded chin all the way back until his neck popped, staring straight up at Constantino Brumidi's painted canopy floating nearly twenty stories overhead while Jonas let out a soft, resonant three-note hum of wonder and mute Nora's eyes crinkled into a silent, radiant grin beside Molly.

Then Katie steered us through the tall arched doorway into the semicircular marble colonnade of National Statuary Hall—the old chamber of the House of Representatives—and stopped her SwiftChair right beside a small brass plaque set into the dark marble floor.

"Chelsea, stand right here on John Quincy Adams's brass desk marker with Brittany," Katie said with a playful glint in her blue eyes, before leading Wesley, Heather, Dan, Jonas, Nora, Molly, Dad, and me forty feet across the bustling hall to an unmarked square of stone on the opposite curve of the room. "Back in the eighteen-thirties, the curved plaster ceiling in here acted like a giant acoustic mirror. Go ahead, Brittany—have Chelsea whisper something toward the floor in front of her!"

Forty feet away across a dozen chattering tour groups, Brittany leaned down and pointed gently toward the floor tiles. Chelsea cupped both hands around her mouth and whispered as softly as a summer breeze.

Right beside Wesley's ear—clear as if Chelsea were standing three inches from his cheek—every syllable bounced off the high curved ceiling straight down onto our spot:

*"Hi, Wes, my speshul fwiend! I wuv you!"*

Wesley jumped half an inch in his tan khakis, whipped his head left and right looking for a hidden speaker, and then stared forty feet across the marble hall at a giggling Chelsea before clapping one palm over his heart and laughing a warm, joyous vocal sound that made Heather wipe the corner of her eye.

Right at that moment, two sharp electric buzzes rang out from the ornate golden wall clock mounted above the corridor doorway, lighting up two white stars around its face.

Wesley's happy grin paused. Looking from the buzzing two-star clock to the round gold Senate Member pin on the lapel of Katie's royal-blue blazer, forty-year-old Wesley stepped over beside my right armrest, knitted his sandy eyebrows together in deep, earnest concentration, and tapped my forearm twice.

First, Wesley pointed out the tall Capitol window at the bright emerald-green May oak leaves fluttering in seventy-five-degree sunshine. Second, he held both hands flat in midair and mimicked typing briskly on a computer keyboard—Katie and my lifelong job in Wesley's mental map of the world. Third, he shivered his shoulders like January snow, pointed squarely at Katie's suit blazer, held up both palms in an unmistakable *What on earth is going on?!* shrug, and let out a long, genuinely baffled upward hum toward Heather and me.

Heather let out an affectionate laugh, resting a gentle hand on her cousin's elbow.

"Okay, Katie, Nate—Wes has been asking Dan and me this on the calendar all week back in Maplewood, and clearly seeing the statues hasn't cleared it up," Heather admitted warmly. "Back when Katie was a Delegate and a State Senator in Richmond, Wes finally figured out after watching you on television in 2016 that Katie went up to Richmond for six or eight weeks when there was snow on the ground, and by spring break she was right back home in Virginia Beach typing computer code with Nate. Virginia's General Assembly is a part-time citizen legislature—so Wes still doesn't fully understand what Katie actually *does* for a living up here, or why it's almost Memorial Day and she's still working in a giant marble museum all year round!"

Katie didn't laugh at Wesley or simplify a single word into baby-talk; instead, she rolled her SwiftChair six inches closer so she was looking straight up into Wesley's brown eyes with complete respect.

"That's actually a really smart question, Wes," Katie told him warmly. "In Richmond, Virginia's state legislature only meets in the wintertime—forty-six days one year and sixty days the next—so lawmakers go back to their regular jobs in the spring. The United States Senate is completely different. Up here in Washington, a hundred Senators—two from Virginia, two from Minnesota, and two from every other state—work almost all year round making laws and funding buses, hospitals, and schools for the whole country. And remember last month on television when the old Speaker of the House, Clara Morales, raised her right hand and became the new President of the United States?"

Wesley nodded once, tapping his blue silicone ring thoughtfully as he remembered watching the Mansfield Room broadcast in Maplewood.

"Well, for the first three months I worked in this building, the man in the White House refused to help disabled people or public transit," Katie explained clearly. "Now that former Speaker Clara Morales is President of the United States, whenever we pass a good bill downstairs on that blue carpet—like the public-bus bridge-tunnel bill I'm voting on after lunch—President Morales actually signs her name to make it the law!"

Wesley studied Katie's wheelchair, looked up again at the buzzing golden clock with its two lit white stars, and gave a slow, game nod—clearly still half-convinced that ringing bells in a statue building was a mighty strange way for a computer programmer to earn a paycheck, yet beaming with pride for his buddy Nate's twin sister all the same.

## Thursday, May 20, 2027, 1:45 PM — Holding Hands in the Senate Gallery & The Promise on the East Front Plaza

![From inside the elevated brass-railed United States Senate Gallery on Thursday afternoon, May 20, 2027, 40-year-old Wesley Collins in his forest-green collared polo shirt with his blue silicone ring and 45-year-old Chelsea Prairie in her lavender cardigan sit side by side holding hands warmly behind the brass gallery rail—flanked by Nathaniel Prairie in his SwiftChair, David Prairie, Brittany Fletcher, Heather and Dan Lindstrom, fraternal twins Jonas and Nora holding inner hands, and 18-year-old Molly—watching 42-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie speak from her SwiftChair beside her top-tier mahogany desk on the blue Senate carpet below.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/may_2027_us_senate_gallery_wesley_chelsea_holding_hands_katie_floor.jpg)

At a quarter to two—after eating grilled turkey melts together down in the Dirksen cafeteria—Katie rolled through the tall brass doors onto the Senate Floor while I chose to ride the third-floor gallery elevator upstairs with both of our families so Wesley and Chelsea could sit right beside me.

"Remember, everyone—quiet voices all the way off, and no leaning over the brass rail," a soft-spoken Senate Gallery Doorkeeper reminded our group kindly with a welcoming smile as he unhooked the velvet cord to the front accessible gallery bay directly across the marble chamber from Katie's top-tier mahogany desk.

Locking my SwiftChair brakes inside the widened wheelchair bay beside Dad and Brittany while Jonas, Nora, Molly, Dan, and Heather settled smoothly into the cushioned dark-wood pew to our right, I watched forty-year-old Wesley and forty-five-year-old Chelsea sit side by side right at the center behind the polished brass gallery rail.

Without needing a plush toy or a distraction screen, Chelsea immediately slipped her left hand into Wesley's right hand, lacing her fingers snugly beside his smooth blue silicone ring, and rested their joined hands calmly atop his knee as both of them looked down into the sunlit chamber of the United States Senate.

Down on the royal-blue patterned carpet below the cream marble Ionic columns, Katie sat upright in her matching black-and-silver SwiftChair beside her custom-height mahogany desk. When the Presiding Officer tapped the gavel and recognized *"the junior Senator from Virginia,"* Katie clicked on her lectern microphone—her clear Tidewater voice rising effortlessly up to our gallery pew without a single sheet of staff-written boilerplate in front of her.

"Madam President," Katie said evenly across the chamber, closing floor debate on the bipartisan Federal Rural and Bridge-Tunnel Transit Connectivity Act she and Senior Senator Thomas Keane had authored with Eastern Shore and coastal lawmakers, "for sixty-three years since the Chesapeake Bay Bridge-Tunnel opened across the mouth of the Chesapeake Bay, anyone without a private automobile trying to cross seventeen miles of open water between Virginia's Eastern Shore and Hampton Roads—to reach cancer treatment in Norfolk, classes at the Community College of Hampton Roads, or a shipyard shift—hit a wall of water with zero public transit across it. By dedicating Section 5311 intercity transit operating funds, capping public-bus bridge tolls at a single dollar, and requiring hundred-percent cross-honored transit passes on both shores, this Senate is proving today that public infrastructure belongs to every citizen—including those of us who roll up a hydraulic bus ramp instead of driving a car."

Beside me behind the brass rail, Wesley had squinted in earnest bewilderment through the ten minutes of arcane parliamentary Latin and *"reserving the right to object"* that had preceded Katie's turn—still visibly mystified why grown men and women in dark suits kept calling each other *"my distinguished friend from Delaware"* instead of just using first names.

Yet the instant Katie spoke the words *public transit*, *across the water*, and *roll up a hydraulic bus ramp*, Wesley's eyebrows shot up in instant, crystal-clear recognition.

*Buses! Wheelchair ramps! Helping people ride the bus!*

Ten minutes later, when the Reading Clerk called the alphabetical roll on final passage and called out *"Ms. Prairie,"* Katie kept her hands steady on her armrests and answered clearly across the quiet chamber:

"Aye."

Remembering the Doorkeeper's rule not to clap or call out from the gallery, Wesley kept his lips pressed heroically shut—turning his beaming, sunlit face straight toward Chelsea and giving her laced hand two firm, triumphant squeezes while Chelsea squeezed right back with shining eyes and mouthed a silent *"Yay Katie!"* behind the brass rail. Down on the floor, once the Presiding Officer announced final passage seventy-nine to twenty-one to send the bill down Pennsylvania Avenue to President Clara Morales's desk, Katie allowed herself one brief, rule-compliant upward glance toward our gallery bay—sharing a tiny, unmistakable twin smile with me and with Wesley and Chelsea's joined hands before rolling smoothly out toward the Senate Reception Room to meet us.

Twenty minutes later, once Sam and Mandy brought everyone's bags and Shelly Turtle down to the sunny marble steps of the East Front Capitol plaza, forty-five-year-old Chelsea stood hugging Shelly Turtle under one arm while keeping her other hand locked tightly in Wesley's—neither of them wanting to let go even for a snapshot in front of the white cast-iron Capitol Dome.

"Look at the two of them," Dan murmured warmly beside Dad, before glancing at his wristwatch with a quiet, reluctant sigh. "We've got to catch our Sunday afternoon flight back to Minneapolis so Wes is back at *North Lion* Monday morning—and so we stay well clear of the thirty-day out-of-state Medicaid waiver cutoff."

"And clear of Social Security's holding-out rules," Heather added quietly, looking from Wesley's blue silicone ring to Katie's wheelchair with the candid trust twenty-four years had finally earned between us. "Every single time Wes and Chelsea visit across state lines, Dan and I and Uncle David have to count calendar squares and keep separate receipts just so some field auditor doesn't pull out a marriage-penalty questionnaire and try to slash their benefits for holding hands like a couple."

Out on the sunlit limestone plaza—with the avenue stretching west toward a White House where Clara Morales now sat behind the Resolute Desk instead of Garrett Thorne, a White House that Chelsea, Brittany, and Heather's family hadn't ever visited yet—Katie locked her electromagnetic brakes beside mine and looked straight at Wesley and Chelsea standing shoulder to shoulder in the May breeze.

"Then let's stop counting calendar squares just to keep two people who love each other safe," my twin sister said quietly, that unmistakable legislative iron settling behind her blue eyes. "Now that Speaker Morales is President of the United States and Leader Van Deren holds the Senate gavel, give Nate, Sam, Mandy, Sarah, and me twelve months to build the budget math. Next spring, we're repealing the Supplemental Security Income marriage and holding-out penalties and passing nationwide Medicaid waiver portability—and when President Morales signs it into law, we're bringing all of you to the White House for the very first time to watch her do it."
