## Wednesday, July 21, 2027, 11:39 AM — ARTS Train 51 Out of Union Station & Eighty-Four Pages of Bureaucratic Smoke

![Inside the 1970s red-brick and terrazzo front visitor sign-in lobby of the Bates-Harding Building at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in July 2027, 42-year-old U.S. Senator Katie Prairie and her twin brother Nate sit side by side in their black motorized SwiftChair power wheelchairs wearing casual polos, t-shirts, and jeans as an icy, determined Katie speaks directly to Governor Rebecca Langford and Chief of Staff Arthur Lawrence on speakerphone while Director Evelyn Cross and her panicked staff look on beside a stack of yellow Level 2 passes.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/jul_2027_rrc_unannounced_visit_calling_governor_katie_nate.jpg)

"Watch the yellow lip plate there, folks—you're clear right into the coach vestibule," the uniformed ARTS conductor called out over the low hum of the locomotive on Track 24 at Washington Union Station, unlatching the safety chain on the blue hand-cranked mobile wheelchair lift at eleven-thirty-two on Wednesday morning so Katie and I could glide our SwiftChairs one behind the other into the accessible end of the *Cardinal*.

Neither the conductor nor a single passenger boarding westbound Train 51 gave the two of us a second glance, which was the entire point.

Nine days after Inspector Marcus Vance had stood down our round-the-clock Capitol Police protective detail, Katie and I were traveling the way we had traveled our whole adult lives—just the two of us, side by side, with zero advance staff, zero press advisories, and not a suit jacket or Senate lapel pin in sight. Katie wore a plain navy cotton V-neck polo shirt, dark jeans, and white sneakers; I wore a heather-grey crew-neck t-shirt, jeans, and canvas sneakers. Clipped to the rear uprights of our SwiftChairs were our two everyday daypacks, and strapped snug across the luggage rack behind my seatback was a dark-green compression sack holding our rolled flannel-lined double sleeping bag for tonight's hotel room in Staunton.

At eleven-thirty-nine sharp, the twin air horns sounded softly beneath the concrete deck, the couplers gave a gentle metallic tug, and Train 51 eased southward through the First Street Tunnel toward the Long Bridge across the Potomac River.

Once the train cleared our weekday stop in Alexandria and picked up speed past Manassas toward the Piedmont foothills, I reached into my backpack, pulled out an eighty-four-page glossy white comb-bound report stamped with the seal of the Virginia Department for Aging and Rehabilitative Services, and laid it flat across the wide drop-down accessible table in front of our locked wheelchairs.

"Listen to how Commissioner Sterling and Director Cross phrased Section Three on page twenty-nine," I murmured, angling the pages so Katie could read right alongside me without anyone two rows ahead overhearing. "In bold type at the top of the page, they certify to the Senate Appropriations Subcommittee that all five of your February seventeenth mandates have reached *Substantial Phase-One Implementation* ahead of next Thursday's full fiscal year 2028 markup, and formally ask your office to release the eighteen-point-five-million-dollar hold."

Katie rested her chin on two fingers, her blue eyes scanning down the dense paragraphs below the heading. "Now read the actual sentences underneath the bold header, Nate."

"On wheelchair bodily autonomy and client handbooks," I read quietly, tracing the lines with my index finger, "*Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center has convened an interdisciplinary Stakeholder Policy Revision Working Group scheduled to circulate draft handbook language for administrative comment during the first quarter of 2028.* On ending mandatory two-week manual-shop Vocational Evaluation tours for college-degreed students and unlocking the developer workstations in the Bates-Harding computer lab: *DARS Vocational Services has initiated a pilot waiver scoping study, and VITA has logged an Enterprise Architecture Exception Request pending quarterly security board review.* And on abolishing yellow Level 2 off-campus passes and adult sibling separation contracts in Barton Hall: *Residential Operations is evaluating modernized community-sign-out workflows.*"

Katie closed the white plastic comb binder with a crisp, quiet snap.

"Five months, Nate," she said softly, looking out the wide coach window as green Piedmont pastures flashed past the glass. "Five months since Marcus Sterling and Evelyn Cross sat behind those microphones in Dirksen Room 124 and promised under oath—in front of Natalie and Mrs. Brooks—that they would carry out every single one of those five reforms. Beyond locking down perimeter badge access and turning Christopher Vance's unredacted 2007 files over to the Virginia State Police because state detectives and Chairman Harrington had a Friday five-o'clock clock hanging over their heads, everything affecting how disabled adults are treated inside Fishersville was buried in fake subcommittees—hoping nobody in Washington would check before next Thursday's markup."

"And if Sarah or Sam had called Richmond yesterday to schedule an official Senate oversight visit," I said, resting my right hand over Katie's across the two-inch gap between our armrests, "Sterling and Cross would have spent forty-eight hours hiding every yellow pass clipboard in a locked filing cabinet, taping temporary notices over the lobby racks, parking two VITA technicians in the computer lab with unlocked loaner laptops, and lining up five rehearsed administrators at the visitor sign-in desk."

"Which is why nobody in Richmond, nobody at DARS, and nobody in Fishersville knows we got on this train," Katie replied evenly, lacing her fingers through mine. "And why coming down a day early on Wednesday afternoon was the only way to do it right. If you try to ride down from Washington on the morning of an inspection, Train 51 doesn't pull into Staunton until past three-thirty in the afternoon, when the Bates-Harding classrooms are already letting out for the day. Sleeping over in Staunton tonight puts us on the eight-thirty Route 250 Connector bus tomorrow morning right alongside the morning commuters—rolling up to the front visitor lobby at eight-fifty-two like any other pair of forty-two-year-old wheelchair riders in jeans, and only showing our ID when the sign-in desk requires it."

All afternoon, as the *Cardinal* wound past Culpeper, Orange, and Charlottesville before climbing the steep eastern grade of Afton Mountain and plunging through the dark stone bore of the Blue Ridge Tunnel into the Shenandoah Valley, Katie and I worked quietly side by side on my MagnaBook Pro—cross-checking every page of DARS's glossy compliance binder against the verbatim *Congressional Record* transcript from February seventeenth.

At three-thirty-four in the afternoon, the station brakes hissed gently beside the historic red-brick platform at Staunton Station in the Wharf district, one short block uphill from the Staunton Hub on Lewis Street. After the station agent cranked us down the mobile lift onto the warm sunlit bricks, Katie and I rolled across the quiet cobblestone crossing into the historic brick hotel beside the tracks, checked into our ground-floor accessible room under my credit card without mentioning the United States Senate once, shared a quiet supper of grilled chicken sandwiches and two ice-cold bottles of ginger ale, and unrolled our flannel-lined double sleeping bag inside the wide, carpeted ground-floor coat closet. Curling up side by side in the dark with Katie's head tucked under my chin and our two SwiftChairs plugged into the wall outlet outside the closet door, we slept nine uninterrupted hours before the morning bus.

## Thursday, July 22, 2027, 8:30 AM — Fifty Cents on the Route 250 Connector & The Front Visitor Sign-In Lobby

The air suspension of the white-and-green Shenandoah Valley Transit cutaway coach knelt with a soft pneumatic sigh against the concrete curb of the Staunton Hub on Lewis Street at eight-twenty-six on Thursday morning, and the driver stepped out in his short-sleeved uniform shirt to unfold the front wheelchair ramp.

"Morning, y'all—heading out toward Fishersville, the rehab center, or Waynesboro?" the operator asked cheerfully as Katie and I rolled up the aluminum incline one after the other.

"Roosevelt Avenue in Fishersville, please," Katie smiled, dropping a single shiny quarter into the farebox coin slot for the twenty-five-cent half-fare disability rate on the fifty-cent valley route right before I dropped my own quarter in behind her.

Once the operator clicked the four retracting SafeLock floor straps onto our black SwiftChair frames and pulled away from Lewis Street at eight-thirty sharp onto U.S. Route 250 East, we didn't even have to wait until we reached the front visitor desk to find out how much of Commissioner Sterling's eighty-four-page binder was fiction.

Sitting directly across the center aisle from our wheelchairs were two twenty-something Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center residents riding back from an early-morning pharmacy run in downtown Staunton: a twenty-three-year-old woman named Kayla with dark curly hair, a plastic resident photo keycard hanging on a lanyard around her neck, and silver forearm crutches propped neatly between her knees; and a twenty-four-year-old guy named Trevor wearing his own resident keycard lanyard over a faded Blue Ridge Community College sweatshirt with a sketch tube across his lap.

Clipped right next to Kayla's plastic door badge—flapping lightly in the cool air-conditioning vent every time the bus crested a rolling valley hill—was a crisp, freshly signed, canary-yellow rectangular slip of cardstock stamped across the top in heavy black ink: *BARTON HALL — LEVEL 2 OFF-CAMPUS HALL PASS*.

Beside me, Katie didn't stiffen or stare; she just gave my pinky finger the tiniest tap across our inner armrests and smiled warmly across the aisle when Kayla glanced over at our powerchairs.

"Those compact black chairs turn way tighter than the loaner clunkers they've got down at the center," Kayla remarked appreciatively, nodding toward our SwiftChair joysticks. "Are you two heading in for a nine-o'clock intake appointment at Fishersville today?"

"Just stopping by the front desk this morning to check on a few things," Katie said gently, without a single untruth. "My brother Nate and I actually spent twelve days in Barton Hall twenty years ago before we went home and started our own software business. Don't tell me they're still making grown adults carry signed yellow paper Hall Passes just to ride a fifty-cent bus into Staunton."

Trevor let out a bark of dry, incredulous laughter while Kayla rolled her eyes toward the ceiling of the bus.

"Oh, they upgraded the outer doors with new electronic badge readers after that big Senate hearing back in February so visitors can't get past the front lobby without signing in," Kayla said, flicking the corner of the canary-yellow paper with her thumb, "but for us residents? Not a single rule changed. We still have to beg the morning tech in Barton Hall to sign a yellow Level 2 slip before we step off campus, and the second we get off this bus we have to get it time-stamped at the front lobby window before nine o'clock—otherwise the dorm supervisor threatens to drop us down to a two-week Level 1 restriction where you aren't allowed past the courtyard benches."

"Wait until you hear the best part," Trevor chimed in, leaning forward on his elbows as the green pastures of Augusta County rolled past the windows. "Back in February, when Virginia's U.S. Senator tore the DARS bosses apart on television over grabbing wheelchair handles, locking down the computers, and forcing college graduates into manual trade shops, everybody in the dorms watched the clip on their phones and thought things were finally going to change."

"And did anything change inside the classrooms?" I asked evenly.

"Not a thing," Trevor snorted. "I graduated in May with an Associate of Applied Science in graphic and web design—three-point-eight GPA, full portfolio—and enrolled here strictly for an assistive-screen-magnifier accommodation setup and advanced front-end certification. Instead, Vocational Evaluation in the Bates-Harding Building forced me to spend all last week sorting lug nuts in the auto bay and folding hospital corners in the nursing lab. Yesterday afternoon—literally at three-fifteen yesterday—my evaluator looked across her desk at my college transcript and told me that if coding stylesheets strained my eyes too much, the center still has its wonderful retail-readiness partnership down the road in Waynesboro training clients to stand by the automatic doors as a ValueMart greeter."

Across the two inches between our wheelchairs, I felt the muscles in Katie's forearm go dead still against her armrest, though her voice stayed warm and steady for the two students across the aisle.

"What about the Web and Software classroom in Bates-Harding?" I asked. "Did VITA finally unlock local developer permissions on the state desktop computers?"

"Are you kidding?" Trevor laughed, shaking his head. "My roommate's in that class right now. Every student still has to haul their own personal laptop from home and daisy-chain orange surge strips across the floor because VITA still has every state desktop locked down tighter than a bank vault. When the instructor asked administration about it on Monday, the Assistant Director told him Richmond parked all of the Senator's Washington demands inside a *2028 Strategic Transition Task Force* until after Congress passes their budget next week."

"And heaven help anybody in a wheelchair who tells staff not to shove their chair," Kayla added quietly as the green highway sign for Fishersville came into view ahead. "Last Thursday morning right inside the Bates-Harding visitor lobby, an intake supervisor walked up behind our friend Marcus, grabbed his push-handles without asking, and yanked him backward so a tour group could pass. When Marcus told him to take his hands off his chair, the guy threatened him with an aggression write-up and pointed straight at the old 2019 handbook rack in the lobby."

At eight-fifty-two in the morning, the automated chime dinged above the windshield, and the SVT Route 250 Connector pulled onto the concrete bus pad along Roosevelt Avenue outside the sprawling red-brick campus of Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center.

"Thanks for the ride—and good luck in class today, Kayla and Trevor," Katie said warmly as the driver unclipped our SafeLock straps and lowered the ramp onto the sunlit sidewalk.

"Nice meeting you guys!" Kayla smiled, swinging her crutches briskly up the paved front walk ahead of us toward the main entrance of the Bates-Harding Building without the faintest idea that she had just handed a live field briefing to the sitting United States Senator holding an eighteen-point-five-million-dollar padlock over the entire facility.

Side by side in our jeans and casual shirts, Katie and I rolled up the gentle concrete approach and glided through the aluminum-framed glass front entrance doors into the public visitor sign-in lobby.

Just as Kayla had told us on the bus, nobody could wander unbadged into the campus corridors anymore: twenty feet past the red-brick lobby pillar stood a raised security podium beside a set of badge-locked interior doors manned by a uniformed security attendant.

Yet RRC didn't even bother hiding its foot-dragging behind the locked doors—it was sitting right out in the open inside the front entrance lobby.

Directly ahead of us at the sliding glass reception window, Kayla handed her canary-yellow *Barton Hall Level 2 Off-Campus Hall Pass* under the glass so the receptionist could thump it with a mechanical time-stamp clock—right beside a tall, four-inch-thick stack of blank canary-yellow Level 2 pass pads—before Kayla and Trevor tapped their resident lanyards at the inner security podium and disappeared through the locked hallway doors toward class. And mounted to the cinder-block wall beside the visitor sign-in counter was the public intake rack stuffed with crisp blue client handbooks stamped *REVISED OCTOBER 2019* across the bottom corner, right underneath a laminated sign taped to the reception glass warning incoming clients and visiting relatives that Level 2 off-campus pass sign-outs and eleven-o'clock opposite-gender dorm lockouts were enforced without exception for adult siblings and family members.

Katie and I rolled quietly up to the glass visitor sign-in counter and paused so I could pull one of the blue October 2019 handbooks off the public rack, flipping straight to the residential conduct pages—not an addendum sheet, sticker, or wheelchair bodily-autonomy clause anywhere inside it. Beside me, looking from Kayla's time-stamped canary-yellow slip to the unamended 2019 handbook in my hand, Katie's jaw set hard, her blue eyes turning to pure ice.

"Good morning—if the two of you are signing in as visitors, I just need you to print your names on the log and slide a government photo ID under the window," the receptionist behind the glass said pleasantly without looking up from her keyboard.

Katie reached into the front pocket of her dark jeans, and I reached for my wallet—when the aluminum-framed outer front entrance doors swung open behind our wheelchairs, letting in a gust of warm July air from the staff parking lot.

## Thursday, July 22, 2027, 9:00 AM — Director Cross Arrives for Work, Signing In at the Front Window, & Calling Governor Langford Directly

Walking briskly through the front entrance doors with her leather work tote over one shoulder and a travel mug of coffee in her hand—arriving right on the dot of nine o'clock to start her workday alongside Assistant Director Greg Saunders in his crisp light-grey button-down shirt—was Director Evelyn Cross.

"Did Richmond confirm whether Dirksen Room 511 cleared our hold memo yet, Greg?" Dr. Cross was asking cheerfully as the glass door hissed shut behind her heels.

Then Dr. Cross glanced toward the front visitor sign-in window—and stopped dead in her tracks on the green-and-white terrazzo floor.

For three full seconds, she stared at the back-quarter profile of our two matching black motorized SwiftChairs in casual jeans, sneakers, a navy polo, and a grey t-shirt as if her eyes were playing a cruel trick on her. Slowly, Katie and I pivoted our wheelchairs forty-five degrees from the sign-in counter to look straight at her—and one single look at the cold, silent fury written across Katie's face drained every drop of color from Evelyn Cross's cheeks.

"S-Senator Prairie?" Dr. Cross breathed nervously, her voice catching in her throat as Saunders froze solid beside her with his clipboard clutched against his chest. "...Nate?"

"Good morning, Dr. Cross," Katie said, her voice quiet, level, and cold as iron—without a trace of polite small talk.

Neither Katie nor I skipped a single line of the building's security procedure. Turning calmly back to the startled receptionist behind the glass while Dr. Cross and Assistant Director Saunders looked on in dawning horror, we printed both of our names onto the paper visitor log at nine-oh-one and slid our photo identification under the window slot just like any other visitors signing into a state facility: my horizontal Virginia DMV State ID card on the left—since under federal anti-nepotism law I never held a Senate employee badge, using my Sergeant at Arms family-companion pass strictly inside the Capitol complex and my Virginia State ID everywhere else as a private citizen—and, right beside it on the right, Katie's official wallet-sized United States Senate Member Identification Card issued by the Senate Sergeant at Arms and embossed with the gold Seal of the United States Senate.

Behind the reception glass, the young woman at the counter looked down at the gold Senate seal and holographic portrait beside Katie's name, looked out through the glass at Katie in her navy polo and jeans, and went nearly as pale as her boss. With trembling fingers, she slid both ID cards back under the slot along with two clip-on *VISITOR — ESCORT REQUIRED* badges.

"W-we had no idea—nobody in Richmond told us your office was coming to Fishersville today, Senator!" Dr. Cross stammered, taking a nervous half-step toward the counter.

"That's why it's called an unannounced visit, Dr. Cross," I said evenly, clipping my visitor pass to the collar of my grey t-shirt as Katie clipped hers to the placket of her polo. "Now that we're signed in and badged, we need fifteen minutes behind your security doors—escorted by you and Mr. Saunders so every campus security rule is followed to the letter. Take us straight to the Web and Software Applications classroom window and the Vocational Evaluation suite."

With zero minutes of lead time to send a runner ahead or hide a single extension cord, a visibly shaken Dr. Cross tapped her director's master badge against the security reader beside the guard podium and led our two wheelchairs down the interior corridor—while behind us in the front lobby, the reception staff didn't wait five seconds after the security door clicked shut before snatching up the office telephone to call DARS headquarters in Richmond in a full-blown panic.

Fifteen minutes behind the badge doors was five minutes more than we needed.

Through the wire-reinforced observation glass of the Web and Software Applications lab, Dr. Cross had to stand in mortified silence right beside our wheelchairs as Katie and I counted seven disabled adult students huddled over personal laptops plugged into daisy-chained orange floor surge strips while twelve state-issued desktop towers sat dark and unusable along the cinder-block wall beneath blue VITA asset tags. Two doors down in the Vocational Evaluation intake suite, sitting in a neat wire tray right beside the evaluator sign-in desk, was a freshly printed stack of glossy recruitment flyers for the Waynesboro ValueMart *Retail Greeter Readiness Partnership* alongside mandatory two-week manual-shop rotation checklists for incoming degree-holders.

At nine-twenty, Katie didn't ask to see another room. Without a word, she and I turned our SwiftChairs around in tight parallel formation and rolled right back out through the security podium into the public front entrance lobby.

Behind the glass reception window—right beside the four-inch stack of canary-yellow Level 2 passes—one staffer was clutching the beige office handset tightly to her ear on hold with DARS executive offices in Richmond while a second alarmed staffer stood beside the filing cabinets clutching two manila compliance folders to her chest.

"Senator Prairie, please," Dr. Cross pleaded breathlessly, hurrying out into the terrazzo front lobby beside a pale, silent Greg Saunders as we locked our wheelchair brakes near the red-brick reception pillar. "If your office had just let Commissioner Sterling know you were coming, we could have walked you through the timeline! Our stakeholder working groups are meeting biweekly on the 2019 handbook, VITA has our classroom exception ticket queued for next quarter's security board, and Commissioner Sterling's office in Richmond is pulling the Secretary onto the phone right now—"

"Stop talking, Dr. Cross," Katie said quietly.

She didn't raise her voice by a single decibel, yet the ice-cold fury behind those four words froze everyone in the front lobby where they stood.

Sitting straight in her SwiftChair in her navy cotton polo and jeans while I flipped open my MagnaBook Pro across my knees to our timestamped morning notes, Katie looked straight across the terrazzo floor at Dr. Cross and Assistant Director Saunders.

"Forty-eight hours ago, you and Commissioner Sterling delivered an eighty-four-page signed certification to my office on the Senate Appropriations Committee claiming that Phase One of all five statutory reforms you promised under oath on February seventeenth was substantially implemented—asking me to release eighteen and a half million federal tax dollars in next Thursday's markup," Katie said, every word landing like cold steel. "Yesterday afternoon, Nate and I rode ARTS Train 51 down to Staunton in our regular clothes, stayed overnight, and paid twenty-five cents apiece to ride the eight-thirty Route 250 Connector bus onto Roosevelt Avenue this morning. You fixed your front door locks because the cameras shamed you into it—and then you parked every single reform protecting the dignity and education of disabled adults inside fake *2028 task forces* while telling your instructors you only had to wait until Congress passed your budget."

Dr. Cross swallowed hard, unable to speak.

"In less than thirty minutes," Katie continued evenly, "we watched a twenty-three-year-old resident on crutches get a canary-yellow Level 2 Hall Pass time-stamped at that window under threat of a two-week Level 1 lock-in, and heard how an intake supervisor right here in this lobby grabbed a wheelchair user's push-handles without consent last Thursday and threatened him with an aggression write-up. We spoke to an honors web-design college graduate whom your staff forced to sort lug nuts in the auto bay all last week before pitching him yesterday afternoon on standing by the doors of the Waynesboro ValueMart as a greeter. We pulled an unamended October 2019 handbook right off your front lobby rack under a laminated eleven-o'clock adult-sibling lockout sign. And twenty seconds down that hall, twelve VITA-locked desktops are still gathering dust while students code over floor extension cords."

Behind the glass window, the receptionist cupped her hand anxiously over the telephone mouthpiece and leaned toward the slot: "Dr. Cross—DARS Richmond has Commissioner Sterling's deputy on the line asking what he should tell the Secretary—"

"Tell your receptionist she can hang up on DARS, Dr. Cross," Katie said flatly, sliding her dark mPhone out of her jeans pocket. "If Commissioner Sterling thinks five months of foot-dragging and a dishonest binder to the United States Senate is how state agencies treat an Appropriations hold, Nate and I aren't wasting another breath on DARS."

During her final year in the Senate of Virginia in Richmond—and throughout every mile of the 2026 statewide campaign after Virginia voters elected fellow Democrat Rebecca Langford to the Governor's Mansion—Katie and Governor Langford had forged a direct, zero-drama working partnership built on mutual trust and plain speaking. Neither woman ever called the other's personal cell for small talk during a legislative week; when Senator Prairie called Governor Langford directly, it was time to talk business.

Right there in the front visitor lobby in front of Dr. Cross, Assistant Director Saunders, and the panicked staff behind the glass window, Katie tapped *Rebecca Langford* on her screen and held the phone up on speakerphone.

The line rang once. Halfway through the second ring, it clicked open.

"Katie! Good morning," Governor Rebecca Langford's crisp, warm voice filled the terrazzo front lobby. "Tell me you and Nate aren't already buried in Appropriations markup spreadsheets before ten o'clock on a Thursday."

"Good morning, Rebecca," Katie said clearly, holding the matte-black phone steady near her chin while I kept our audit log open on my laptop beside her. "Nate is right here beside me, and I want to let you know up front that you're on speakerphone—because Nate and I aren't in Washington. Yesterday afternoon we took the ARTS train down to Staunton, and at eight-fifty-two this morning we rolled off the Route 250 Connector bus in street clothes into the front visitor lobby at Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center in Fishersville, where Dr. Evelyn Cross, her Assistant Director Greg Saunders, and her front-desk staff are standing in front of us right now."

Over the speaker grille, the shift in atmospheric pressure from Richmond was instantaneous. Every trace of casual morning warmth vanished into pure, razor-sharp executive command.

"Hold five seconds while I shut my office door and conference Arthur Lawrence onto this line with us, Katie," Governor Langford said briskly. Two crisp clicks sounded over the speaker, followed immediately by Chief of Staff Arthur Lawrence's voice joining from Capitol Square:

"Senator Prairie, Nate—I'm on with the Governor. Go ahead. Commissioner Sterling sent the Governor's Office a signed memo on Tuesday afternoon swearing up and down that Fishersville had completed Phase One of every single mandate from your February hearing ahead of next week's markup."

"Commissioner Sterling's memo lied to the Governor and lied to the Senate Appropriations Committee, Arthur," Katie replied evenly.

Without raising her voice or adding a single word of embellishment, Katie walked Governor Langford and Chief of Staff Lawrence through every fact we had verified on the bus, at the front visitor sign-in window, and on our fifteen-minute escorted walk behind the security doors—while I read out the exact form titles, the October 2019 handbook revision stamp from the lobby rack, Kayla's time-stamped canary-yellow Level 2 pass, Marcus's wheelchair-handle write-up threat in the lobby the previous week, Trevor's three-fifteen Wednesday ValueMart greeter pitch, and the twelve dark VITA-tagged desktop towers in the classroom.

Behind the reception glass to Katie's left, the receptionist quietly lowered the DARS Richmond telephone receiver onto her shoulder, staring out through the window in stunned silence. There was no point begging DARS headquarters for talking points when the Governor of Virginia and her Chief of Staff were speaking live into the front lobby.

When I finished reading the last line from my screen, two seconds of icy silence came over the line from Richmond.

"Dr. Cross," Governor Langford's voice cut across the front lobby like a gavel strike. "I assume you can hear Arthur and me clearly?"

Dr. Cross swallowed visibly, staring at the phone in Katie's hand as her fingers trembled at her sides. "Y-yes, Governor Langford. Yes, Mr. Lawrence."

"Listen to me very carefully, Dr. Cross," the Governor said, her tone clinical and utterly implacable. "Your front desk can hang up with DARS headquarters in Richmond, because Arthur just summoned Commissioner Marcus Sterling and the Secretary of Health and Human Resources across Capitol Square to my office in ten minutes to explain to me face-to-face why an agency of my administration submitted a false compliance certification to Virginia's United States Senator—and anyone whose signature is on a false representation to Congress will have their resignation on my desk before five o'clock today."

Across the terrazzo floor, Dr. Cross went ashen, while beside her Assistant Director Saunders stared straight ahead at the brick column without moving a muscle.

"Second," Chief of Staff Arthur Lawrence cut in on the conference line, his voice just as hard as he had sounded in February, "the State Inspector General, the Chief Information Officer of VITA, and I are getting into a state car at Capitol Square within thirty minutes and driving straight up Interstate 64 to Fishersville. We will be walking through those front lobby doors behind you before one o'clock this afternoon—and don't bother trying to hide a single clipboard before we get there, because we already have Nate's inventory list."

"By noon tomorrow," Governor Langford added firmly, "an emergency executive directive under my signature will permanently abolish off-campus paper pass restrictions and adult family lockout rules across Fishersville, pull every 2019 handbook from circulation, require signed acknowledgment of wheelchair bodily autonomy from every employee on your payroll, end mandatory manual-shop Vocational Evaluation rotations and retail-greeter pitches for college-degreed or credentialed adults, and keep VITA's engineering strike team physically on site in that classroom until every developer workstation is unlocked on an isolated instructional network. Do you understand me, Dr. Cross?"

"Yes, Governor," Dr. Cross whispered hoarsely.

"Katie, Nate," Governor Langford said, her voice steadying with unmistakable respect as she addressed us directly, "thank you for calling me straight away instead of letting Sterling's binder sit until markup. Arthur and the Inspector General will transmit a full audit readout to Dirksen Room 511 tonight."

"Thank you, Rebecca, and thank you, Arthur," Katie said, looking squarely across the lobby at Dr. Cross and Saunders so everyone in Fishersville heard the federal standard in plain English at the exact same moment Richmond did. "And so there is zero confusion between Richmond, Fishersville, and Washington about what proof my office needs before I lift my hold on that eighteen-point-five-million-dollar appropriation: I do not lift holds for task forces, VITA tickets, or promises. My hold remains locked next Thursday in Subcommittee markup, and it stays locked through full Committee conference until the Virginia Office of the State Inspector General and federal Rehabilitation Services Administration auditors certify to my office in writing—backed by live unlocked classroom developer logins, published updated handbooks, signed employee training rosters, electronic keycard security logs, and confidential, unmonitored interviews with current Barton Hall residents—that all five reforms are operating in real life every day when nobody thinks a Senator is watching."

Katie paused for half a beat, glancing toward the sunny bus stop out on Roosevelt Avenue beyond the glass entrance doors.

"And Rebecca?" Katie added calmly. "Tell whoever is sitting in the Director's chair next month not to bother staging a VIP tour for us—because Nate and I still have quarters left in our pockets, and we know the Route 250 bus schedule by heart."

Over the speakerphone, Governor Langford answered without a fraction of a second's hesitation:
