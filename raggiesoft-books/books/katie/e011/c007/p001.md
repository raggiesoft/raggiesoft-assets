## Thursday, May 11, 2028, 10:15 AM — Dirksen Room 124: Reading Federal Form SSA-4178 Into the Senate Record

"Question Three on page one of federal Form SSA-4178, titled *Marital Relationship Questionnaire*," forty-three-year-old Senator Katie Prairie read aloud into her dais microphone inside the packed wood-paneled hearing room of Dirksen 124, holding a crisp two-page Social Security Administration interrogation sheet high above her mahogany lectern so every Capitol Network camera lens in the back row could zoom straight onto the government form number. "Let me read the exact words our federal government instructs field-office claims specialists to ask two disabled adults who dare to care for each other under the same roof: *How do you introduce the other person to friends, relatives, or neighbors? How is mail addressed to each of you? Do you wear rings? And if you separate, how will you divide your belongings?*"

Along the raised semicircular committee dais to Katie's right, silver-haired Senate Majority Leader Chris Van Deren of Maryland leaned forward with his hands clasped beside his microphone, while fellow Senate Wheelchair Caucus co-sponsors Senator Tess Dunmore of Illinois and Senator Josh Novak of Iowa watched the minority side of the dais from their ultralight manual wheelchairs with identical expressions of quiet steel.

Parked two feet behind Katie's left wheel inside the widened thirty-eight-inch accommodation bay—wearing my Sergeant at Arms disability-support companion floor pass under our written Senate Ethics firewall while my privately owned MagnaBook Pro displayed the open-source Congressional Budget Office and Treasury tables I had published that morning through Prairie Twin Interactive—I looked down across the green felt witness table in the center of the room.

Sitting side by side at the lowered accessible witness bay, holding inner hands right on top of the polished oak table without guarding themselves for a single second, were Wesley Sorenson in his neatly pressed forest-green collared polo shirt—his smooth blue silicone ring resting comfortably over his left thumb—and our paternal cousin Chelsea in her soft lavender cardigan, keeping plush green Shelly Turtle tucked securely beneath her free arm and both asthma inhalers in her pocket. Immediately flanking them sat their two court-appointed legal guardians: my wife Heather Prairie—Wesley's plenary guardian since he turned eighteen in Virginia Beach—and our dad David Prairie in his navy blazer beside Brittany in her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses.

"Look at the two witnesses holding hands at that table, Mr. Chairman," Katie continued, lowering Form SSA-4178 onto her desk without raising her voice an inch. "Wesley has lived with my brother Nate, his wife Heather, and me in Virginia Beach since he finished Northwood High School twenty-three summers ago, illustrating transit maps and tending rescued painted turtles every single afternoon. Our forty-six-year-old cousin Chelsea survived eight years in an abusive Rhode Island group home before our dad, Brittany, my brother Nate, and I brought her home to Virginia Beach twelve summers ago. Both Wesley and Chelsea live under loving, court-supervised plenary guardianship and conservatorship because neither of them has the cognitive contracting capacity to take out a thirty-year mortgage, buy real estate on their own, or fend off predatory debt collectors. They couldn't walk into a county courthouse and sign a legal marriage contract on their own even if they tried."

Down at the witness table, Chelsea beamed proudly up at Katie's wheelchair and gave Wesley's knuckles an affectionate squeeze, eliciting a warm, resonant two-note hum from Wesley's chest that made half the gallery smile.

"And yet," Katie said, her clear Tidewater voice cutting straight across the marble room, "under Section 1614 of the Social Security Act—written back in 1972 and untouched for fifty-six years—two disabled Americans don't even have to be legally married to get hit with the federal marriage penalty. If two adults on Supplemental Security Income marry, or if an overzealous bureaucrat pulls out Form SSA-4178 and decides they are 'holding out' as a couple because they share a cozy companion suite under their family's roof, eat supper together, or wear a four-dollar silicone ring, the United States government slashes their combined monthly subsistence check by twenty-five percent, shrinks their combined nineteen-eighty-nine savings limit from four thousand dollars down to three thousand dollars, docks their checks by one-third for in-kind family meals, and strips Disabled Adult Child beneficiaries of the survivor benefits their late parents worked a lifetime to earn. Worse still, for millions of families across our constituent casework queue, if a disabled adult visits grandparents across state lines for thirty-one days or moves with an aging guardian to a neighboring state, state Medicaid disability waiver rules cut off their coverage cold and drop them to the bottom of a ten-year waiting list."

Down at the far Republican end of the dais, Ranking Member Senator Lawrence Halstead of Wyoming cleared his throat, tapping a thick blue Congressional Budget Office folder against his blotter.

"Nobody on this side of the aisle begrudges your cousin or Mr. Sorenson their companionship, Senator Prairie," Senator Halstead drawled in practiced committee cadence. "However, your bill—Senate Bill 1418, the Supported Companions, Waiver Portability, and Social Security Solvency Act—doesn't merely tweak a form for guardians. Title One repeals the Supplemental Security Income and Disabled Adult Child marriage, in-kind shelter, and holding-out penalties for every married and cohabiting disabled couple in the United States and indexes the asset cap to ten thousand dollars per person. Title Two mandates nationwide interstate Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services waiver portability and dual-state family reciprocity with zero waitlist resets. And Title Three establishes federal Guardian-Blessed Companion declarations granting automatic hospital intensive-care visitation, airline and rail companion passes, and Fair Housing zoning protection for family-connected accessible companion suites while preserving full plenary guardianship and anti-exploitation shields against real-estate or debt contracts. Have you read the Congressional Budget Office's ten-year mandatory spending score on Title One and Title Two, Senator? Forty-one point eight billion dollars! Where on earth do you expect the taxpayers of Wyoming and Virginia to find forty-two billion dollars—and sixty votes on the Senate floor?"

Across the narrow gap between our armrests, I saw the faintest, sharpest corner of a smile touch Katie's lips—the exact expression she used to get at age twelve right before checking my rook in four moves.

## Thursday, May 11, 2028, 10:42 AM — Majority Leader Van Deren's Reconciliation Gavel & Four Pay-Fors That Lock In Seventy-Five Years

"Thank you for teeing up both halves of the math, Senator Halstead," Katie replied pleasantly, glancing once toward Majority Leader Chris Van Deren before looking straight back across the curve of the dais. "First, as Majority Leader Van Deren and Budget Chairman Harrington confirmed when they placed Senate Bill 1418 inside Title Four of this spring's Budget Reconciliation Act under Section 310 of the Congressional Budget Act, every single provision affecting Social Security outlays, Medicaid waiver outlays, and Internal Revenue Code revenue qualifies for reconciliation—which requires fifty-one votes on the floor of the United States Senate, not sixty."

Senator Halstead's pen stopped moving. Beside him, two corporate-backed minority counsel whispered urgently into his ear.

"Second," Katie continued evenly, opening the certified Joint Committee on Taxation and Congressional Budget Office revenue score sheet that our legislative correspondent Sam Hawkins had placed neatly beside her glass of water—built from the public federal ledger models I had published online for every economist in America to verify—"working families in Virginia Beach, Danville, and Cheyenne aren't paying a single dime more to fund those forty-one point eight billion dollars. In fact, Title Five of our reconciliation bill pays for every penny of disability marriage equality and Medicaid waiver portability more than seventy times over, locks in full Social Security solvency past the year 2105, and funds twelve billion dollars in federal public school roof and wheelchair-elevator modernization grants so local shoppers never get squeezed with a regressive one-percent retail sales tax."

Katie lifted four fingers from her right armrest, ticking off the four revenue pillars one by one into the live national broadcast.

"One: we repeal the regressive Social Security payroll tax income cap under Section 3121 of the Internal Revenue Code," Katie said clearly. "Right now, a Coastal Virginia Transit Authority bus driver in Norfolk, a public-school teacher in Henrico, or a shipyard pipefitter in Newport News pays six point two percent into Social Security on every single dollar they earn from January second through December thirty-first—while a Wall Street executive making ten million dollars a year stops paying into Social Security before lunch on January ninth because every dollar above one hundred and eighty-four thousand five hundred dollars is completely exempt. Starting January first, all income—including high-earner pass-through and net investment compensation above four hundred thousand dollars—pays into Social Security all year long."

In the public gallery behind Heather, Dad, and Brittany, quiet, spontaneous nods rippled from wall to wall.

"Two," Katie said, "we enact a twenty-five-percent federal Billionaire Minimum Income and Extreme-Wealth Tax on net fortunes exceeding one billion dollars—ending once and for all the 'buy, borrow, die' tax shelter that lets centi-billionaires borrow tax-free against surging stock portfolios while paying a lower effective federal tax rate than my cousin Brittany or the nurses at Tidewater General Hospital. Three: we enact a permanent Corporate Windfall Profits Tax clawing back excess price-gouging margins whenever defense conglomerates, oil giants, pharmaceutical monopolies, or corporate grocery chains spike their profit margins above their five-year baseline on the backs of working families. And four: we codify permanent treble-damages clawbacks closing the offshore no-bid Pentagon and healthcare contractor shell-company loopholes this Congress uncovered last spring."

Katie folded her hands calmly atop Form SSA-4178 and looked straight down the mahogany dais at Senator Halstead.

"The Congressional Budget Office and the Chief Actuary of Social Security scored all four revenue provisions last night at three point four trillion dollars over ten years, reducing the federal deficit while keeping Social Security one hundred percent solvent for the next seventy-seven years," Katie said softly. "So when this bill comes to the Senate floor next month, every Senator in this chamber is going to cast a very simple recorded vote: do you stand with centi-billionaires and price-gouging monopolies who stop paying Social Security taxes on January ninth, or do you stand with couples like Wesley and Chelsea who just want to hold hands, share a family-connected sanctuary, and wear a four-dollar blue silicone ring without a federal agent interrogating their guardians?"

Down at the center witness table, Dad reached over and patted Heather's shoulder as Heather wiped a bright tear from her cheek—and right between them, Wesley lifted his joined hand with Chelsea's two inches above the oak table and gave two firm, happy taps against the polished wood.
