## Saturday, October 24, 2043, 4:30 PM — Our Living Room on Arvin Road: Quietly Recording Our Refusal Surrounded by Family & Signing Five Shields Against the Bubble

![On a warm October 2043 evening inside their Virginia Beach home on Arvin Road, 58-year-old Nathaniel Prairie relaxes happily in the middle of a custom wheelchair-transfer-height four-person cuddle nest platform bed in cozy grey plaid flannel sleepwear atop a patchwork navy-and-cream quilt, cuddling with the three women he loves—his 61-year-old paternal cousin Chelsea in cream knit sleepwear resting her head contentedly on his right shoulder, his 58-year-old twin sister Katie in lavender knit sleepwear leaning against his left shoulder holding his left hand, and his 58-year-old maternal cousin Brittany in burgundy flannel sleepwear and thin silver wire-rimmed oval glasses hugging Katie's shoulder—while Katie and Nate's two SwiftChair power wheelchairs sit parked beside the bed and Chelsea's two asthma inhalers rest on the headboard shelf.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/oct_2043_arvin_road_four_person_cuddle_nest_katie_nate_brittany_chelsea.jpg)

"They asked if the four of you could meet eleven governors and the national party chair at the Military Highway bus shelter on Monday morning with a live satellite truck," forty-one-year-old Sam Hawkins said from our dining room table on Arvin Road, resting his hand atop five thick manila folders stacked neatly beside Mandy's notary seal and Sarah's express-mail labels. "I told them no satellite trucks, no bus-stop press scrums, and nobody coming near your house."

"Thank you, Sam," fifty-eight-year-old Katie said softly from her brushed-silver and black SwiftChair, glancing warmly over her shoulder toward our living room sofa.

Seated side by side on the deep cushions in warm autumn sweaters were the other two halves of our four-person household: our fifty-eight-year-old maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher, adjusting her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as she tucked a soft fleece throw across her lap and her cousin's knees, and our sixty-one-year-old paternal cousin Chelsea, whose silver-streaked chestnut hair rested happily against Brittany's shoulder while she held her stuffed plush sea turtle in one hand and kept her maintenance and rescue asthma inhalers tucked safely in her cardigan pocket.

"Nate?" Chelsea asked in her thickly slurred voice, looking up anxiously when she heard the words *satellite truck*. "N-no loud people come here, right?"

Rolling my SwiftChair right up beside the end of the sofa so Chelsea could immediately reach out and hold my forearm with both hands, I smiled reassuringly at her as Brittany reached warmly across Chelsea's shoulder to give my wrist an affectionate squeeze too.

"Nobody loud is coming anywhere near our door, Chelsea," I promised gently. "Just Sam, Mandy, and Sarah—and the four of us."

Chelsea let out a happy sigh and leaned her cheek against my sleeve, while Brittany smiled across at Katie and me with quiet gratitude shining behind her wire-rimmed lenses.

If anybody had told seventeen-year-old Katie and me behind our locked bedroom door on New Year's Eve of 2002—or told nineteen-year-old me shivering outside the weathered public-housing buildings on Dirty Old Tonomy Hill in Newport, Rhode Island on Christmas Eve of 2004—that at fifty-eight years old all four of us would be living together full-time as one inseparable family, split seamlessly between Arvin Road here in Virginia Beach and Sunset Drive up in Alexandria whenever the Senate was in session, neither of us would have dared to believe it.

First, in February of 2016—fourteen years after the drunken New Year's Eve betrayal that had barred Brittany from our house, even though her parents, our beloved Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven Fletcher, had remained warmly in our corner all along and Aunt Karen had even helped Dad and us out financially whenever things were tight—Brittany found her way home. Living across the James River in Hillside Court public housing in South Richmond and watching the Thursday evening news air thirty-year-old Katie fearlessly ripping apart House Bill 663 on the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates had shattered the fog Brittany had been trapped inside for years, right as abusive, controlling Tyler—whom Brittany had started dating four years earlier with no idea he was the exact same Northwood High senior who had maliciously lied to Heather Collins about me after that drunken New Year's Eve, otherwise she never would have dated him in the first place—was forcing her toward a Saturday afternoon wedding in a bleak South Richmond church she didn't even want to set foot in. After Brittany rode a JRTC bus across the river without an appointment on Friday, held open the General Assembly Building elevator when I shouted *"Hold the elevator!"*, and wept inside Room 518 begging us to help her get out—and after it dawned on Brittany on Saturday wedding morning that Tyler was the exact guy who had lied to Heather about me, becoming the straw that broke the camel's back—Katie and I figured out who he was the instant we saw him step out at the church, where Katie sat beside me in complete silence during Saturday's February 6, 2016 ceremony so no tabloid could claim she had pulled a stunt as a Delegate while I spoke up—*"I object!"*—at *"Speak now or forever hold your peace."* Once Brittany bravely showed the minister and church coordinator the bruises Tyler had left on her in the side room, we brought her back to our Richmond apartment for Saturday night—where terrified Brittany climbed right into our latched walk-in closet with Katie and me and showed us her bruises before climbing into the sleeping bag, horrifying both of us and finalizing our decision on the spot to take her someplace Tyler would never think to look by bringing her all the way up to Washington on Sunday's *Meet the Nation* train so Aunt Karen, Uncle Steven, and Dad could meet her right outside the King Street–Old Town faregates. The very next afternoon—after a raging Tyler turned left off Earlton Court down the Macgregory Street dead end at two o'clock Monday dismissal, plowed through the fence into the steel frame rail of a departing Northwood High School bus so the bus driver and all fifty-four students escaped with bumps and bruises to get checked out at the hospital while Tyler was rushed under armed guard to the Level 1 Trauma Center at Tidewater General Hospital, left severely disabled for life and bound for a state medical prison—Brittany enrolled at the Coastal Adult Learning Center off Virginia Beach Boulevard to earn her HSE, got her life back together, and moved in to live with Katie and me on Arvin Road the moment the 2016 legislative session adjourned in March, just as she had promised us back in high school.

Then, in July of 2016—once Brittany had earned her HSE, joined Prairie Twin Interactive, and settled safely under our roof while our dad David was still alive to drive his wheelchair van straight up Interstate 95 with us and help bring her home—we rescued our thirty-four-year-old paternal cousin Chelsea from Braxton, Rhode Island. After Aunt Elizabeth and Grandma died in 2004 and Newport demolished the old World War II public housing on Tonomy Hill, Chelsea had tried living on her own in a private apartment, and because of her developmental delays it had fallen apart. Contracted out to Regalis Community House—a bleak brick group home tucked beside the dumpsters at the bottom of Mount Regalis on the campus of elitist King's College, where wealthy undergraduates looked down their noses from the upper stone balustrade while staff rode BTA Route 99 up the hill—Chelsea had spent eight miserable years under a two-faced male director whom she called Group Home Two-Face, who locked away her rescue inhaler, confiscated Shelly Turtle, worked residents at unpaid campus mailers, and hid behind a falsified 2008 probate form claiming Chelsea had no living next of kin. Armed with Aunt Liz's certified birth records and an emergency release order Dad, Katie, Brittany, and I won the next morning in the Braxton Municipal Probate Court, we rolled right back through King's College's main gate with a sheriff's deputy and brought a weeping, relieved Chelsea home to Virginia Beach so nobody would ever mistreat or abandon her again.

Twenty-seven years later, save for an occasional overnight Senate oversight trip, the four of us had shared one inseparable home every single week. Compensated privately through our software company, Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC—never a dime of taxpayer Senate payroll—Brittany lovingly ran our household and cared for Chelsea alongside us so that even after Dad passed away before the 2032 election, Chelsea never once had to deal with rotating strangers from an outside care agency whenever Katie and I were at the Capitol, and all four of us traveled together between our Virginia Beach home and Sunset Drive so Chelsea's routine never broke. Dragging Chelsea out to a noisy intersection full of shouting political reporters just so Katie could turn down a presidential draft movement she had never asked for was out of the question.

Even more than that, Katie and I knew firsthand what winning the 2044 presidential election and taking the oath on January 20, 2045 would actually do to the four of us.

"Mandy, is the tripod level?" Katie asked, rolling her SwiftChair into position right beside mine directly in front of the living room sofa so Brittany could rest her left hand warmly on Katie's shoulder while Chelsea held tight to my left arm from the cushion behind me.

"Dead level, Senator," Mandy said softly, checking the frame on my mPhone mounted beside the dining table. "Just the four of you in your own living room—zero campaign banners, zero podiums."

"Before we hit record," Katie said, turning her head slightly toward Sam, Mandy, and Sarah with the thorough legislative caution that had defined all eighteen years of her three Senate terms, "walk Nate and Brittany and me through the five folders one last time. If we upload this video tonight, who has to receive formal legal notice on Monday morning so that no draft committee, no state primary ballot, and no federal agency can ever wrap the four of us into the Secret Service bubble during the 2044 election?"

Sam picked up the five folders in order, ticking each one off cleanly.

"Folder One goes by courier Monday morning to the Secretary of Homeland Security and all five congressional leaders on the candidate-protection advisory committee under Title 18, Section 3056(a)(7)," Sam explained. "While a sitting President or Vice President is barred by statute from refusing Secret Service protection, Section 3056(a) explicitly provides that major-candidate protection may be declined. Your signed letter puts Homeland Security and the Senate and House leadership on formal advance notice that you are not a candidate for President or Vice President in 2044—and that even if anyone ever tried to designate you, you formally exercise your statutory right in advance to decline any Secret Service detail."

"Folder Two is the fifty-state ballot shield," Mandy continued, tapping a stack of crisp pages stamped with her Virginia notary seal. "Fifteen states—including Michigan, Wisconsin, Maryland, Oregon, Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Nebraska—have automatic media-recognition laws requiring their Secretaries of State to print on the presidential primary ballot every national figure discussed in the press unless that person files a sworn, notarized Affidavit of Non-Candidacy. We prepared notarized Affidavits of Non-Candidacy for every automatic-ballot Secretary of State and State Board of Elections in the country, swearing under oath that you are not and will not become a candidate in 2044."

"Folder Three goes to the Federal Election Commission under 11 C.F.R. Section 100.3," Sarah said, holding up the third packet, "formally disavowing every unauthorized 'Draft Katie Prairie' committee within the statutory thirty-day window so none of their fundraising can trigger federal candidate status. Folder Four goes to the Chair and the Secretary of the Democratic National Committee—your irrevocable Sherman declaration instructing the party that you refuse to participate in any debate, refuse the seating of any draft delegates, and will not accept a nomination from the convention floor and would not serve if elected. And Folder Five goes to the Virginia Department of Elections in Richmond confirming that you are retiring at the conclusion of your third Senate term on January 3, 2045."

Katie looked at the five folders, looked up at Brittany and Chelsea behind us, and let out a long, relieved breath. Rolling over to the dining table first, she signed her clear, steady signature—*Katie Prairie*—across every notarized affidavit and statutory notice while Mandy pressed her embossed seal onto the final sheet, and then rolled right back beside my wheelchair in front of the sofa.

"Ready when you are, Nate," Katie said quietly, lacing the fingers of her left hand through my right hand while Brittany squeezed Katie's shoulder and Chelsea rested her chin happily against mine.

I tapped the wireless shutter remote in my palm, and the small red recording light glowed on my mPhone.

"Good evening from our home here in Virginia Beach," fifty-eight-year-old Katie said calmly into the quiet living room, her voice warm, unscripted, and crystal clear. "I am sitting here tonight with the three people who share every day of my life: my twin brother Nate, our cousin Brittany, and our cousin Chelsea. Over the past few months, governors, colleagues, and kind citizens across the country have asked me to enter the 2044 presidential race for the term beginning in January of 2045, and draft committees have begun circulating petitions in primary states. I am deeply grateful for your trust—and tonight, surrounded by my family, I want to tell you plainly why my answer is an irrevocable no."

Beside me, Katie gave my hand a gentle squeeze.

"Sixteen Octobers ago, Nate and I flew down to Chambers Field at Naval Station Norfolk aboard Air Force One with President Clara Morales," Katie continued evenly. "Clara Morales was an honorable President, and restoring our sailors' stolen housing and pier funds that day is one of the proudest memories of my Senate career. That Thursday was also the very first time Nate and I ever stepped inside the presidential Secret Service bubble. Even behind the gates of a military base, we watched every cross-street freeze thirty minutes ahead of our wheels, and we watched a good President realize she couldn't even take ten unscripted steps off a pre-swept carpet to buy a cold drink for herself. Under federal law, once you become President of the United States, you can never decline that bubble. Armed agents and armored motorcades shut down every street you touch, pull public buses out of service, and move permanently into your family's home."

Katie glanced affectionately back at Chelsea—who smiled shyly at the mention of home—and at Brittany beside her.

"Our cousin Chelsea survived an abusive group home before Nate and I brought her home to live with us, and she deserves a quiet, peaceful house where strangers and cameras never intrude," Katie said softly. "Our cousin Brittany, Nate, and I waited half a lifetime to keep our high-school promise to live together under one roof. And Nate and I fought our entire lives for the simple freedom to roll down to our corner bus stop on Military Highway, tap our fare passes alongside our neighbors, and go wherever we please unannounced. Monday morning, my staff is filing notarized Affidavits of Non-Candidacy with state election officials nationwide, disavowal notices with the Federal Election Commission, an irrevocable refusal of nomination with the Democratic National Committee, and formal declination notices under federal statute so I am never placed on a 2044 ballot or wrapped inside a Secret Service detail. When my third Senate term ends on January 3, 2045, I will retire from elected office—though anyone seeking my endorsement in next spring's primary should be ready to talk to everyday Virginians without a motorcade. Thank you for eighteen wonderful years, and good night from all four of us."

"Good night!" Chelsea chimed in happily over my shoulder, giving a little wave with her free hand before I clicked the remote to stop the recording.

## Saturday, October 24, 2043, 9:45 PM — Out of the Closet & Safe Together in Our Four-Person Nest

By quarter to ten that night, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah had walked back to their ranch house two streets over with the five sealed express-mail packets tucked safely in Sam's messenger bag, the unedited four-minute refusal video was quietly live on StreamCast, The Grid, and Chirp with comments turned off, and the brick ranch house on Arvin Road belonged completely to the four of us again. Before heading down the hall for bed, we had spent a quiet half hour piled together on the deep living-room sofa under our soft fleece throw—Chelsea curled contentedly against my right side, Brittany tucked right up against my left shoulder with her wire-rimmed glasses set on the end table as she hugged my arm, and Katie nestled right against Brittany and reaching across to hold my hand while the autumn wind rustled the oak leaves outside.

Now, down the widened hallway, the warm amber glow of two bedside lamps lit the spacious master bedroom sanctuary we had built both here on Arvin Road and up at Sunset Drive in Alexandria: a custom wall-to-wall, wheelchair-transfer-height four-person cuddle nest platform bed topped with deep orthopedic mattresses, soft flannel sheets, bolstered side rails, and an oversized patchwork navy-and-cream quilt.

For more than thirteen years after our estranged mother Brenda had bang-searched the back alley outside Apartment A6 at Greenway Court in August of 2003, Katie and I had been so conditioned by trauma and autism that the only place on earth we felt safe enough to fall asleep was zipped inside a single sleeping bag on a hard carpeted closet floor behind a latched door. Even though terrified Brittany had gratefully squeezed right into that windowless walk-in closet with us on her very first night rescued from the wedding in Richmond, once Brittany moved in with us on Arvin Road after session adjourned in March 2016 she had insisted on climbing right into our sleeping bag and pillows inside the bedroom closet so we never felt rushed—and when we rescued Chelsea from Regalis Community House that July, Chelsea had been so terrified that Group Home Two-Face might snatch her back through the window that she crammed right into the closet with the three of us too, leaving all four grown cousins wedged wall to wall inside two zipped-together sleeping bags on the closet carpet.

It had taken months of gentle, patient teamwork—first building our custom oak platform bed in September 2016, and then Brittany leading all four of us through zero-pressure twenty-minute chamomile-tea tries that stretched from twelve minutes in October to forty-five minutes on Thanksgiving night and a sunlit Sunday afternoon nap in January—before all four of us had finally felt safe enough on the night of February 11, 2017 to roll up our closet sleeping bags and come out of the closet for good.

Rolling my SwiftChair parallel to the smooth wooden transfer edge beside Katie's frame tonight, I locked my brakes, slid smoothly across onto the navy-and-cream patchwork quilt in my grey plaid flannel pajamas, and helped Katie settle onto the plush pillows to my left in her soft lavender knit sleepwear—Katie and Brittany happily trading off that inner spot next to my left shoulder from night to night so all three of them got their turn tucked right against my side—while Brittany set Chelsea's maintenance and rescue inhalers on the wooden headboard shelf within easy reach, helped Chelsea curl up cozily against my right shoulder in her warm cream thermal pajamas, and climbed in right beside Katie in her burgundy flannel pajamas, smiling through her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as she wrapped one arm around Katie's shoulder and reached her other hand warmly across to clasp my forearm.

Katie let out a long, blissful groan as the thick mattress cradled her shoulders and lower back, lacing the fingers of her right hand through my left hand across my lap.

"Remember when we used to insist the hall closet carpet was perfectly comfortable?" my fifty-eight-year-old twin sister murmured sleepily.

Brittany laughed softly, leaning across Katie's shoulder to give my arm an affectionate squeeze before resting her cheek gently against Katie's hair. "It only took eleven months on that closet plywood—and four sets of knees wedged between the drywall studs—to get all of us onto a real mattress."

"Okay, fine," I admitted with a sheepish grin in the warm lamplight as Katie nodded in complete agreement against my shoulder. "Sleeping on carpeted closet floors really did hurt."

"Bed is soft," Chelsea murmured contentedly, snuggling closer against my right arm and resting her silver-streaked head right in the crook of my shoulder where nobody in a group home could ever yell at her again. "Just us four."

"Just us four, Chelsea," Brittany whispered affectionately across the pillows, her fingers still resting warmly over mine and Katie's.

Lying right there in the warm center of our four-person nest—with my sweet paternal cousin Chelsea breathing evenly and safely against my right shoulder, my twin sister Katie nestled against my left shoulder holding my hand, and my maternal cousin Brittany tucked right into our embrace just like that snowy evening in the Blizzard of '96 and all those afternoons on Earlton Court—I looked toward the two quiet SwiftChairs parked side by side in the soft glow and felt my whole chest relax.

Out in Washington and across fifty state capitals, politicians were spending hundreds of millions of dollars tonight fighting each other for the privilege of getting locked inside an armored limousine for the rest of their lives. Lying here cuddling directly with the three women I loved most in the world—my sister Katie, my cousin Brittany, and my cousin Chelsea—I already had the best spot on earth.
