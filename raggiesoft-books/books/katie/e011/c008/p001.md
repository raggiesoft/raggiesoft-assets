## Saturday, November 10, 2029, 11:00 AM — A Bare Black Lapel in Pittsylvania County, Old-Guard Virginia Respect, & Sitting Where Aunt Marge Put Us

![On a crisp November 2029 afternoon along the sunlit brick ramp courtyard outside a historic red-brick chapel in Southside Virginia near Danville with golden oak leaves and a distant flag at half-staff, forty-four-year-old Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their two compact black-and-silver SwiftChair power wheelchairs in modest black mourning attire—Katie wearing no Senate lapel pin—leaning gently forward to hold twenty-seven-year-old triplets Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins's hands beside sixty-eight-year-old widowed Aunt Marge Callaway as seventy-year-old David Prairie, Brittany Fletcher in her silver wire-rimmed glasses, Chelsea holding Shelly Turtle, and Wesley wearing his blue silicone ring stand quietly behind them.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/nov_2029_danville_uncle_richard_funeral_promise_to_triplets.jpg)

"Right this way, Senator Prairie," the well-meaning chapel usher murmured at two minutes before eleven, unclipping a brass hook on the crimson velvet cord that reserved the second-row left-hand pews for visiting constitutional officers inside the quiet red-brick vestibule outside Danville. "We left sixty inches clear at the end of the Governor's and Senators' row for you and your brother—"

"Katie and Nate aren't sitting in the dignitaries' row today, Thomas," sixty-eight-year-old Aunt Marge Callaway interrupted gently, stepping straight across the heart-pine vestibule floor in her black wool coat and single strand of white pearls with twenty-seven-year-old Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins pressed shoulder to shoulder right behind her. Reaching down with steady, warm hands, Aunt Marge squeezed Katie's shoulder first and mine second. "They're sitting up front with my kids and me."

"Yes, ma'am—of course, Mrs. Callaway," the usher said immediately, bowing his head in quiet apology and stepping back so our family could roll down the wide center aisle together.

Beside me in her brushed-silver and black SwiftChair, Katie reached up for half a second and touched the plain, unadorned collar of her black mourning jacket.

Her gold United States Senate lapel pin wasn't there. She had unpinned it the night before in Kempsville and left it zipped inside a velvet pouch in our desk drawer before Dad and Brittany shared the four-speed manual drive down U.S. Route 58 in Dad's pickup—with Katie, Heather, Chelsea, Wesley, and me riding together across the roomy six-passenger front and rear bench seats while our two unoccupied powerchairs rode locked onto the four-point tracks beneath the fiberglass camper shell. Nobody in Dirksen Room 511 had sent a press advisory to the Richmond or Roanoke bureaus. No staff photographer waited on the brick sidewalk. Katie hadn't come to Pittsylvania County as the junior United States Senator from Virginia.

She had come—and all six of us had come—because sixty-nine-year-old Richard Callaway had been like a second father to my twin sister and me, and today our heart was broken right alongside Aunt Marge's and the triplets'.

Outside the tall, clear-paned colonial windows, golden November oak leaves drifted silently past the white churchyard flagpole, where the deep blue flag of the Commonwealth of Virginia hung at half-staff by executive order of Governor Rebecca Langford. Inside, the scent of polished cedar pews, beeswax candles, and crisp autumn coats filled a sanctuary packed from the front rail to the rear gallery doors—not with Washington cable pundits or loudmouthed political operatives, but with the real Southside Virginia Uncle Richard had spent twenty-four years in the House of Delegates faithfully serving: Pittsylvania County tobacco and grain farmers in pressed dark suits, retired Dan River textile machinists, high-school civics teachers, Danville Mayor Lawrence Carter and members of the Danville City Council, three uniformed Piedmont Regional Transit bus operators who drove the Route 58 connector between Martinsville and Danville that Uncle Richard and the triplets had championed from an eighth-grade Mock Session bill, veteran clerks and sergeant-at-arms officers from the Virginia General Assembly, retired Virginia Capitol Police troopers, and two dozen young men and women in their twenties who had once worn navy-blue House Page blazers under Leader Callaway's watchful eye.

Across the center aisle in the visiting officials' pews, Virginia's bipartisan institutional memory had shown up in quiet, unmistakable force.

Sitting side by side without an ounce of campaign theater were sitting Democratic Governor Rebecca Langford, Senior United States Senator Thomas Keane, and retired three-term Senator Martin Warren, each of them giving Dad, Katie, and me a small, solemn nod of understanding when they saw us park our SwiftChairs in the front-right family cut-out directly beside Aunt Marge, Sam, Mandy, Sarah, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and Wesley. One row behind the Governor sat the honorable, old-school Virginia Republicans who had stood shoulder to shoulder with Richard on the House floor when Delegate Martin Cobb's bathroom bill imploded under thirteen-year-old Sarah's simple question in February of 2016, and who had spent the past two and a half years since Garrett Thorne's impeachment conviction and lifetime disqualification working tirelessly alongside Richard to scrub the rot out of their party and return Virginia conservatism to constitutional decency: former House Speaker William J. Harwell, former House Appropriations Chairman S. Christopher Jennings, and our longtime Virginia Beach friend across the aisle, former Delegate Ronald Villa.

Notably—and mercifully—absent from every pew, sidewalk, and gravel parking lot in Pittsylvania County were the Restore the Republic grifters and talk-radio screamers who had spent 2026 calling a twenty-four-year Republican House Majority Leader *RINO Richard* for refusing to bow to Garrett Thorne and for defending his autistic nephew, his autistic nieces, and my sister on live television. None of them dared show their faces in Richard Callaway's hometown today.

In the vestibule floral alcove just behind us, nestled among dozens of white chrysanthemum wreaths from Danville neighbors, rested a woven basket of autumn mountain laurel and white roses sent down from Lee Highway in Montgomery County with a handwritten card signed by Jordan Shawn Kelly, Aubrie Jean Kelly, and Madison Elizabeth Scott—remembering the sunny May afternoon at the Danville bus shelter when Uncle Richard had peeled off a royal-blue campaign sticker, pressed it onto his Republican suit lapel, and told all of rural Virginia that character mattered more than party labels.

As the organ's soft opening chords faded into stillness and the gray-haired Southside minister invited the congregation to bow their heads in prayer, Katie, Dad, and I bowed our heads quietly beside Aunt Marge.

Neither Katie, Dad, nor I believed in any god or belonged to any church, and Uncle Richard had known that about us for thirteen years—ever since watching Katie affirm her oaths with her left hand on the Constitution of Virginia and the Constitution of the United States without saying *So help me God*, and telling a sneering cable reporter on his front porch that Katie Prairie lived more honest neighborliness in an ordinary Tuesday on a city bus than half the Pharisees shouting Bible verses on television. Respecting Uncle Richard meant respecting the house, the neighbors, and the quiet country hymns he and Aunt Marge loved, sitting in reverent silence while Chelsea hugged plush green Shelly Turtle softly against her black sweater and Wesley kept his warm, steady hand over hers beside his matte blue silicone ring.

Halfway through the service, former Democratic House Minority Leader and Speaker Tom Toscano of Charlottesville walked slowly up the three oak steps to the lectern, resting both hands on the worn wood as he looked down first at Aunt Marge, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah, and then straight at Katie and me.

"For eighteen winters in Richmond, Richard Callaway and I had the exact opposite job description every day the gavel came down at noon," Tom Toscano said, his voice Steady and rich across the hushed sanctuary. "His job at the front-row center-aisle desk was to pass the Republican majority's calendar, and my job ten feet across the green carpet was to stop him when we thought they had it wrong. We argued over highway formulas, tax brackets, hospital beds, and committee dockets until five o'clock in the afternoon—and not once in eighteen years did Richard Callaway ever break his word to me, rig a procedural trap behind a colleague's back, or mistake a political opponent for an enemy."

Tom paused, taking off his reading glasses for a second as a quiet murmur of recognition rippled through the rows of former Delegates and Capitol clerks.

"People in Washington and Richmond sometimes ask me which floor debate or budget conference with Richard I remember best," Tom continued softly. "It wasn't on the House floor at all. It was a Friday morning in February of 2016 outside Room 518 on the fifth floor of the old General Assembly Building. Somebody who worked for my own caucus had shouted inside a small office and overwhelmed two brilliant young public servants we all deeply admire—and three thirteen-year-old House Pages in navy blazers sprinted down four flights of marble stairs to get their Uncle Richard. Within three minutes, the Republican Majority Leader and the Democratic Minority Leader of the Virginia House of Delegates were standing shoulder to shoulder in that hallway like two grey-haired courthouse bailiffs so nobody could bother that room, while Richard knelt right down at eye level—no cameras, no reporters, no party pins—just a good, decent Southside gentleman making sure his kids' friends knew they were safe. That was Richard Callaway when nobody was watching. And Virginia is poorer today without him."

Beside me, Katie's breath hitched silently in her throat.

Without saying a word, I clicked my right armrest up so Katie could click her left armrest up, leaned across the narrow gap between our SwiftChairs, and wrapped my right arm firmly around my twin sister's shoulders while Dad rested his steady hand gently on the back of Katie's neck from the pew seat right beside us and Brittany silently passed Sarah and Mandy two clean folded handkerchiefs down the row.

## Saturday, November 10, 2029, 12:35 PM — Outside on the Sunlit Brick Ramp Courtyard: Katie's Lifelong Promise to Sam, Mandy, and Sarah

Twenty minutes after the final benediction—while Governor Langford, Senator Keane, Speaker Harwell, and Tom Toscano stood quietly down by the gravel drive gently intercepting well-wishers so the family could have five unhurried minutes in the November sun—Aunt Marge, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah stepped out the side accessibility doors onto the warm red-brick ramp courtyard beside our family.

Standing so close together that their dark wool coat sleeves touched—twenty-seven-year-old Sam in the middle with his thin glasses fogging slightly at the corners, and his identical twin sisters Mandy and Sarah pressed tight against his left and right arms—our three favorite triplets looked younger and more vulnerable than we had seen them look since they were thirteen-year-old eighth-graders clutching yellow transportation folders outside Room 518.

Katie and I knew every word of what all three of them were feeling without anyone having to explain it.

Fourteen winters ago, when Sam, Mandy, and Sarah were twelve-year-old autistic kids trapped in a chaotic house where adults screamed at them whenever sensory overload hit, Uncle Richard and Aunt Marge had driven across Pittsylvania County through a blinding snowstorm, walked into Juvenile and Domestic Relations District Court, and built an unbreakable fortress of calm around all three of them. Uncle Richard had made sure nobody separated them in eighth grade when they served as House Pages, cheered them through four years together at Central Virginia University along the Route 6 bus corridor, beamed with pride when Katie hired all three of them side by side in the Senate of Virginia and then in Dirksen Room 511, and stood on his Danville front porch like a lion tearing apart anyone on television who dared call autism *broken*.

Now the lion who had guarded their front porch since they were twelve years old was gone, and all three triplets were staring down at the red bricks trying with everything in them not to shake.

"He kept telling Marge and us on Tuesday night not to worry about the farm roof or the paperwork," Sam whispered unevenly, his fingers tightening around the folded memorial program in his hand. "He made lists on yellow sticky notes for all three of us right from his hospital bed so nothing would catch us by surprise."

"He hated surprises almost as much as we do," Sarah whispered, a tear slipping straight down her cheek as Mandy held tightly onto Sarah's wrist and Aunt Marge wrapped one arm gently around Mandy's waist.

Katie released her brake lever, eased her SwiftChair six inches closer across the sunlit brick courtyard until her footrests stopped right in front of the triplets' shoes, and held out both of her hands—no gloves, no podium, no Senatorial distance.

Mandy put her left hand into Katie's right hand, and Sarah put her right hand into Katie's left hand, while I rolled up beside my sister and reached up to clasp Sam's forearm firmly in mine. Behind our backrests, Dad, Brittany, Chelsea, and Wesley stood in a quiet, protective half-circle blocking the autumn wind off the pasture.

"Sam, Mandy, Sarah—look right at Nate and me for a second," Katie said softly, her voice steady, literal, and crystal clear in the quiet courtyard air.

All three twenty-seven-year-old triplets lifted their eyes together to meet hers, and Aunt Marge looked down at Katie with glistening eyes.

"Thirteen years ago in Richmond, your Uncle Richard trusted Nate and me with the three people he loved most in the world alongside Aunt Marge," Katie said quietly, giving Mandy's and Sarah's fingers a gentle, grounding squeeze while I held fast to Sam's arm. "And two Januaries ago in Dirksen Room 511, when he looked at your three desks together and asked me, *All three?*, he was making sure nobody in Washington would ever pull you apart after he wasn't here to stand on the porch anymore. So I am making all three of you—and Aunt Marge—a promise right here today, and you know Nate and I don't say words we don't mean literally."

Katie's voice trembled once with emotion, then locked right back into the unshakeable quiet certainty I had trusted since the day we were born.

"Not just in our Senate office—for the rest of our lives," Katie promised them, looking from Sam to Mandy to Sarah and then up to Aunt Marge. "As long as Nate and I are alive on this earth, nobody is ever going to separate the three of you, nobody is ever going to yell at you, and none of you—including you, Aunt Marge—will ever be left alone without family. You are our family. Whether we are up on Sunset Drive in Alexandria, or home on Arvin Road in Kempsville, or long after I finish serving in the Senate one day, our dining room table is your table, our holidays are yours whenever you want them, and if the day ever comes that the three of you want a house right down the street from ours, you will always have a home right beside Nate, Brittany, Chelsea, Wesley, Dad, and me. Uncle Richard never left you behind, and neither will we. Ever."

For three full seconds nobody on that brick courtyard breathed.

Then Mandy let out a quiet sob of pure relief, Sarah dropped to one knee on the sun-warmed brick beside Mandy so both sisters could wrap their arms tightly around Katie's shoulders, Sam leaned down to hug both my shoulder and Katie's at the exact same time, and sixty-eight-year-old Aunt Marge pressed a trembling hand briefly to her lips before leaning down to kiss the top of Katie's blonde hair.

"Richard knew," Aunt Marge whispered against Katie's temple, her voice thick with tears and Southside grace as Dad stepped forward to rest a quiet, brotherly hand beside hers. "Lord have mercy, Katie, my husband knew exactly whose hands he was leaving our babies in."
