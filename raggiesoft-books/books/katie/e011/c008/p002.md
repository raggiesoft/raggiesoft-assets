## Saturday, October 18, 2031, 10:30 AM — Dad's Navy Coffee Mug, White Roses Without a Motorcade, & Seven Family Hands Around Our Chairs

![Inside a warm, sunlit zero-step brick-and-oak memorial chapel in Kempsville, Virginia Beach in October 2031 with golden autumn oaks outside the tall arched windows, forty-six-year-old Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their two compact black-and-silver SwiftChair power wheelchairs leaning close together in a comforting twin embrace facing an oak memorial table that holds a silver-framed smiling portrait of their father David Prairie, his favorite plain navy ceramic coffee mug, and pure white heirloom roses with a handwritten White House card, surrounded closely by widowed Aunt Marge Callaway and twenty-nine-year-old triplets Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins representing their late Uncle Richard alongside Brittany Fletcher, Chelsea holding Shelly Turtle, Wesley wearing his blue silicone ring, Heather and Dan Lindstrom, Aunt Karen, and uniformed CVTA transit operators seated respectfully in the pews.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b006/oct_2031_kempsville_david_prairie_funeral_family_rose_garden_roses.jpg)

With both hands steady even though her chin was trembling behind her thin silver wire-rimmed oval glasses, forty-six-year-old Brittany set Dad's plain navy-blue ceramic coffee mug down on the warm oak memorial table beside his silver-framed photograph—turning the handle forty-five degrees to the right, the exact angle seventy-two-year-old David Prairie had left it beside the kitchen kettle on Arvin Road every morning of our adult lives.

Right next to that chipped-glaze mug stood a crystal vase holding two dozen freshly cut white heirloom roses that filled the sunlit Kempsville chapel with the unmistakable scent of June rain and damp Washington soil, resting beside a thick cream card written from edge to edge in fountain pen.

At eight-thirty the night before, while the five of us were sitting quietly together on the living-room sofa at Arvin Road after the funeral director left, Katie's personal phone had rung from the private residence of the White House.

President Clara Morales hadn't called through a switchboard operator or a scheduling aide. Speaking softly from her study upstairs, the woman who had stood on the green grass of the restored Rose Garden three summers earlier watching Dad and Heather co-sign Supported Companion Declaration Number One had told Katie how deeply fond she had been of the quiet, silver-haired retired systems engineer from Newport, Rhode Island who stood in the front row of the Senate Family Gallery beaming down at both of his twins without ever once asking for a VIP seat or a camera shot. Then, with the rare empathy of someone who had watched our family up close, Clara Morales had asked a question almost no head of state ever thinks to ask: whether bringing the mandatory presidential Secret Service package down to Virginia Beach—with K-9 bomb sweeps, rooftop countersnipers, magnetometers at the chapel doors, and frozen cross-streets shutting down the Saturday morning Route 12 and Route 24 buses—would frighten forty-nine-year-old Chelsea or turn our dad's quiet goodbye into a spectacle he would have hated.

When Katie's voice caught thanking her for remembering Chelsea, President Morales had promised gently that not a single black SUV would touch Virginia Beach Boulevard or Kempsville Road today. Instead, at seven o'clock this morning, an unmarked floral courier van had rolled quietly up our driveway with twenty-four white heirloom roses clipped at dawn by the White House groundskeepers from the exact Rose Garden border Dad had admired in 2028, accompanied by a two-page personal letter in Clara Morales's own handwriting:

> *Dear Katie, Nate, Brittany, Chelsea, and Wesley,*<br>
> *Every person who serves in Washington meets plenty of loud men who want the whole room to know how important they are. Your father was the rarest and finest kind of American citizen: a quiet, steadfast dad who measured his life by whether the people he loved were safe, respected, and free to go wherever they wished at eye level. When I watched David Prairie sign his name beside Heather's on our Rose Garden table in June of 2028, I knew immediately where Senator Katie Prairie and Nathaniel Prairie got their backbone and their heart. Thank you for sharing him with our country, and know that my prayers and deep gratitude are with your family in Kempsville today.*<br>
> *Warmly,*<br>
> *Clara Morales*

Sitting side by side with forty-six-year-old Katie in our compact black-and-silver SwiftChairs on the wide, zero-step heart-pine floor at the front of the secular memorial hall—no pulpit, no brass cross, no velvet ropes, just tall arched brick windows framing golden Tidewater oaks in the crisp October sun—I flipped our two inner armrests straight up, wrapped my right arm tightly around my twin sister's shoulders, and felt Katie lean her head directly against mine as she laced the fingers of her left hand into my lap.

The deep, steady pressure of holding my sister close kept both of our chests from splintering apart.

Dad had gone the way a good, tired seventy-two-year-old engineer deserved to go—peacefully in his sleep in his own familiar downstairs bedroom at home on Arvin Road four days ago, with zero sirens, zero hospital tubes, and zero unfinished worries left hanging over the people he loved. Eighteen months earlier, in the spring of 2030 when his cardiologist Dr. Evelyn Carter had told him plainly that his heart was slowing down, Dad had sat down with Brittany, Katie, and me at the dining table and walked straight into the Virginia Beach Circuit Court while he was still sharp and smiling—standing proudly beside Brittany as the judge appointed her Chelsea's successor plenary guardian and conservator, with Katie and me certified right alongside her as standby co-fiduciaries. And this morning at quarter to ten, Brittany—using the four manual gears Uncle David had patiently taught her in an empty Kempsville parking lot after she came home—had eased the clutch out on Dad's gleaming Dark Midnight Blue and Ultra Silver 1991 Crestline Garrison 350 Crew Cab and driven Katie, Chelsea, Wesley, and me smoothly down Indian River Road to say goodbye to him together.

Just to Katie's left, seventy-year-old widowed Aunt Marge Callaway stood in her black wool coat and single strand of white pearls with twenty-nine-year-old Sam, Mandy, and Sarah Hawkins lined up shoulder to shoulder right beside her.

The four of them had driven down together from Danville and Alexandria before dawn. As soon as we had rolled up the zero-step brick walkway at ten o'clock, Aunt Marge had wrapped Katie and me in a long, quiet hug while Sam rested a steady hand on my left shoulder and Mandy and Sarah held Katie's hands.

"Two Novembers ago in Pittsylvania County, your daddy sat right in our front row and didn't let the four of us stand alone for a single minute," Aunt Marge whispered softly into Katie's hair. "Today Sam, Mandy, Sarah, and I are right here representing their late Uncle Richard—and we're staying beside you all weekend long."

To my right stood Brittany, Aunt Karen Fletcher, and Uncle Steven Fletcher—the aunt and uncle who had sheltered ten-year-old Katie and me down on their Blackwater Road farm back in the summer of 1995, stood unflinchingly in Dad's corner through the autumn of 2001, and never stopped loving our household for thirty-six years. Right beside Brittany stood forty-nine-year-old Chelsea in her soft tagless black sweater—cradling plush green Shelly Turtle gently against her chest with her purple and blue inhalers tucked safely inside the front pocket Brittany had stitched for her—holding hands with forty-four-year-old Wesley, whose matte cobalt-blue silicone ring caught the warm window light as he stood beside Heather and Dan Lindstrom and twenty-five-year-old Jonas and Nora and twenty-three-year-old Molly, all five of whom had flown in from Minneapolis–Saint Paul last night the instant Heather heard the news.

Before the piano music began, Wesley leaned down quietly beside my armrest and bumped his knuckles gently against mine.

"Mr. David never talked over my head once, Nate," Wesley said earnestly, his voice thick as Chelsea nodded hard against his sleeve. "Very first day I came up your ramp on Arvin Road, he shook my hand like a regular man and showed me his socket wrenches."

"Uncle David was good and quiet," Chelsea whispered in her thick, halting voice, patting Shelly Turtle's plush green head softly. "Never yelled one time. Kept my puffers safe."

Not a trace of our estranged mother Brenda Walsh was anywhere near the property—no drama in the parking lot, no raised voices in the lobby, nothing to mar the gentle, unhurried peace Dad had spent thirty years building around our lives.

Instead, every oak pew behind our family was filled with the quiet, extraordinary tapestry of people whose lives David Prairie had touched without ever once seeking applause.

In the second row sat Dr. Evelyn Carter, our lifelong Kempsville family physician who had met terrified sixteen-year-old Katie, Dad, and me under the fluorescent lights of Crossroads Memorial's emergency room on the third Friday of September 2001, prescribed our backup collapsible joystick wheelchairs the Monday morning after Hurricane Isabel when she learned Dad had crawled across the carpet with us for two days, and cared gently for Dad's heart to the very end. Beside Dr. Carter sat our former Delaney Street neighbor Sharron—quietly dabbing her eyes with a tissue—alongside retired Navy Master Chief Robert H. Callahan from Kempsville, Dad's former computer-science professor from the Community College of Hampton Roads who still remembered the forty-year-old single father enrolling at night after watching his twelve-year-old twins hand-code HTML in seventh grade, and six grey-haired systems engineers who had worked alongside Dad in Tysons Corner after he bought Sunset Drive in Alexandria in March of 2007 while stubbornly keeping Arvin Road in Virginia Beach so Katie's and my autistic routine would never be broken.

Across the center aisle, four uniformed Coastal Virginia Transit Authority bus operators—led by our longtime Route 15 driver Darnell—sat shoulder to shoulder with two ARTS passenger-rail station attendants from Norfolk and Alexandria who had wheeled the portable mobile lift up to Business Class doors for Dad, Katie, and me across twenty winters, every one of them holding their navy uniform caps respectfully in their laps. Nestled beside the guestbook table stood a fragrant wreath of Blue Ridge pine and white lilies sent down from Lee Highway in Montgomery County with a handwritten note of love from Jordan Shawn Kelly, Aubrie Jean Kelly, and Madison Elizabeth Scott.

And tucked modestly into the rear oak pews—with zero press secretaries, zero lapel microphones, and zero entourage—sat the public servants who had come strictly as friends to honor Katie's father: Senior Virginia United States Senator Thomas Keane, Senate Majority Leader Chris Van Deren, Katie's fellow Senate Wheelchair Caucus co-founders Senator Tess Dunmore of Illinois and Senator Josh Novak of Wisconsin parked quietly at the accessible end of the back aisle, sitting Virginia Governor Walter Mercer alongside former Governor Rebecca Langford, Virginia Beach State Senator Aaron Ross, Delegate Clara Fowler, our longtime bipartisan friend former Republican Delegate Ronald Villa, and Kempsville City Councilman Marcus Vance.

## Saturday, October 18, 2031, 11:05 AM — Crawling on the Carpet During Hurricane Isabel & Four Manual Gears in an Empty Church Lot

When the soft acoustic guitar instrumental of The Stardust Engine's *The Family* faded gently into the high cedar rafters, nobody stepped behind a raised lectern—because Dad had told Brittany and us back in the spring that if anybody ever stood behind a five-foot wooden box talking down over our wheelchairs at his memorial, he would come back and unscrew the hinges himself.

Instead, Katie and I unlocked our brakes together, turned our two SwiftChairs smoothly in place on the level oak floorboards to face the room at eye level, and held hands across the two-inch gap between our frames.

"Most of Virginia first saw our dad on television standing behind Katie's and my wheelchairs on Election Night in 2010 or up in the Senate Gallery in 2027," I began, surprised by how steady my voice felt with Katie's fingers woven tightly through mine and Aunt Marge, Sam, Mandy, Sarah, Brittany, Chelsea, and Wesley right behind our shoulders. "People who didn't know him used to assume it must have taken some grand political ambition to raise a daughter who grew up to sit in the United States Senate. The truth is, Dad never cared a bit about titles. Everything Dad ever did came down to one rule: nobody in our family gets talked down to, and nobody gets left behind."

Beside me, Katie lifted her chin, tears shining brightly in her pale-blue eyes as a tiny, tender smile touched the corners of her mouth.

"Twenty-eight Septembers ago, when Nate and I were eighteen years old and Hurricane Isabel knocked the electricity out across Kempsville for two whole days, the twenty-four-volt batteries in both of our heavy powerchairs died completely on Friday afternoon," Katie said, her clear voice carrying effortlessly to the back row without an amplifier. "Nate and I had to slide out of our chairs onto the floor and crawl on our hands and knees down the hallway just to reach the bathroom or get a glass of water. At eighteen years old, we felt so small and humiliated we didn't even want to look up from the carpet."

In the second row, Dr. Evelyn Carter pressed two fingers softly to her lips, nodding through her tears as Darnell and Senator Keane leaned forward listening intently.

"Our dad walked down the hall holding a battery lantern, took one look at our faces, and didn't give us a pep talk from six feet up in the air," Katie said softly, squeezing my hand tighter. "He carried the sofa cushions, a cooler of ginger ale, yellow legal pads, and three board games into the living room, set them down on the rug—and then fifty-four-year-old David Prairie dropped straight down onto his own hands and knees and crawled everywhere in that house right beside us until the power came back on Saturday night, just so his two kids would never have to look up at him from the floor."

A quiet, collective catch of breath moved across the entire chapel—Aunt Karen reaching for Uncle Steven's hand, Heather wiping both cheeks against Dan's sleeve, and twenty-nine-year-old Sarah Hawkins slipping her arm tightly through Aunt Marge's.

"Two Novembers later, when Nate and I were twenty years old and melted down crying into a flip phone on a curb in Ocean View unable to get full sentences out," Katie continued gently, "Dad didn't panic or raise his voice. He remembered we could answer yes-or-no questions, calmly narrowed down where we were street by street in ninety seconds flat, drove across town in his 1991 pickup, and brought us home to Arvin Road for good. When our cousin Brittany needed a fresh start in 2016 after thirteen years with someone who wouldn't even let her hold a set of car keys, Dad handed her the brass ignition key to his prized four-speed truck in an empty Sunday church parking lot off Kempsville Road and taught her every gear without raising his voice once. When our cousin Chelsea was trapped eight states away behind falsified probate papers, Dad marched into a Rhode Island courtroom at seventy miles an hour of pure quiet fatherly steel until she came home with us. And when Wesley and Chelsea fell in love, Dad was the very first person at our dining room table sketching pocket-door widths on graph paper so they could share three connected homes without ever saying goodbye at an airport gate again."

I looked sideways at Dad's smiling silver-framed photograph beside his plain navy coffee mug and the white Rose Garden roses—remembering Grandpa Walter Prairie's white marble headstone up at Arlington National Cemetery where Dad used to take us as kids, and thinking of the shaded granite garden plot two hundred yards down the paved oak path right outside these doors where Katie, Brittany, Chelsea, Wesley, and I could roll up beside a wheelchair-height stone bench whenever we wanted to talk to him.

"Goodnight, Dad," I said softly, wrapping my arm back around my twin sister's shoulders as Katie leaned her cheek against mine and Brittany stepped forward to rest one warm hand on Katie's shoulder and one on mine. "Thank you for staying at eye level with us every single day of our lives."
