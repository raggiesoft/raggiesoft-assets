## Saturday, October 24, 2043, 4:30 PM — Our Living Room on Arvin Road: Quietly Recording Our Refusal Surrounded by Family & Signing Five Shields Against the Bubble

"They asked if the six of you could meet eleven governors and the national party chair at the Military Highway bus shelter on Monday morning with a live satellite truck," forty-one-year-old Sam Hawkins said from our dining room table on Arvin Road, resting his hand atop five thick manila folders stacked neatly beside Mandy's notary seal and Sarah's express-mail labels. "I told them no satellite trucks, no bus-stop press scrums, and nobody coming near your house."

"Thank you, Sam," fifty-eight-year-old Katie said softly from her brushed-silver and black SwiftChair, glancing warmly over her shoulder toward our living room sofa.

Seated side by side on the deep cushions in warm autumn sweaters were the other four members of our unbroken six-person household: my fifty-eight-year-old wife Heather Prairie, resting a steady hand near my shoulder, fifty-six-year-old Wesley Sorenson in his forest-green collared shirt with his smooth blue silicone ring over his thumb, sixty-one-year-old Chelsea holding her stuffed plush sea turtle in one arm with her maintenance and rescue asthma inhalers tucked safely in her cardigan pocket while Wesley kept a calm arm gently around her shoulders right outside the open oak pocket door leading into their connected companion and turtle suite, and our fifty-eight-year-old maternal cousin Brittany Fletcher adjusting her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as she tucked a soft fleece throw across her lap.

"Nate?" Chelsea asked in her thickly slurred voice, looking up anxiously as Wesley gave her shoulder two reassuring squeezes when they heard the words *satellite truck*. "N-no loud people come in our house or Wes's door, right?"

Rolling my SwiftChair right up beside the end of the sofa so Chelsea could immediately reach out and hold my forearm while Wesley bumped his knuckles warmly against mine and Heather laced her fingers through my other hand, I smiled reassuringly at all of them.

"Nobody loud is coming anywhere near our front door or your turtle room, Chelsea," I promised gently. "Just Sam, Mandy, and Sarah—and the six of us."

Chelsea let out a happy sigh and leaned back against Wesley's chest while Wesley tapped his knee twice in quiet approval and Brittany smiled across at Katie and me behind her wire-rimmed lenses.

If anybody had told seventeen-year-old Katie and me behind our locked bedroom door on New Year's Eve of 2002—or told eighteen-year-old Katie and me beneath the Virginia Beach Pavilion canopy on Graduation Day in June of 2003 when we cautiously welcomed Heather back for Wesley's sake—that at fifty-eight years old all six of us would still be sharing one seamless, joyful family life across Virginia Beach and Alexandria, neither of us would have dared to believe how good life could get.

First, in February of 2016—fourteen years after the drunken New Year's Eve betrayal that had barred Brittany from our house, even though her parents, our beloved Aunt Karen and Uncle Steven Fletcher, had remained warmly in our corner all along—Brittany found her way home. Living across the James River in Hillside Court public housing in South Richmond and watching the Thursday evening news air thirty-year-old Katie fearlessly ripping apart House Bill 663 on the floor of the Virginia House of Delegates had shattered the fog Brittany had been trapped inside for years, right as abusive, controlling Tyler—who had latched onto lonely Brittany out at Smoker's Corner in the spring of senior year without Brittany ever knowing he was the sleazebag who had maliciously lied to Heather about me in homeroom—was forcing her toward a Saturday afternoon wedding in a bleak South Richmond church she didn't even want to set foot in. After Brittany rode a JRTC bus across the river without an appointment on Friday, held open the General Assembly Building elevator when I shouted *"Hold the elevator!"*, and wept inside Room 518 begging us to help her get out—and after Tyler inadvertently bragged on Saturday morning about lying to Heather in high school—Katie, Heather, Wesley, and I rode JRTC Route 74 into South Richmond together, where Katie sat beside me in complete silence so no tabloid could claim she had pulled a stunt as a Delegate while I spoke up—*"I object!"*—sending Tyler into a purple fury at the sight of Heather sitting happily married beside me and my blonde twin sister Katie. Once Brittany bravely showed the minister, Mrs. Holloway, and Heather the bruises Tyler had left on her in the side room, we brought her back to our West Broad Street loft and took her, Heather, and Wesley all the way up to Washington on Sunday's *Meet the Nation* train so Aunt Karen, Uncle Steven, and Dad could meet Brittany right outside the King Street–Old Town faregates. After a raging Tyler plowed down the Macgregory Street dead end into a departing Northwood High School bus at Monday dismissal—landing himself in a state medical prison while every student walked off safely—Brittany earned her HSE at the Coastal Adult Learning Center off Virginia Beach Boulevard and moved home to Arvin Road with Katie, Heather, Wesley, and me in March 2016.

Then, in July of 2016—while Dad rented a roll-off ramp minivan so Katie, Brittany, Dad, and I could drive up Interstate 95 unannounced while Heather and Wesley prepared her welcome room at home—we rescued thirty-four-year-old Chelsea from King's College's Regalis Community House on Mount Regalis in Braxton, Rhode Island, overturning a falsified 2008 probate form in Braxton Municipal Probate Court so nobody would ever lock away her asthma inhalers or yell at her again.

Twenty-seven years later—with Wesley and Chelsea enjoying fourteen happy years in their two identically mirrored ground-floor companion and turtle suites in Kempsville and Sunset Drive while all six of us still slept side by side every single night on our nine-foot solid-oak Alaskan King platform beds—none of us ever had to be pulled apart. Working alongside Heather and us at Prairie Twin Interactive, LLC—never a dime of taxpayer Senate payroll—Brittany had succeeded Dad in Virginia Beach Circuit Court as Chelsea's legal guardian and conservator before his passing in 2031, while Heather continued as Wesley's lifelong guardian, and Brittany had inherited Dad's beloved Dark Midnight Blue and Ultra Silver 1991 four-speed Crew Cab 4x4, keeping every bolt maintained on her popular StreamTube and Grid truck channel. Dragging Chelsea and Wesley out to a noisy intersection full of shouting political reporters just so Katie could turn down a presidential draft movement she had never asked for was out of the question.

Even more than that, Katie and I knew firsthand what winning the 2044 presidential election and taking the oath on January 20, 2045 would actually do to all six of us.

"Mandy, is the tripod level?" Katie asked, rolling her SwiftChair into position right beside mine directly in front of the living room sofa so Heather and Brittany rested their hands warmly on our shoulders while Chelsea held tight to Wesley's hand behind us.

"Dead level, Senator," Mandy said softly, checking the frame on my mPhone mounted beside the dining table. "Just your family in your own living room—zero campaign banners, zero podiums."

"Before we hit record," Katie said, turning her head slightly toward Sam, Mandy, and Sarah with the thorough legislative caution that had defined all eighteen years of her three Senate terms, "walk Nate, Heather, Brittany, and me through the five folders one last time. If we upload this video tonight, who has to receive formal legal notice on Monday morning so that no draft committee, no state primary ballot, and no federal agency can ever wrap our homes into the Secret Service bubble during the 2044 election?"

Sam picked up the five folders in order, ticking each one off cleanly.

"Folder One goes by courier Monday morning to the Secretary of Homeland Security and all five congressional leaders on the candidate-protection advisory committee under Title 18, Section 3056(a)(7)," Sam explained. "While a sitting President or Vice President is barred by statute from refusing Secret Service protection, Section 3056(a) explicitly provides that major-candidate protection may be declined. Your signed letter puts Homeland Security and the Senate and House leadership on formal advance notice that you are not a candidate for President or Vice President in 2044—and that even if anyone ever tried to designate you, you formally exercise your statutory right in advance to decline any Secret Service detail."

"Folder Two is the fifty-state ballot shield," Mandy continued, tapping a stack of crisp pages stamped with her Virginia notary seal. "Fifteen states—including Michigan, Wisconsin, Maryland, Oregon, Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and Nebraska—have automatic media-recognition laws requiring their Secretaries of State to print on the presidential primary ballot every national figure discussed in the press unless that person files a sworn, notarized Affidavit of Non-Candidacy. We prepared notarized Affidavits of Non-Candidacy for every automatic-ballot Secretary of State and State Board of Elections in the country, swearing under oath that you are not and will not become a candidate in 2044."

"Folder Three goes to the Federal Election Commission under 11 C.F.R. Section 100.3," Sarah said, holding up the third packet, "formally disavowing every unauthorized 'Draft Katie Prairie' committee within the statutory thirty-day window so none of their fundraising can trigger federal candidate status. Folder Four goes to the Chair and the Secretary of the Democratic National Committee—your irrevocable Sherman declaration instructing the party that you refuse to participate in any debate, refuse the seating of any draft delegates, and will not accept a nomination from the convention floor and would not serve if elected. And Folder Five goes to the Virginia Department of Elections in Richmond confirming that you are retiring at the conclusion of your third Senate term on January 3, 2045."

Katie looked at the five folders, looked up at Heather, Wesley, Brittany, and Chelsea behind us, and let out a long, relieved breath. Rolling over to the dining table first, she signed her clear, steady signature—*Katie Prairie*—across every notarized affidavit and statutory notice while Mandy pressed her embossed seal onto the final sheet, and then rolled right back beside my wheelchair in front of the sofa.

"Ready when you are, Nate," Katie said quietly, lacing the fingers of her left hand through my right hand while Heather squeezed my shoulder.

I tapped the wireless shutter remote in my palm, and the small red recording light glowed on my mPhone.

"Good evening from our home here in Virginia Beach," fifty-eight-year-old Katie said calmly into the quiet living room, her voice warm, unscripted, and crystal clear. "I am sitting here tonight with the five people who share my everyday home life: my twin brother Nate, his wife Heather, Heather's cousin Wesley, and our cousins Brittany and Chelsea. Over the past few months, governors, colleagues, and kind citizens across the country have asked me to enter the 2044 presidential race for the term beginning in January of 2045, and draft committees have begun circulating petitions in primary states. I am deeply grateful for your trust—and tonight, surrounded by my family, I want to tell you plainly why my answer is an irrevocable no."

Beside me, Katie gave my hand a gentle squeeze.

"Sixteen Octobers ago, Nate and I flew down to Chambers Field at Naval Station Norfolk aboard Air Force One with President Clara Morales," Katie continued evenly. "Clara Morales was an honorable President, and restoring our sailors' stolen housing and pier funds that day—along with watching her sign our Supported Companions and Waiver Portability Act in the restored Rose Garden the following June—are two of the proudest memories of my Senate career. That Thursday in Norfolk was also the very first time Nate and I ever stepped inside the presidential Secret Service bubble. Even behind the gates of a military base, we watched every cross-street freeze thirty minutes ahead of our wheels, and we watched a good President realize she couldn't even take ten unscripted steps off a pre-swept carpet to buy a cold drink for herself. Under federal law, once you become President of the United States, you can never decline that bubble. Armed agents and armored motorcades shut down every street you touch, pull public buses out of service, and move permanently into your family's home."

Katie glanced affectionately back at Heather, Wesley, Chelsea, and Brittany behind us.

"Our cousin Chelsea survived an abusive group home before we brought her home, and for fourteen years she and Wesley have shared quiet, peaceful connected companion suites right off our kitchens where strangers, checkpoints, and cameras never intrude," Katie said softly. "Our family waited years to build a peaceful life together under one roof. And Nate and I fought our entire lives for the simple freedom to roll down to our corner bus stop on Military Highway, tap our fare passes alongside our neighbors, and go wherever we please unannounced. Monday morning, my staff is filing notarized Affidavits of Non-Candidacy with state election officials nationwide, disavowal notices with the Federal Election Commission, an irrevocable refusal of nomination with the Democratic National Committee, and formal declination notices under federal statute so I am never placed on a 2044 ballot or wrapped inside a Secret Service detail. When my third Senate term ends on January 3, 2045, I will retire from elected office—though anyone seeking my endorsement in next spring's primary should be ready to talk to everyday Virginians without a motorcade. Thank you for eighteen wonderful years, and good night from all six of us."

"Good night!" Chelsea chimed in happily over my shoulder while Wesley smiled warmly and gave a single friendly wave beside his blue silicone ring before I clicked the remote to stop the recording.

## Saturday, October 24, 2043, 9:45 PM — Six Under One Patchwork Quilt on Our Solid-Oak Alaskan King

By quarter to ten that night, Sam, Mandy, and Sarah had walked back to their ranch house two streets over with the five sealed express-mail packets tucked safely in Sam's messenger bag, the unedited four-minute refusal video was quietly live on StreamCast, The Grid, and Chirp with comments turned off, and our brick ranch house belonged completely to our six-person family again. Inside the connected companion suite, fifty-six-year-old Wesley and sixty-one-year-old Chelsea finished feeding their painted turtles their evening pellets, switched the seventy-five-gallon tank over to its soft amber night lamp, and walked hand in hand through the open solid-oak pocket door down the widened hallway toward our master bedroom.

Inside our master suite, the warm amber glow of two bedside lamps lit the nine-foot solid-oak, wheelchair-transfer-height six-person Alaskan King platform bed we had built both here in Virginia Beach and up at Sunset Drive in Alexandria, topped with deep orthopedic mattresses, soft flannel sheets, bolstered side rails, and our oversized patchwork navy-and-cream quilt.

For more than thirteen years after our estranged mother Brenda had bang-searched the back alley outside Apartment A6 at Greenway Court in August of 2003, Katie and I had been so conditioned by trauma and autism that the only place on earth we felt safe enough to fall asleep was zipped inside a sleeping bag on a hard carpeted closet floor behind a latched door, while Heather and Wesley had slept in side-by-side bedrooms down the hall so Wesley could climb into Heather's bed whenever a storm scared him. Even after Brittany and Chelsea crammed into that six-foot bedroom closet beside Katie and me in 2016, it had taken months of gentle, patient twenty-minute chamomile-tea tries led by Heather and Brittany—and building one bed wide enough for all six of us—before we had rolled up our closet sleeping bags forever on February 11, 2017 and migrated Wesley full-time onto the Alaskan King right next to Heather.

Rolling my SwiftChair parallel to the smooth wooden transfer edge beside Katie's frame tonight, I locked my brakes and slid smoothly across into my lifelong spot in the warm center of the quilt in my grey plaid flannel pajamas. On my right, fifty-eight-year-old Heather snuggled warmly against my shoulder in her forest-green flannel pajamas while fifty-six-year-old Wesley stretched out peacefully and securely right beside Heather in his navy flannel holding his smooth blue silicone ring—still falling asleep in two minutes flat every night knowing Heather was right there beside him. On my left, Katie settled onto the plush pillows in her soft lavender knit sleepwear lacing her fingers through mine, while sixty-one-year-old Chelsea curled up cozily beside Katie hugging Shelly Turtle beneath her two inhalers on the oak headboard shelf and fifty-eight-year-old Brittany climbed in on the outer edge in her burgundy flannel pajamas, smiling through her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses as she wrapped a warm arm around Chelsea and Katie.

Katie let out a long, blissful groan as the thick mattress cradled her shoulders and lower back.

"Remember when we used to insist the hall closet carpet was perfectly comfortable?" my fifty-eight-year-old twin sister murmured sleepily.

Brittany laughed softly across Chelsea's curls. "It only took eleven months on that closet plywood to convince the two of you."

"And twenty-six years on this Alaskan King hasn't gotten old once," Heather whispered fondly against my right collarbone, resting her left hand reassuringly over Wesley's arm as his breathing slowed into deep, tranquil sleep.

"Bed is soft," Chelsea murmured contentedly, snuggling Shelly Turtle beneath her chin. "All six safe."

Lying right there in the warm center of our solid-oak Alaskan King—with my wife Heather and Wesley peaceful on my right, my twin sister Katie holding my left hand beside Chelsea and Brittany, and our two quiet SwiftChairs parked side by side in the soft glow—I felt my whole chest relax.

Out in Washington and across fifty state capitals, politicians were spending hundreds of millions of dollars tonight fighting each other for the privilege of getting locked inside an armored limousine for the rest of their lives. Surrounded right here by the five people I loved most in the world, I already had the best spot on earth.
