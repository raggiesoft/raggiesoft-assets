## Saturday, July 12, 2003, 9:00 AM — Stops One and Two in Norfolk: CCHR & Virginia Dominion University

"Ten campuses on one laminated clipboard—nine across Virginia plus North Star up in Minnesota next month—zero deadline pressure, and Wesley holds the marker," eighteen-year-old Heather announced cheerfully at nine o'clock on a bright July Saturday morning outside the Workforce and Inclusive Transition Lab on the Norfolk campus of the Community College of Hampton Roads.

Aunt Linda's idea around our kitchen table three weeks ago had turned out to be pure gold. With sixteen-year-old Wesley sitting right between his sophomore and junior years at Northwood High School—two full school years ahead of his own graduation—none of us had to guess under a stopwatch where Wesley would want to go once high school ended, or where Katie, Heather, and I should transfer together once we finished our two-year associate credits. Because wherever Katie and I went, we went together, and wherever Heather went, Wesley went with her, we had the whole summer before August classes began to let Wesley walk and feel every single campus at his own unhurried pace.

Stepping across the low-pile carpet of CCHR's quiet Norfolk transition lab in his sky-blue polo shirt and khakis, Wesley turned his smooth blue silicone ring twice between his left thumb and forefinger and studied the row of matte-screen workstations. Slipping a pair of cushioned noise-dampening headphones carefully over his sandy-reddish-blond hair when I gestured toward the end desk, sixteen-year-old Wesley tapped through a sample visual-schedule lesson and gave a calm one-note hum—comfortable with the quiet lab itself, even if the narrow commuter hallways outside looked tight whenever summer-session classes let out at once.

By quarter to eleven, Heather had driven our group ten minutes west to Stop Two along Hampton Boulevard: Virginia Dominion University.

Rolling hub to hub with Katie down the wide, flat light-gray concrete plaza of Dominion Mall beneath shady live oaks and blooming pink crape myrtles—past contemporary buff-brick and blue-tinted glass buildings without a single curb step in sight—we followed Heather through the automatic sliding doors of the education building to peek inside the quiet assistive-communication clinic and student transition lounge. Sixteen-year-old Wesley paused by the wide first-floor window looking back toward Hampton Boulevard, relaxing his shoulders noticeably as summer students strolled past on the wide concrete mall without staring at our wheelchairs or crowding his elbow, before tapping a neat check mark beside Stop Two so we knew he was ready for lunch.

Back at the accessible bay in Lot 12, Heather clicked the four-point floor tie-downs snug around Katie's and my SwiftChair frames inside the lowered channel of Aunt Linda's accessible side-ramp minivan and handed back chilled juice boxes from the cooler.

"Southside Norfolk stops done," Heather smiled, buckling Wesley into the front passenger bucket seat before sliding behind the wheel. "Now we head across the harbor tunnel for our two Peninsula stops—Warwick University in Newport News first, and the College of Charles and Anne up in Williamsburg before supper."

"Honestly, having you drive us across the water today is a lifesaver, Heather," Katie called forward over the hum of the tires as our minivan merged north onto Interstate 64 toward Willoughby Spit and dipped into the bright sodium-lit underwater tube of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel. "Ask Nate and me anything about Southside CVTA transfers from Virginia Beach Boulevard down to Emerald Creek Mall and we can recite every timepoint in our sleep, except the second you cross north of the harbor into Hampton and Newport News, neither one of us knows a single Peninsula bus route."

"Not one," I agreed with a laugh beside my sister, lacing my left fingers comfortably through Katie's right hand as we climbed back out into bright Peninsula sunshine past Fort Monroe and Mercury Boulevard. "Southside route numbers start in the single digits and twenties; up here everything is in the hundreds, and Katie and I have never once had a reason to ride a bus on this side of the water."

"That's why teams have drivers *and* Southside route encyclopedias," Heather grinned in the rearview mirror, signaling cleanly onto Jefferson Avenue and turning west toward U.S. Route 60 and State Route 312 into Newport News. "Hold onto your pretzel bag, Wes—Stop Three coming up on the right."

## Saturday, July 12, 2003, 1:45 PM — Stop Three at Warwick University Beside 112 NNTC & Stop Four on the Uneven Colonial Bricks of Charles & Anne

![Along the wide red-brick campus walkway of Warwick University in Newport News in front of a Neo-Georgian white-columned brick hall as a blue-and-silver CVTA bus exits the landscaped SR-312 and Shoe Lane roundabout on the left with its amber LED sign reading 112 NNTC, eighteen-year-old Katie and Nathaniel Prairie sit side by side in their two compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs holding inner hands while eighteen-year-old Heather stands warmly beside Nate's chair and sixteen-year-old Wesley holds a warm pretzel bag and shares a happy fist-bump with Nate.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b004/oct_2005_warwick_university_shoe_lane_roundabout_112_nntc_wesley_tour.jpg)

"Look at the brickwork on this quad compared to Hampton Boulevard," Katie marveled softly at a quarter to two, easing her SwiftChair hub to hub beside mine along the wide, barrier-free herringbone red-brick walkway curving past Warwick University's stately Neo-Georgian academic hall—its tall white Doric columns and crisp white rooftop cupola gleaming above a landscaped circular roundabout.

For nearly an hour, Warwick University's summer transition liaison had walked the four of us at an unhurried, respectful pace through small-group seminar rooms and the sunlit dining hall—speaking directly to sixteen-year-old Wesley at eye level rather than talking over his head to Heather. Now, with a brown paper bakery bag of warm salted soft pretzel bites tucked securely in Wesley's left hand beside his blue silicone ring, we strolled out toward the sunny campus perimeter where Shoe Lane met State Route 312 at the brick-and-flowerbed roundabout.

At the low rumble of a diesel engine easing counterclockwise around the central flowerbed island, Wesley's ears perked up instantly.

Exiting the roundabout cleanly down the approach lane to our left—its right-side passenger boarding doors tracking smoothly along the outer concrete curb—rolled a clean blue-and-silver Coastal Virginia Transit Authority low-floor coach, its rectangular amber LED destination header glowing crisp and bright above the windshield: <led>112 NNTC</led>.

Wesley beamed from ear to ear, lifted his chin toward the glowing amber sign, and let out a happy, rising two-note vocal hum so I wouldn't miss it before reaching his right fist straight across to bump my knuckles.

"Good eye, Wes—Route 112!" I smiled, glancing curiously at the four uppercase letters beside the route number as the Peninsula coach glided past the crosswalk. "What does *NNTC* stand for up here, Heather?"

"Newport News Transit Center—the main downtown Peninsula transfer hub down at Thirty-Fifth Street and Washington Avenue by the shipyard," Heather explained easily, walking right beside my right wheel. "All the Peninsula hundred-series routes—the 112 here at the Shoe Lane roundabout, plus the 106 and 107 over on U.S. Route 60—funnel down to Thirty-Fifth and Washington."

Thirty-five minutes later, after climbing back into the air-conditioned minivan and driving twenty-five miles farther west up Interstate 64 into historic Williamsburg for Stop Four at the College of Charles and Anne, the contrast underneath our wheelchair casters couldn't have been sharper.

Beneath towering, centuries-old gnarled oaks shading the historic green, the ancient campus of Charles and Anne looked like an oil painting out of the seventeen-hundreds—and rode like a washboard road. Within fifty yards of turning onto the historic sunken courtyard path, three-hundred-year-old handmade clay bricks heaved up by massive tree roots had both Katie's and my front SwiftChair casters chattering and rattling so hard our knuckles bounced against our armrests. Half the colonial brick halls flanking the yard sat behind worn slate steps with narrow doorways and tiny retrofitted lifts tucked down dim service alleys.

Without anybody prompting him, sixteen-year-old Wesley stopped dead beside an uneven ridge of sunken brick, looked straight down at Katie's vibrating footrests, frowned deeply in loyal concern for his friends, and gave one firm side-to-side shake of his head before pointing back toward the van.

"Couldn't have summed up seventeenth-century masonry better myself, Wes," Katie laughed warmly, reaching up to pat Wesley's forearm as we carefully turned our chairs back toward the level lot—four Tidewater stops down on Wesley's rising-junior summer list, five more across Richmond, Northern Virginia, and the Shenandoah mountains next weekend, and our August trip up to Minnesota waiting right behind them.
