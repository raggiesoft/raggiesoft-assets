## Saturday, August 2, 2003, 11:20 AM CDT — Meeting Heather and Wesley's Minnesota Family & Touring Stop Ten at North Star State University

"Hold the screen door wide, Grandpa—Nate and Katie made it all the way up from the ocean without the airline losing a single battery charger!" eighteen-year-old Heather laughed brightly as Aunt Linda eased her rented silver accessible minivan up to a freshly built, sturdy birch-plywood ramp bolted cleanly over the two front steps of a white clapboard farmhouse twenty minutes outside Saint Paul.

Before Katie and I had even finished rolling down the side ramp onto the level concrete driveway under tall northern white pines, Heather's gray-haired maternal grandfather—still brushing fresh birch sawdust off his flannel cuffs from building that porch ramp at seven o'clock this morning just for us—stepped out onto the grass beside Heather's grandmother, two smiling aunts, and three cousins holding chilled pitchers of lemonade.

Nobody leaned over our armrests, patted our heads, or talked about us in the third person.

Instead, Grandpa shook my right hand and Katie's right hand firmly at eye level, Grandma set a plate of warm cinnamon rolls directly on the low patio table beside our chairs, and sixteen-year-old Wesley—positively glowing in his favorite blue polo shirt on his home summer turf—proudly led Katie and me down the paved garden walk to show off the giant backyard oak tire swing he had played on every August since he was five, tapping his chest twice and pointing straight at me so every aunt, uncle, and cousin on the lawn knew beyond a shadow of a doubt that I was his guy friend from Virginia.

On Monday morning, August fourth, Heather, Aunt Linda, sixteen-year-old Wesley, Katie, and I rode into the Twin Cities together to check off Stop Ten at the very bottom of Wesley's summer clipboard: North Star State University.

For two transit fans from Virginia Beach, boarding the wide, low-floor intercampus *NSSU Campus Connector* shuttle bus on its dedicated transitway bridge high above the sparkling Mississippi River—gliding smoothly from the bustling urban Minneapolis East Bank over to the leafy brick quads of the Saint Paul campus—was genuinely cool. Inside the education and inclusive-transition offices on the Saint Paul side, the advisors welcomed Wesley warmly, and the flat upper-midwestern walkways felt effortless under our SwiftChair wheels in the seventy-eight-degree August sunshine.

Then our student tour guide pointed overhead toward the enclosed glass second-story pedestrian skyways and led us down an elevator into the miles of fluorescent subterranean *Gopher Way* tunnels linking the lecture halls underground.

"Once November hits and temperatures drop to fifteen or twenty below zero with two feet of lake-effect snow blowing off the river," the guide explained cheerfully, "almost nobody walks outside at street level until April—everybody uses the tunnels and the skyways!"

Down in that windowless concrete tunnel, Katie and I glanced once at the narrow double fire doors, thought simultaneously of what twenty-below-zero Minnesota January ice drifts would do to CVTA-style bus stops and twenty-four-volt wheelchair batteries—on top of forfeiting Heather's hard-earned Virginia in-state tuition domicile—and saw sixteen-year-old Wesley tug gently on Heather's sleeve pointing back up toward the sunny Virginia beach-bus sticker on Katie's binder.

That evening back at Grandpa and Grandma's farmhouse dining table over homemade wild-rice soup, with all ten campuses across Virginia and Minnesota honestly seen ahead of Wesley's junior year of high school, nobody had to pretend or guess anymore. By the time Wesley walked across Northwood High's stage two Junes from now, everybody around that table knew Tidewater was home—and hugging Heather's grandparents goodbye on the birch ramp Wednesday morning meant Katie and I now had extended family cheering for us up north too, without anybody ever having to move thirteen hundred miles apart.

## Wednesday, May 12, 2004, 4:35 PM — Our Norfolk Apartment, Brandon's Red Flags, & Wesley's Sentinel Glare

"Oh, come on, Katie—you're nineteen years old, you don't seriously need a whole entourage tagging along just to grab iced coffee up in Williamsburg on Saturday," twenty-three-year-old Brandon Mercer laughed smoothly nine months later from the middle of our zero-step Norfolk living-room rug, flashing a practiced, overly familiar smile as he took an uninvited step toward the rear push-handles of Katie's SwiftChair.

Starting our freshman associate classes together at CCHR last fall had been everything Katie, Heather, and I had hoped for—and whenever sixteen-year-old Wesley rode over with Heather after his junior-year afternoons at Northwood High to do homework at our dining table, our ground-floor apartment right on CVTA Routes 2 and 15 felt like the safest harbor in Virginia. Katie had met Brandon three afternoons earlier outside a CCHR general-education lecture hall when he held an exterior glass door; invited over to meet our household in broad daylight before Katie ever agreed to go anywhere off campus with him, Brandon hadn't lasted four minutes before showing every card up his sleeve. The moment Wesley walked out of the kitchen carrying his blue silicone ring and hummed a polite two-note hello, Brandon had smirked sideways as if Wesley couldn't understand plain English—and now he was trying to peel my twin sister forty-five miles up Interstate 64 without me.

"Don't touch my sister's push-handles," I said flatly, rolling my chair six inches forward so my front caster blocked his shoe.

Before Brandon could even pivot into a fake apology, nineteen-year-old Heather stepped straight out from the kitchen breakfast bar with a dish towel still folded in her hand—her hazel eyes sharp as laser glass.

"Let me save everybody a whole lot of time, Brandon," Heather said in a crisp, level voice that froze his hand in midair. "First, nobody in this apartment ever talks over Wesley like he isn't standing in the room. Second, nobody touches Katie's or Nate's wheelchairs without permission. And third, when a twenty-three-year-old guy walks into a nineteen-year-old girl's living room for the very first time and immediately tries to talk her into riding an hour out of town alone without her twin brother, every single person in this room knows what kind of controlling garbage that is."

Brandon's polite mask cracked in an instant, an ugly red flush creeping up his collar. "Excuse me? I wasn't talking to you—"

"Yes, you are, right on your way out our front door," Katie cut in coolly, backing her chair two inches beside mine and lacing her fingers firmly through my hand. "Lose my email address, Brandon. Don't sit near us at CCHR again."

Right beside Heather's left shoulder, six-foot-tall Wesley didn't say a spoken word. Stepping squarely into the center of the rug between Brandon's loafers and our two footrests, Wesley planted his sneakers shoulder-width apart, folded both forearms tight across his navy polo shirt over his smooth blue silicone ring, and fixed Brandon with an unblinking, immovable sentinel glare while pointing one thumb straight at the front doorlatch.

Brandon looked once from Wesley's broad shoulders to Heather's unyielding face to Katie's and my locked wheels, muttered something bitter under his breath, yanked the front door open, and disappeared down the breezeway walk for good.

Two months later in July of 2004, when Aunt Linda's sudden cardiac hospitalization meant she could no longer manage heavy daily physical care alone and nineteen-year-old Heather stood before a Norfolk Circuit Court judge to be appointed Wesley's plenary legal guardian and conservator ahead of his senior year, Dad spent an entire Saturday with his cordless drill helping the four of us settle into a sunlit three-bedroom corner apartment five minutes from Virginia Dominion University—four teammates under one roof where nobody would ever back either of us into a corner, and where rising-senior Wesley already knew down to the sidewalk brick which local campus had won his heart.
