The morning sun filtered through the large window of their room at the Three Rivers Grand Hotel, casting a warm glow over the four of them as they stirred awake. The routine of waking up together, untangling from the blankets, and transitioning into the day was becoming a well-worn, comfortable rhythm.

They started their second full day in Pittsburgh with a quiet, satisfying room service breakfast. Stella had ordered a massive spread of pancakes, eggs, and bacon, ensuring everyone was well-fueled for the day ahead. Sitting around the small table by the window, they ate while watching the morning commuter traffic cross the massive steel bridges spanning the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers. 

Leo was quiet, thoughtfully chewing his bacon. The deep, heavy sleep surrounded by his fortress had restored some of his physical energy, but the underlying emotional current—the steady drumbeat of time slipping away—was still there, humming just beneath the surface. 

"Okay, team," Stella announced, wiping her mouth with a napkin and pulling out her trusty clipboard. "Today is going to be a little different. Yesterday was ancient history and dinosaurs. Today, we're doing modern art."

She had mapped out a route to the Garrison Museum of Modern Art, located just across the river. It was dedicated to one of Pittsburgh's most famous native sons, a pioneer of the pop art movement. 

A short 3RTA bus ride later, they found themselves rolling through the doors of a sleek, renovated industrial building. If the natural history museum had been dim, hushed, and low-sensory, the Garrison Museum was the exact opposite. 

It was an explosion of color and repetition. Entire walls were covered in bright, neon silk-screens of celebrity portraits and commercial soup cans. The galleries echoed with the sound of avant-garde film projections. It was a visually loud, highly stimulating environment. 

Leo gripped the wheels of his manual chair as they moved through the first floor. It was fascinating, but it was also a lot to process. He felt the familiar urge to retreat, to pull his hat down and block out the visual noise. 

But then he looked at Olivia. She was staring at a massive, neon-pink portrait with wide, delighted eyes. "This is so cool," she whispered, pointing out the subtle variations in the repeated prints.

Leo saw her joy, and the urge to retreat vanished, replaced by a desperate, heartbreaking desire to perform. He wanted her to have a perfect day. He wanted to be the perfect companion. He forced a smile, pushing his chair closer to the artwork and tapping enthusiastically on his Quantum Communicator. 

"VERY BRIGHT. I LIKE THE COLORS," he typed, showing the screen to Olivia. 

She beamed at him. "Right? It's like looking at a comic book blown up to the size of a building."

For the next two hours, Leo maintained the mask. He smiled, he pointed, and he engaged with the art. He navigated the crowded galleries, absorbing the overwhelming visual input without complaint. He was a master of his own presentation, convincing everyone—even himself, for fleeting moments—that he was simply enjoying a beautiful morning of art with his family. 

But beneath the forced smiles and the enthusiastic typing, the emotional toll was mounting. The effort to remain present, combined with the sensory load of the bright pop art, was quietly, steadily draining his reserves. The "ticking clock" was still there, loud and persistent in his mind, and the bright neon colors of the museum were only serving to illuminate his exhaustion.
