## 8:45 AM — Good Morning, Chicago

The gentle rocking of the *Capitol Limited* came to a gradual halt. Leo blinked his eyes open in the lower bunk of their Family Bedroom. The night had been a blur of dark, rolling plains and the steady, rhythmic clatter of the tracks. 

"Good morning, Chicago!" Luna declared brightly, pulling back the heavy curtain on the cabin window. 

Sunlight flooded the tiny room, revealing a sweeping view of a massive railyard transitioning into a dense, towering skyline of glass and steel. They had officially arrived in the Midwest. 

The train pulled slowly into the grand, subterranean depths of Chicago Union Station. Unlike the smaller, quieter station in Pittsburgh, Chicago's terminal was a colossal, thrumming hive of activity. It was the great rail hub of America. 

After wrestling their bags out of the cramped cabin and successfully navigating Leo's chair down the narrow Superliner hallway and out onto the platform, they rode the elevator up to the Great Hall. The sheer scale of the room—with its towering Corinthian columns, sweeping marble staircases, and massive arched skylight—was breathtaking. 

"Alright," Stella said, surveying the bustling morning crowds of commuters. "We're officially on Central Time, so we gained an hour. It's almost 9:00 AM. Let's grab some breakfast right here in the station before we tackle the transit system."

They found a quiet corner in the food court, eating hot breakfast sandwiches and drinking strong coffee while watching the endless stream of people crisscrossing the Great Hall. Leo watched the crowds with a mix of awe and trepidation. The scale of the city was intimidating, but he was surrounded by his fortress, well-rested after the long night on the train. 

"So, where's the hotel?" Olivia asked, wiping a smear of ketchup from her thumb.

Stella tapped her clipboard. "I booked us into The Heritage House. It's an iconic, historic hotel right in the middle of The Loop, about a mile east of here. To get there, we get our first taste of the Chicago transit system."

"The CMA?" Luna asked, remembering the transit briefings Stella had given them weeks ago.

"Exactly," Stella nodded. "The Chicago Metropolitan Authority. We're going to buy our magnetic CityCards from the kiosks upstairs, and then we're going to catch a city bus straight down Jackson Boulevard. We drop the bags at the hotel, and then the city is ours."

Leo finished his sandwich and rested his hands on his wheels, looking up at the grand skylight of Union Station. They were deeper into the country now. The air felt different, the pace felt faster, and the architecture was staggering. The timer was still ticking, but the bright, bustling energy of Chicago was an incredible distraction. He was ready to explore.

***

## 10:30 AM — Holding the Line

The CMA bus ride down Jackson Boulevard was a sensory overload of towering skyscrapers and roaring city traffic, a stark contrast to the quiet, scenic arrival they’d had in Pittsburgh. By the time they reached the opulent, gilded lobby of The Heritage House, they were all thoroughly exhausted from the overnight train ride. 

Standard check-in wasn't until 3:00 PM, but Stella, anticipating the exhaustion of a 9:00 AM arrival, had called ahead weeks ago to guarantee an early check-in. The desk clerk handed over the keycards, and moments later, they were dropping their heavy bags on the floor of a sprawling, beautifully decorated room with two queen beds. 

The moment the door clicked shut, the adrenaline of navigating the city faded. The exhaustion of sleeping upright or in cramped train bunks caught up with all of them. 

Leo was the first to seek out comfort. His nervous system was humming with the low-level anxiety of the new city, and he desperately needed grounding. He transferred out of his chair and crawled onto the center of the nearest bed, looking up at Olivia with a silent, pleading expression. 

She understood immediately. Olivia kicked off her shoes and climbed onto the mattress beside him, wrapping her arms tightly around his torso and burying her face in the crook of his neck. The familiar pressure of her embrace was an instant anchor. 

But as Leo lay there, he noticed Luna standing near the window, her shoulders slumped, rubbing her arms. The overnight train had been hard on her, too. He reached out a hand, offering a silent invitation. Luna didn't hesitate. She crawled onto the bed on his other side, throwing a leg over his and pressing her forehead against his shoulder. 

A moment later, he felt the mattress dip near his feet. Stella, usually the indefatigable commander of their journey, let out a long, weary sigh. She kicked off her sensible walking shoes and crawled up from the foot of the bed, settling her head against Luna's shoulder and resting her hand on Leo's ankle. 

It was a massive, tangled, four-way cuddle. A physical manifestation of the fortress. 

Lying there in the center of it, entirely surrounded by the deep pressure and warmth of his entire world, Leo felt an immense sense of safety. They were a single, breathing entity, anchored together against the chaos of the city outside. 

Yet, even wrapped in this absolute security, a quiet, persistent ache pulsed in his chest. The transition to Central Time had deeply unsettled him. It wasn't just the fatigue of losing an hour of sleep; it was the psychological reality of the timezone itself. The clocks had shifted. The earth had physically rotated them further away from the sun they were used to. It was undeniable, geographical proof of how far West they had come, and how much closer they were to Seattle. 

The timer was ticking louder than ever. The realization threatened to spike his heart rate, pushing him toward the edge of another panic. 

But then Luna shifted, sighing happily in her sleep, her weight pressing heavier against him. Olivia tightened her arms around his waist, her breathing slow and steady. Stella’s hand was a warm, grounding anchor on his leg. 

The panic crested, hovered, and then slowly began to recede. The physical reality of the timezone change was terrifying, but the physical reality of the pack surrounding him was stronger. Being held so tightly by both of his sisters and his best friend was enough to hold the line against the grief. For now.

***

## 2:00 PM — The Zenith Tower

After an hour of quiet, grounding sleep tangled together on the hotel bed, the pack finally began to stir. The heavy knot of anxiety in Leo's chest hadn't entirely vanished, but the deep pressure from his sisters and Olivia had successfully pushed it back to a manageable simmer. 

"Alright," Stella declared, stretching her arms above her head and slipping back into her "tour guide" persona. "Who wants to go to the top of the world?"

Their destination for the afternoon was the Zenith Tower. At 110 stories tall, the massive, black-steel monolith was an architectural marvel and the undisputed king of the Chicago skyline. For years, it had held the title of the tallest building in the world. 

Neither Luna nor Leo had ever been in a skyscraper before. Growing up in the flat, coastal sprawl of Virginia Beach, the tallest things they usually saw were beachfront hotels. The prospect of riding an elevator to the 103rd-floor Skydeck was genuinely thrilling. 

"I want to see if we can see across Lake Michigan," Luna said excitedly as Olivia pushed Leo's wheelchair along the busy sidewalks toward the tower. 

<aac>I want to see the trains from above,</aac> Leo typed on his communicator, a rare spark of unburdened enthusiasm lighting up his eyes. 

After buying their tickets and passing through the crowded lobby, they packed into the high-speed elevator. The doors slid shut, and the elevator shot upward with a smooth, ear-popping intensity. A digital display above the door rapidly counted off the floors—10, 30, 50, 80—until finally, with a soft *ding*, they arrived at the 103rd floor.

The elevator doors opened, revealing a sprawling, panoramic observation deck enclosed entirely in massive panes of glass. The view was staggering. The city of Chicago spread out below them in a vast, sprawling grid of concrete, steel, and water. 

"Wow," Stella breathed, stepping out of the elevator. 

Olivia wheeled Leo right up to the nearest floor-to-ceiling window. Luna practically bounced with excitement as she ran up to stand beside his chair. 

"Look at this!" Luna grinned, pressing her hands against the glass. 

Leo leaned forward in his chair, gripping the armrests, eager to look for the tiny, toy-like trains weaving through the city grid below. He tilted his head down and looked straight past his footrests, past the window ledge, and down to the street. 

The sidewalk was 1,353 feet straight down. 

The cars looked like microscopic specks of dust. The people were entirely invisible. 

In a fraction of a second, the excitement evaporated. A primal, evolutionary alarm bell shattered Leo's consciousness. His nervous system didn't just spike; it short-circuited. His brain, suddenly processing the horrifying reality that he was suspended in a glass box a quarter of a mile in the sky, screamed at him that he was falling. 

He gasped, a sharp, choked sound, and threw himself backward in his chair, his hands flying up to cover his eyes. 

At the exact same moment, Luna let out a high-pitched, strangled yelp. She physically recoiled from the glass as if it had burned her, stumbling backward until her back hit the interior wall of the observation deck. 

"Nope. Nope. Nope," Luna gasped, her eyes wide with sudden, absolute terror. She sank to the floor, pulling her knees to her chest and wrapping her arms tightly around them. 

Leo was paralyzed in his chair, his chest heaving, his eyes squeezed shut so tightly it hurt. The vertigo was dizzying. Neither of them had known they were terrified of heights, because neither of them had ever been high enough to test it. 

Olivia and Stella, who had been admiring the view across the lake, spun around in alarm. 

"Whoa, hey," Olivia said, instantly abandoning the view and dropping to her knees beside Leo's chair. She grabbed his hands, pulling them gently away from his face. "Leo, look at me. Look at me."

Leo opened his eyes, but his gaze was wild and unfocused. He aggressively pointed a trembling finger toward the interior wall, desperate to get away from the glass. 

Stella was already crouched beside Luna, rubbing her back. "Okay, okay. I've got you," Stella murmured, realizing instantly what had happened. It was a shared, identical twin phobia, triggered at the exact same second. 

<aac>Get me away from the window,</aac> Leo typed frantically on his communicator, hitting the keys so hard the swing-arm rattled. 

Olivia didn't hesitate. She unlocked the brakes on his chair and quickly pulled him backward, turning the chair around so he was facing the solid interior wall, right next to where Luna was curled up on the carpet. 

The Skydeck, which just a minute ago had been the thrilling highlight of the day, was now a 103-story prison.

***

## 2:15 PM — The Descent

The 103rd-floor Skydeck, a bustling tourist attraction full of chattering families and flashing cameras, felt like a vacuum of terror for the twins. 

Luna was crying now, quiet, gasping sobs as she stayed curled in a tight ball against the interior plaster wall. She had her eyes squeezed shut, refusing to look anywhere near the perimeter of the room. "I want to go down," she whimpered, her voice tight with panic. "Stella, please. I want to go downstairs. *Now*."

Stella, who had paid a small fortune for the four Skydeck tickets, didn't hesitate for a single microsecond. The view, the money, the experience—none of it mattered. "Okay," Stella said softly, her voice an anchor of absolute, unwavering calm. "We're leaving right now. Do not open your eyes, Luna. Just listen to my voice."

Leo was still trembling violently in his wheelchair, his chest heaving as his own brain struggled to process the adrenaline spike. He was parked directly beside Luna, facing the same blank interior wall. 

Olivia stood up, moving to grab the handles of Leo's wheelchair, but Luna reached out a shaking hand and grabbed the metal frame of the chair first. 

"I'll push him," Luna gasped, struggling to her feet while keeping her back pressed firmly against the wall. She didn't want Olivia to do it. She needed the physical anchor. She needed to feel the heavy, grounding weight of her twin's wheelchair under her hands to prove she was tethered to something solid. 

"You got it," Olivia said smoothly, stepping aside and moving to flank them instead, creating a physical barrier between the twins and the horrifying glass windows. 

With Luna gripping the push-handles like a lifeline and keeping her eyes locked firmly on the carpet, she pushed Leo blindly forward as Stella guided them straight back to the elevator banks in the center core of the building. 

The moment they were inside the elevator and the heavy steel doors slid shut, sealing them inside a windowless, solid box, the collective relief was palpable. 

The elevator began its rapid, ear-popping descent. 

Luna immediately let go of the push-handles and stepped around to the front of the wheelchair. She sank to her knees right on the elevator floor and threw her arms around Leo's neck, burying her tear-streaked face in his shoulder. 

Leo didn't have the motor control to hug her back effectively, but he leaned his head heavily against hers, pressing his cheek against her hair. He closed his eyes, focusing entirely on the solid pressure of her embrace and the familiar scent of her shampoo. 

Both of them were hyperventilating, their chests heaving in synchronized, ragged gasps as the digital display above the door rapidly counted down the floors. 100... 80... 50... 

Stella stood behind the wheelchair, placing one hand firmly on Luna's shoulder and the other on the back of Leo's neck. It was a silent, steadying pressure. Olivia stood close by, her presence a quiet guard over the pack. 

"Breathe, you two," Stella murmured gently over the hum of the descending elevator. "You're safe. We're in a solid box. We're going back to the ground."

"I hated that," Luna choked out, her grip on Leo tightening. "I hated that so much."

"ME TOO," Leo's communicator chimed, his thumb trembling as he hit the pre-programmed button. 

The digital display hit *1*, and the elevator slowed to a smooth stop. The doors slid open, revealing the bustling, windowless, solid-stone lobby of the Zenith Tower. 

They had made it back to ground level. The terrifying ordeal was over, but it had drained whatever energy the afternoon nap had given them. The twins were exhausted, their nervous systems completely fried. The Midwestern leg of the great journey had officially begun with a spectacular misfire.

***

## 2:20 PM — Solid Ground

The ground-floor lobby of the Zenith Tower was a cavernous, echoing space lined with heavy travertine marble and polished black granite. It was solid, windowless, and reassuringly attached to the earth. 

Stella guided them to a quiet alcove near the security desk, far away from the bustling queues of tourists waiting to ascend to the Skydeck. As soon as the wheelchair came to a stop, the adrenaline that had fueled their frantic escape finally crashed. 

Luna, who was still kneeling in front of Leo, buried her face in his shoulder again, her shoulders shaking with fresh, quiet sobs. The sheer, primal terror of the experience was finally catching up to her. 

Leo leaned his head against hers, tears spilling hot and fast down his own cheeks, fogging up his thick glasses. His nervous system was completely fried. He hadn't just been scared; he had been genuinely, fundamentally convinced that he was going to fall to his death. 

"I'm sorry," Luna choked out, her voice muffled against his t-shirt. "I'm so sorry, Leo. We didn't know. I didn't know."

<aac>It is okay,</aac> he managed to type, his thumb shaking so badly he hit the wrong key twice before getting the message out. 

It was a profound, startling realization for both of them. In eighteen years, neither twin had ever been higher than a third-floor balcony. They simply hadn't known they possessed a shared, debilitating fear of extreme heights. But it made a twisted kind of sense; their brains were wired with so many identical quirks and sensitivities, it was only natural that their phobias would match, too. 

As they clung to each other, crying and trying to regulate their ragged breathing, Leo felt a different kind of ache flare up in his chest. The panic of the tower was fading, but the heavy, relentless dread of the ticking clock was rushing in to fill the void. 

He lifted his head from Luna's shoulder and looked up, his tear-filled eyes desperately searching the alcove. 

Olivia was standing right there. She didn't need him to ask. She immediately knelt down beside Luna, wrapping her long arms around both of the twins in a tight, encompassing embrace. 

Leo slumped against her side, letting out a long, shuddering exhale. He needed the deep pressure of his twin sister to recover from the phobia, but he needed the specific, unwavering warmth of Olivia to remind him that she was still here. That she hadn't left for Washington state yet. That they were still together on solid ground. 

Stella stood a few feet back, giving them the space they needed while maintaining a watchful perimeter. She leaned against the cool marble wall, letting out a long, quiet breath of her own. Her heart was still pounding from the suddenness of the crisis. 

For several long minutes, the alcove was quiet except for the muffled sounds of the twins crying, anchored together in the safety of the fortress. The polished marble floor beneath them was hard, cold, and beautifully, undeniably solid.

***

## 3:30 PM — Deep Dish

After twenty minutes of quiet recovery in the Zenith Tower lobby, the twins' tear-streaked faces had dried, and the acute panic had receded into a dull, manageable exhaustion. 

"Okay," Stella said, pushing herself off the marble wall. "We need a reset. We need something heavy, warm, and distinctly ground-level."

A quick conversation with a sympathetic security guard pointed them toward Russo's, a hole-in-the-wall pizzeria a few blocks away. It wasn't one of the flashy, tourist-trap chains with neon signs; it was a dimly lit, brick-walled establishment tucked down a side street, smelling powerfully of garlic, oregano, and baking dough. 

They snagged a corner booth, parking Leo's wheelchair at the end of the table. The ambiance was cozy and wonderfully cavernous—the exact opposite of a glass box suspended in the sky. 

When the waitress brought their order out thirty minutes later, it didn't look like a pizza. It looked like a cast-iron pie pan filled to the brim with a literal mountain of bubbling cheese, chunky tomato sauce, and thick, savory sausage. 

"Oh my god," Luna breathed, her eyes widening as the waitress used a heavy steel spatula to lift the first slice. Thick ropes of mozzarella stretched and pulled, refusing to let go of the pan. 

Stella stared at the monstrous slice as it was deposited onto her plate. The crust was nearly two inches deep. "I am going to need a nap after just looking at this," she muttered, picking up her fork. 

Olivia cut Leo's slice into manageable, bite-sized pieces for him. He used his fork to spear a chunk of the thick, buttery crust covered in cheese and sauce. The moment it hit his tongue, his eyes closed in sheer bliss. It was incredible. It was heavy, rich, and incredibly comforting—exactly the kind of grounding sensory input his fried nervous system craved. 

<aac>This is so good,</aac> Leo typed, reaching eagerly for his second bite. 

"It really is," Luna agreed, her mouth full. The terrifying ordeal at the Zenith Tower seemed a million miles away, replaced by the warm, buzzing atmosphere of the restaurant and the unbelievable weight of the food. 

Stella managed exactly one slice before she had to lay her fork down in defeat, pressing a hand to her stomach. "It's amazing," she admitted, looking at the massive, half-eaten pizza still sitting in the center of the table. "But I think my stomach just waved a white flag. That is an obscene amount of cheese."

Olivia, possessing the legendary metabolism of a tall, athletic nineteen-year-old, happily reached for her third slice. 

Leo watched his best friend eat, a quiet, contented warmth settling in his chest. The heavy food, the dimly lit brick walls, and the lively chatter of the pack were successfully drowning out the ticking clock, at least for the moment. He took another bite of his own pizza, letting the rich flavors ground him firmly to the earth.
