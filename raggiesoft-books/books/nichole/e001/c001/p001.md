## The Bachelor Pad

The heat in Newport wasn't like the heat in Virginia. In Virginia, the heat was a wet blanket that smelled of asphalt and swamp. Here, on Washington Street, the heat was salty. It stuck to your skin, smelling of drying seaweed and low tide.

Uncle Mark lived in a first-floor apartment in a shingled house that dated back to the 1800s. Like most houses in the neighborhood, it didn't believe in central air conditioning. The cooling system consisted of opening the front windows, opening the back windows, and praying for a breeze off Narragansett Bay.

"Welcome to the Bachelor Pad," Mark announced, throwing the front door open. He was wearing cargo shorts and a t-shirt stained with teak oil. "Step inside. It’s a cool... seventy-eight degrees in here."

Patrick rolled into the living room, followed by his father, Tom, pushing Nichole’s manual chair. The apartment didn't smell like their house in Virginia, which always smelled of lemon polish and aggressive laundry detergent. Mark’s place smelled of the ocean, stale coffee, cedar wood, and something sharp and metallic that Patrick would later identify as soldering flux.

It wasn't messy, exactly, but it was *dense*. The living room had a sofa, but the coffee table was buried under stacks of *Classic Keel* magazine and rolled-up blueprints for a hull redesign. A box fan sat in the window, rattling slightly as it churned the heavy air.

"We'll take the pull-out couch," Ellen said, eyeing the living room and the rattling fan. She wiped a sheen of sweat from her forehead. "Mark, are you sure about the kids in the office? Is there room?"

"It's the only other bed, El," Mark shrugged, grabbing two suitcases. "And honestly, it's the best room. It faces north. Doesn't get the morning sun baking the glass."

He gestured for them to follow. "Plus," Mark grinned at Patrick, "It's got the toys."

He led Patrick and Nichole down the narrow hallway. The floorboards creaked—old, solid wood that had settled over a century. Mark opened the door to the spare room. "Behold," Mark said. "The Cockpit."

Patrick stopped in the doorway. His jaw dropped. It wasn't a bedroom. It was a command center.

Along the right wall ran a massive workbench made of a solid-core door resting on two heavy sawhorses. It was a landscape of technology:

- A SignalMaster 5000 Transceiver with glowing amber dials and a heavy, coiled microphone cable.

- A Vulcan Soldering Station with a sponge that looked like a dried sea creature.

- A stack of Modern Tinkerer magazines teetering like a skyscraper.

- And the crown jewel: a Quantix Oscilloscope, currently humming, with a small green dot drifting lazily across the grid screen like a digital ghost.

"Whoa," Patrick whispered.

"And here's the crash pad," Mark said, pointing to the corner. It was a low, Japanese-style futon frame made of black lacquered wood. The mattress wasn't a soft, springy thing like Patrick’s bed at home. It was a dense, heavy slab of cotton batting covered in a navy blue canvas quilt.

"It's hard," Mark explained, pressing his hand onto the mattress. It barely gave. "But that's the point. Soft beds are quicksand. This?" He slapped the canvas. "This is a launchpad. Firm surface means easier transfers for Nichole. You can get leverage."

Patrick sat down on the edge. It felt like sitting on a gym mat. Solid. Predictable. He looked at Nichole. "It's low," Patrick noted. It was the exact same height as her wheelchair seat. No lifting required. Just a slide.

"Sliding transfer," Mark confirmed. "We designed it for efficiency."

Mark walked over to the workbench. He flipped a switch on a power strip. A small fan clipped to the desk started to whir, blowing air right over the bed. "You guys have the cross-breeze," Mark said. "Open the sash, turn on the fan, and you'll hear the bell buoys out in the harbor all night."

He looked at Patrick, who was eyeing the soldering iron. "Rules of the road," Mark said, his voice turning serious. "Do not touch the Vulcan unless I am in the room. It gets to 700 degrees. It will melt your skin." Patrick nodded, eyes wide. "Everything else?" Mark waved his hand at the radio, the scope, and the bins of parts. "Explore. Just don't transmit on the emergency frequencies."

Mark left the room to help Ellen with the cooler. Patrick and Nichole were left alone in the dim, humming room. It was hot. It smelled like solder and sea salt. The futon was as hard as a rock. But as Patrick looked at the green dot drifting on the Quantix screen, he thought it was the coolest place he had ever been.

He looked at Nichole. She was staring at the amber light of the radio. "This is gonna be a good trip," Patrick whispered.

## The Night Watch

The bedtime routine at Uncle Mark’s was an operation. The bathroom in the 1890s-era apartment was not designed for a wheelchair. It was a narrow, tiled chute with a clawfoot tub and a pedestal sink that seemed determined to block the door. Ellen had to leave the wheelchair in the hallway. She carried Nichole in—a "dead lift" that made Ellen grunt with effort—and balanced her on the edge of the toilet, then the edge of the tub.

It was hot. The bathroom window was painted shut, trapping the humidity. By the time Nichole was scrubbed, dried, and wrestled into her cotton pajamas, both she and her mother were sweating. "Okay," Ellen exhaled, brushing a damp strand of hair from her face. "Let's get you to the cool room."

She carried Nichole down the hall to the spare room. The transition was instant. The hallway was stifling, but the "Cockpit" had airflow. Patrick was already there, wearing his oversized t-shirt and boxers. He was sitting cross-legged on the low futon, staring at the SignalMaster radio, watching the signal needle bounce.

Ellen lowered Nichole onto the futon. "Slide," Patrick said, moving his legs to give them space. It worked exactly as Mark had promised. The firm mattress didn't sink. Nichole slid easily from her mother's arms to the center of the bed.

"You two okay?" Ellen asked, hovering by the door. "Do you need the door open for light?" Patrick looked at the glowing green face of the Quantix oscilloscope and the amber backlight of the radio. "We have lights," Patrick said. Ellen smiled, kissed them both on the forehead, and closed the door.

The Atmosphere The room was dark, but it wasn't silent. Outside, the summer storm had broken. Rain was hammering against the shingles of the house—a heavy, rhythmic drumming. In the window, the box fan was roaring on "High." It pulled the wet, salty air into the room. It was a wall of sound. *Whirrrrrr-Hisssssss.*

Usually, Nichole hated the dark. At home in Virginia, her room was quiet. In the quiet, the dark felt empty. If she dropped her pillow, it was gone forever. If a shadow moved, she couldn't run. The dark was where the spasms waited. It was where she felt the most paralyzed. She usually slept with a nightlight and the door cracked, straining to hear her parents’ TV down the hall just to know she wasn't alone.

But here, the noise wrapped around her like a blanket. And then there was the heat. It was sticky, making her pajamas cling to her skin, but the fan breeze felt good against her face.

She felt the mattress dip. Patrick lay down next to her. He didn't stay on "his side." He shimmied close until his shoulder was pressing against hers. He pulled the navy blue quilt up over them. "Loud rain," Patrick whispered.

Nichole turned her head. She could see his profile in the faint amber glow of the radio. He was right there. She didn't need to yell for Mom. She didn't need to bang on the wall. She closed her eyes. For the first time, she didn't try to fight the sleep.


Sometime deep in the night—maybe 2:00 AM—the enemy attacked. It started in her left calf. A sudden, electric tightening. The muscle seized, pulling her toes down and locking her ankle in a rigid, painful point. *The Cramp.*

Nichole gasped, her eyes snapping open. Pain shot up her leg. She tried to cry out, but the shock seized her vocal cords. She let out a sharp, high-pitched whimper. *Nnnngh.* In Virginia, this would be the start of a five-minute ordeal. She would have to scream until Mom woke up, ran down the hall, and rubbed it out.

But she wasn't in Virginia. Before the second wave of pain could hit, a hand was on her leg. Patrick.

He hadn't woken up groggy. He had woken up *active*. He threw the quilt back. He found her calf in the dark. He knew the knot. He knew exactly where the muscle bunched up under the knee. He dug his thumb in. *Push. Rub. Circle.*

"I got it," Patrick whispered. His voice wasn't scared. It was calm. "Breathe, Nikki. I got it."

He used his other hand to gently flex her foot upward, fighting the spasm, stretching the tendon. "One... two... three..." he counted softly, matching the rhythm of the rain outside.

Nichole bit her lip, tears leaking from the corners of her eyes. The pain was sharp, but Patrick’s hands were warm. He wasn't just rubbing the skin; he was anchoring her. *I am here.*

Slowly, the iron knot in her leg began to loosen. The muscle fluttered, then relaxed. Her foot dropped back to a natural position. The pain faded into a dull, throbbing ache.

Patrick didn't stop. He kept rubbing, lighter now. Smoothing the nerves down. "Gone?" he whispered.

Nichole exhaled, her breath shaking. *Gone.*

She reached out in the dark. She found his arm. She pulled him. It was a clumsy movement, her motor control still fried from the pain, but Patrick understood. He scooted closer. He let her wrap her arms around his chest. He wrapped his arms around her small, trembling shoulders. *The Hug.* It meant: *Thank you. You saved me.*

Patrick squeezed her back. He rested his chin on the top of her head. "You're welcome," he whispered into her hair. He paused. The name felt natural, like it had been waiting for this moment to exist. "Any time, Nikki."

Nichole froze for a second. *Nikki.* Mom called her Nichole. Dad called her Nichole. The teachers called her Nichole. *Nikki* was short. It was fast. It sounded like a secret code. She squeezed him tighter. *Yes.*

They fell asleep like that—tangled together on the hard futon, listening to the rain and the fan, safe in the cockpit of the Bennett Machine.

## The Pitch

Patrick woke up with the mission. The storm had passed, leaving the morning air thick and heavy. The box fan was still rattling in the window, pulling in the scent of wet asphalt and low tide.

He crawled out of the low futon, careful not to wake Nichole. She was sleeping deeply, her arm thrown over her eyes to block the morning light filtering through the blinds. He climbed onto Uncle Mark’s stool at the workbench. He found a pad of graph paper and a box of crayons.

He didn't know the schematic symbols yet. He didn't know what a resistor looked like on paper. But he knew the logic. He drew a blue square: *THE BATTERY.* He drew thick black lines: *THE WIRES.* He drew a red circle with yellow spikes radiating from it: *THE BEACON.*

The door creaked open. Uncle Mark walked in, holding two mugs of coffee. He was wearing a t-shirt that said NEWPORT STORM CHASERS. "Morning, Foreman," Mark whispered, handing a mug to an invisible person in the hallway before stepping inside. "You're up early."

Patrick didn't look up. He was shading the red circle. "I know how to fix it," Patrick said.

Mark set his mug down on a coaster (a piece of scrap cork). He leaned over Patrick’s shoulder. "Fix what?"

Patrick pointed to the SignalMaster radio. Specifically, he pointed to the small, red LED labeled TX STANDBY. Even though the radio was off, that little light pulsed slowly. *Blink... Blink...* "That," Patrick said. "I want to put that on Nichole’s chair."

Mark looked at the radio. Then he looked at Nichole, sleeping in the corner. "A status light?" Mark asked.

"A safety light," Patrick corrected. "Last night was dark. If we were outside, cars wouldn't see her. The wheelchair is black. She disappears." He tapped his drawing. "I want to build a blinker. High visibility. Mounted on the back cane."

Mark stared at the drawing. He rubbed his chin, looking from the nine-year-old boy to the complex radio equipment. He didn't smile like it was cute. He nodded like it was brilliant. Mark turned to the door. "Tom! El! You need to see this."

Tom and Ellen appeared in the doorway a moment later, looking groggy and clutching their coffees. "Is everything okay?" Ellen asked, instantly looking at Nichole to check for distress.

"Everything is better than okay," Mark said. "Patrick has a proposal."

Patrick spun the stool around. He held up the graph paper. "It's a proximity alert system," Patrick said, using words he had picked up from Mark’s magazines. "We mount a red strobe on the back of the chair. Powered by a 9-volt. If we go out at night, we turn it on. Cars see the flash before they see the wheels."

Tom stepped into the room. He took the paper. He looked at the blue square and the red sun. "A taillight," Tom realized. "Like on a bike. But brighter."

"Much brighter," Patrick said. "Like the radio tower."

Ellen looked at Nichole. She thought about the dark parking lots, the crosswalks at dusk, the constant anxiety of pushing a black wheelchair through a world that rarely looked down. "Patrick," Ellen said, her voice soft. "That is a wonderful idea."

"I need parts," Patrick said, looking at Mark. "We need to go to the mall."

"Circuit Barn," Mark agreed. "They open at nine."

Tom handed the paper back to his son. "Okay. While you boys do the engineering run, your mother and I need to figure out how to see Grandma Helen."

"She's at the apartment on Pond Avenue," Mark said. <aac>3c.</aac>

"Exactly," Tom sighed. "Third floor. No elevator. Just that narrow winding staircase. There is no way we are carrying Nichole up three flights of spiral stairs. It’s not safe for her or us."

"So we bring Grandma to us," Ellen suggested. "Is there a park nearby?"

"Equity Park," Mark said. "It's right across from your old stomping grounds on Broadway."

Tom smiled, a look of nostalgia crossing his face. "Dip & Sip," Tom said. "I spent two summers working the fryer there. Best glazed sticks in the state."

"And the coffee is still like rocket fuel," Mark added. "There's a Heritage Farms right next door if you need to grab milk or snacks for the kids."

"Perfect," Tom decided. "Mark, you take the team to Circuit Barn. Get the parts. Ellen and I will walk over to Pond Ave, pick up Mom, and walk her down to Broadway. We'll meet you at the Dip & Sip at... let's say 11:00?"

"11:00," Mark confirmed. "Operation Blinker is a go."

Patrick turned back to his drawing. He added a switch. Nichole stirred on the futon. She opened her eyes, blinking against the light. She saw Patrick at the desk. She saw the adults standing around him. She didn't know what was happening, but she saw Patrick smiling. She pulled the quilt up and smiled back. The Bennett Machine was already at work.
