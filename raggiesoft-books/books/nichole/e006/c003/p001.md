## The Thirteen-Hour Problem

The Fortress was quiet.

After the chaotic retreat from the mountains yesterday, the house on Delaney Street felt like a decompression chamber. The familiar hum of the central air, the smell of their own coffee, and the sight of the specially modified hallway gave them space to breathe.

But the kitchen table was a war zone.

Ellen had the Putney College prospectus spread out next to a large road atlas. The color-coded spreadsheet was there, too, with Roosevelt Rehabilitation Center crossed out in ink so thick it had torn the paper.

"Okay," Ellen said, rubbing her temples. "Virginia is officially a bust. The private business schools are predatory, the state rehab center is a prison, and the community colleges don't have dorms. That leaves the out-of-state list."

Tom stood at the counter, refilling his coffee mug. "Putney College. The open house is next Saturday."

Ellen traced a line on the map with her finger. "Vermont. I’ve been looking at the route. If we want to avoid the massive toll bridges and the metropolitan gridlock of D.C. and New York, we have to swing west. It’s going to be a thirteen-hour drive. Minimum."

Nichole was parked at the table, her tray elevated to clear the edge. She stared at the map. Thirteen hours in the van. The thought made her tailbone ache in sympathy.

"It's a specialized campus, though," Ellen said, trying to inject optimism into her voice as she tapped the glossy brochure. "It's built for accessibility. It says right here: 'Integrated Personal Care Management.'"

"But what does that mean?" Patrick asked. He was sitting next to Nichole, dissecting an orange. "Are they going to do the same thing Roosevelt did? Separate the genders and push the 'independent living' stuff where they force you to use a random aide?"

"It's possible," Ellen sighed. "It's the standard model for these specialty colleges. There's another one down in Florida—Sunstate College—and a big state school out in Ohio that has underground tunnels so students never have to push through the snow. But they all use that same attendant-care model. They think the family is a crutch."

"We're not a crutch," Patrick said, his voice hard. He placed a slice of orange on Nichole’s tray. "We're a machine."

Nichole looked at the orange slice. She didn't eat it. She looked at the brochure for Putney.

Ohio. Vermont. Florida. Thirteen hours in a van just to go to another segregated campus where strangers would touch her. Strangers who didn't know her signals. Strangers who didn't know that she needed her left leg elevated exactly 15 degrees to stop the spasms.

She only trusted Patrick. He was the only one who knew the code.

She reached for her VocaLink. The screen glowed blue in the morning light.

TIRED.

"I know, honey," Ellen said gently. "We'll get a good week of rest here before we pack up for the crusade to Vermont."

Nichole shook her head. That wasn't it.

NO. MORE. LABS.

Patrick stopped peeling the orange. He looked at the screen. "Labs?"

Nichole clicked her tongue—a sharp, frustrated sound. She tapped the keyboard again.

WANT. NORMAL.

"Normal," Patrick repeated. He looked out the sliding glass door at the backyard. The grass needed mowing. It was a normal suburban chore.

"We've been looking at this backwards," Patrick said, turning to his parents. "We keep looking for schools that cater to disabilities, thinking they'll understand us. But they don't. They treat her like a lab experiment. They have rigid protocols and liability lawyers who are terrified of lawsuits."

"So what do we do?" Tom asked, leaning against the counter. "Cancel Vermont?"

"No, we go to Vermont," Patrick said. "We give it a shot. Putney claims they are different. We test that hypothesis. But if it's just another version of what we saw yesterday... then we look at a normal school."

"What normal school?" Ellen asked. "Most campuses are a nightmare for access."

"A massive, regular old state school," Patrick said. "One that's so big, they don't have the time to micromanage our living arrangements. One that's close to home. Like Virginia Dominion."


It was the local giant. Located in Norfolk, a mere twenty-minute drive from their driveway. It was a sprawling, urban campus with Division I sports, rowdy fraternities, and a massive English department. It wasn't "special." It was the definition of average, chaotic college life.

<aac>Vdu?</aac> Ellen asked, her maternal anxiety spiking. "Patrick, their dorms are traditional. Shared bathrooms, tiny rooms, no elevators in half the freshman housing..."

"We wouldn't live in the dorms," Patrick realized, the idea locking into place. "We're eighteen. We could rent an apartment right off campus. Just the two of us."

Nichole’s head snapped up.

"Think about it," Patrick continued, speaking faster now. "We wouldn't have to ask a Dean's permission to share a room. We wouldn't have to deal with aides, or curfews, or single-gender hallways. We pay rent. We go to class. We come home. Just... normal students."

"And the infrastructure is changing," Tom added, nodding slowly. "They're building that Maglev system. It’s supposed to open this fall."

Patrick’s eyes lit up. "Exactly. It’s a magnetic levitation train. It runs on an elevated track right through the center of campus. It connects the Engineering Building, the Library, and the Dorms. No bumps. No traffic. You just roll onto the platform, ride the magnet, and roll off."

"It's perfect engineering," Patrick said, looking at Nichole. "It's smooth. It's fast. It solves the cross-campus transit problem without needing a specialized van service."

Nichole pictured it. Floating above the campus on a magnetic wave. Zipping from English Lit to the Student Union without fighting curbs or potholes.

An apartment. With Patrick. Riding a futuristic train to class. No nurses. No "levels." No "intake forms." Just going to a football game on Saturday. Just rolling into English 101 and handing in a paper without someone congratulating her for holding the pen.

She tapped her armrest rapidly. Yes. Yes. Yes.

NORMAL. GIRL.

Tom smiled, taking a sip of his coffee. He looked at the map of Vermont, then at the siblings sitting at the table.

"Okay," Tom said. "That is a hell of a Plan B. An apartment near VDU and a season pass for the Maglev."

He walked over and tapped the Putney brochure.

"But we still have the reservation in Brattleboro. Let's see how Vermont goes first. If Putney tries to separate you two, or if they try to assign a stranger to manage Nichole... we come home, and we start looking at lease agreements on Colley Avenue."

Nichole settled back in her chair. For the first time since the tour began, she didn't feel like a problem to be solved. She felt like a girl with options.

She looked at the Putney brochure.

Okay, Vermont, she thought. You have one chance. Beat "Normal."

## Home Field Advantage

The drive to Virginia Dominion University didn't require a map. It didn't require a navigation spreadsheet. It was written into the Bennetts' muscle memory.

They merged off I-264 at Exit 11B, the familiar concrete ramp depositing them onto Brambleton Avenue.

"This feels weird," Patrick commented from the back seat, adjusting his sunglasses. "We're going on a college tour, but we're ten minutes from home."

"That's the point," Tom said, cruising past the sparkling water of The Hague.

They crossed the bridge near the NOAA building, its white facade reflecting the harsh summer sun. Brambleton curved sharply to the right, wrapping around the massive brick complex of Tidewater General Hospital.

"If you get sick," Ellen noted, pointing at the ER entrance, "you are literally three miles from your doctor."

The road straightened out and became Hampton Boulevard. The vibe shifted instantly as they entered Ghent. The speed limit dropped to a polite 30 MPH. They drove down the two-lane road, separated by a double yellow line, passing the stately, older apartment buildings with their ivy-covered brick and wrought-iron balconies.

"I remember this," Nichole thought. This was the route to summer computer camp. It wasn't a scary drive into the unknown; it was just Tuesday.

They dipped under the railroad underpass—the unofficial gateway to the university district. The road widened, the lanes expanding as the city gave way to the campus.

From 25th Street to 41st Street, the landscape changed. Fast food joints, bookstores, and students on bicycles replaced the historic apartments.

"Here we go," Tom said.

He made a sharp right onto 43rd Street.

To their right loomed the 43rd Street Parking Garage, a massive concrete hive. To their left was the crown jewel of the campus expansion: The Vanguard Convocation Center. It was a gleaming modern arena, glass and steel flashing in the sun, hosting everything from basketball games to graduation ceremonies.

Tom pulled the van into the garage entrance. The clearance bar read 8' 2".

"Plenty of room," Tom noted. He swiped his credit card and parked on the ground level, right next to the elevator.


Patrick unloaded Nichole. The air in the parking garage was hot and smelled of car exhaust and river water—the signature scent of Norfolk in June.

"Okay," Patrick said, locking her footplates. "Let's go see if 'Normal' works."

They rolled out of the garage and crossed the street toward the Welcome Center, which was nestled into the side of the Vanguard Arena.

The sidewalk was wide. The curb cuts were flush—perfectly graded. Patrick didn't have to pop the front casters; the chair glided up the ramp without a bump.

"Smooth," Patrick murmured.

They weren't the only ones there. A small crowd had gathered near the glass doors of the Welcome Center. There were parents fanning themselves with brochures, and teenagers looking awkwardly at their shoes.

A young woman in a bright blue polo shirt with <aac>Vdu ambassador</aac> embroidered on the chest spotted them. She didn't look at the wheelchair with panic. She didn't look for an aide. She just smiled and waved her clipboard.

"Welcome to VDU!" she chirped, holding the door open for a family ahead of them. "Tour starts in five minutes! Come on in to the AC!"

Patrick looked at Nichole.

"No intake form," he whispered.

Nichole tapped her armrest.

GOOD. START.

They joined the group, rolling into the cool air of the lobby, just another set of prospective students checking out the local giant.

## The Roll Call

The lobby of the Welcome Center was an atrium of glass and school spirit. Banners hung from the ceiling in blue and silver. A massive bronze statue of the school mascot—a Lion wearing a crown—stood guard near the elevators.

The group of prospective students gathered in a loose semi-circle. There were about twelve of them—a mix of bored teenagers, anxious parents, and one very enthusiastic tour guide standing on a small riser.

"Alright everyone!" the guide clapped her hands. "I’m Megan, I’m a Junior majoring in Communications, and I’ll be your guide today. Welcome to Virginia Dominion University!"

She smiled, a high-wattage expression that seemed immune to the Norfolk humidity.

"We have a lot of ground to cover today," Megan continued. "VDU is a big place—over 18,000 students and growing. So, before we head out into the heat, I want to do a quick 'Roll Call of Majors.' Shout out what you’re interested in studying so I can make sure we point out the right buildings."

She started on the left with a lanky boy in a surfing t-shirt.

"Marine Biology," he mumbled.

"Awesome," Megan beamed. "We’re literally right on the water. You’ll want to see the Oceanography & Physics Building. We have our own research vessel docked at the pier."

She moved to the next girl, who was adjusting her glasses.

"Psychology," she said.

"Very popular," Megan nodded. "You’ll be spending a lot of time in the Mills Godwin Building—that's the life sciences hub right in the middle of campus. It's got great labs."

She went down the line. Business (Constant Hall). Sports Management (The Fieldhouse).

Then, she got to the Bennetts.

Megan looked at Patrick. She didn't look at the wheelchair. She didn't look at the parents hovering protectively behind them. She just looked at the prospective student.

"How about you?" Megan asked.

"Computer Engineering," Patrick said.

"Ooh, tough major," Megan grinned. "You’re going to be living in the E&OST Building—that's Engineering & Ocean Science Technology. It’s brand new. Lots of toys in there."

Then, Megan turned her gaze to Nichole.

She didn't skip her. She didn't ask the parents, "And what is she interested in?" She waited.

Nichole felt a flash of gratitude. At Roosevelt, they had talked about her. Here, they were waiting for her.

She typed quickly on her VocaLink. The synthesized voice cut through the lobby chatter, clear and robotic.

<aac>Creative. Writing.</aac>

Nobody gasped. Nobody looked horrified. This was a tech-heavy campus; half the engineering students walked around talking to their PDAs anyway.

"Creative Writing!" Megan said, not missing a beat. "That means you belong to The BAL—the Batten Arts & Letters building. It’s that huge nine-story tower you probably saw driving in. It houses the English Department and the MFA program. You’ll love the view from the eighth floor; it’s where all the writers hang out to brood."

The group chuckled.

"Okay!" Megan clapped again. "So we’re hitting E&OST, The BAL, and we'll swing by the new Maglev station since everyone asks about it. Follow me!"

She pushed open the double doors.

Patrick leaned down as they started rolling.

"Did you see that?" he whispered.

"See what?" Nichole typed.

"She didn't ask about your 'limitations,'" Patrick said, popping the casters over the threshold. "She just told you where the writers hang out."

Nichole watched the back of the blue polo shirt marching confidently ahead.

Writers hang out on the eighth floor.

Not "wheelchair users go to the first floor." Writers.

She tapped her armrest.

NORMAL.

"Let's roll," Patrick said.

## The Tower of Words

"Okay, hydrate!" Megan commanded, uncapping her water bottle. "We are crossing the desert."

The group moved out of the Welcome Center and headed north along Hampton Boulevard. The Virginia heat hit them like a physical blow—humid, heavy, and smelling of hot asphalt.

They rolled past the massive parking garage, heading toward 45th Street. Looming ahead was Batten Arts & Letters—or "BAL" as everyone called it. It was a nine-story brick monolith that looked like a fortress of humanities.

"This is the heart of the College of Arts and Letters," Megan shouted over the traffic noise. "English, History, Foreign Languages, Political Science. If you write papers, you live here."

Instead of going to the main front doors which had heavy foot traffic, Megan led them up a gentle concrete slope to the side entrance of the BAL Addition.

"We’re taking the shortcut to the AC!" she announced.

Patrick pushed Nichole up the grade. It was steep but manageable. He hit the handicap door button with his hip, and the doors swung open, blasting them with frigid, conditioned air.


They entered a wide, tiled hallway. To their left was a small coffee bar, currently shuttered for the summer, a metal grate pulled down over the espresso machine. Across the hall was a large tiered lecture hall, its doors propped open to reveal rows of empty seats waiting for the fall semester.

"This is the 'Fishbowl' coffee spot," Megan noted. "During the semester, the line wraps around the corner. Caffeine is the primary fuel source for English majors."

She led them further down the hallway. Then, the architecture got tricky.

The hallway ended at a wide set of stairs leading down to the main elevator lobby. It was a "split level" design common in 70s buildings—just six steps down, but six steps was a canyon for a wheelchair.

"Okay, folks, watch your step," Megan said, hopping down the stairs. She pointed to a machine on the right side of the staircase. "And for anyone with wheels, we have the VPL right here."

It was a Vertical Platform Lift—a small, open-air mechanical pod designed solely to bridge the six-step gap.

Patrick looked at it. It wasn't a freight elevator. It was a dedicated piece of access tech.

"Go ahead," Megan smiled. "We'll wait."

Patrick rolled Nichole onto the metal platform. He closed the safety gate. He pressed the "Down" arrow.

Whirrrrrrrrr.

The machine was slow, descending with a loud mechanical hum, but it was smooth. Patrick stood next to her on the platform as they sank below the floor level of the upper hallway and arrived at the main lobby level.

The gate unlocked. They rolled out.

"Functional," Patrick noted. "Beats a ramp."


"Alright," Megan gathered the group in the main lobby. "Now we head to the summit. Creative Writing is on the 8th floor. The elevators here serve floors 1 through 9."

She gestured to the elevator bank. There were three elevators, but the doors looked narrow.

"Warning," Megan laughed. "These elevators are... vintage. They are cozy. We're going to have to split up."

She pressed the 'Up' button. Two of the three elevators dinged at once.

"Okay, first half with me!" Megan ushered the Marine Biology kid and the Psychology girl into the first car.

Patrick looked at the second car. It was small.

"Squeeze in," Patrick told his parents.

He rolled Nichole into the back corner. Tom and Ellen squeezed in beside them. Another family—a mom and her son interested in History—crowded in near the doors.

"Everyone breathe in," Tom joked as the doors rattled shut.

The elevator lurched upward. It was hot, smelling of old motor oil and decades of students. It hummed and vibrated as it passed the floors. 2... 3... 4...

"This is tight," Patrick whispered to Nichole. "But it works."


Ding.

The doors slid open on the 8th Floor.

The vibe changed instantly. The lobby here was quieter. The walls were lined with bulletin boards layered thick with flyers: Poetry Slam Tonight, Literary Journal Submissions, Playwright Workshop.

Megan was waiting for them as they spilled out of the elevator.

"Welcome to the Writer’s Colony," Megan whispered dramatically.

She walked them down the hall, pointing to a suite of offices with frosted glass doors.

"This is the English Department," she said. "If you are Creative Writing, this is your home base. VDU has a massive MFA program, which means our undergrads get to take workshops with published authors. We have poets, novelists, and screenwriters on faculty."

She pointed to a seminar room with a large window overlooking the campus.

"That's the Workshop Room. In Creative Writing classes, you don't just listen to lectures. You sit in a circle, you read your work aloud, and you critique each other. It’s intense, but it makes you better."

Nichole looked at the room. She pictured herself at the table. Not in the back. At the table. Reading her sci-fi story.

Megan looked right at Nichole.

"The literary journal, The Mace & Crown, has its office down the hall. They take student submissions starting freshman year. Just saying."

Nichole felt a shiver. Submission. Publication.

She looked at Patrick. The VPL was slow. The elevators were tight. But they got her here. They got her to the room where the writers lived.

She tapped her armrest.

MY. FLOOR.

"Yeah," Patrick grinned, looking at the flyer for a Sci-Fi Lit class. "I think we found your lab."
