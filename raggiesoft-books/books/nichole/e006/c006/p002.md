## The Juggernaut

The fog of the previous day had burned off, leaving behind a brilliant, sunny Saturday.

Nichole sat in the purple Titan AeroMotion outside the Raritan State University Welcome Center, soaking in the July heat. She hadn't seen a single firework on the Fourth of July, and she didn't care. The sky in New Jersey was clear, the ground was flat, and Vermont was officially three hundred miles in the rearview mirror.

"Alright, tour group seven," a tour guide named Brian called out. He was a lanky junior wearing a red RSU polo. "Let's get moving."

This wasn't an open house. It was just a standard, rolling campus tour.

Brian looked at the Bennett family. He saw the heavy wheelchair. He saw Patrick standing behind it with his hands on the grips. He nodded respectfully at Patrick, then looked down to make direct eye contact with Nichole.

"We'll be taking the accessible ramps into the library," Brian said to her directly. "The campus buses are all equipped with hydraulic lifts, so we'll be hopping on one in about twenty minutes. Sound good?"

Nichole smiled. She tapped her VocaLink screen. <aac>Sounds. Good.</aac>

Brian didn't offer to push. He didn't ask about her diagnosis. He just treated them like any other family in the herd. It was a refreshing dose of Jersey apathy.

The tour was an exercise in scale. Raritan State wasn't a campus; it was a city. Forty thousand students spread across multiple campuses connected by a fleet of massive red-and-white buses. Everything was built in concrete and steel.

For accessibility, it was an industrial dream. The sidewalks were twelve feet wide. Every building had heavy-duty automatic doors. The library had a bank of six elevators.

"It's huge," Patrick noted, pushing Nichole easily along the flat quad. "It's like a small country."

Nichole made a soft, agreeable *hummm*. She liked the energy. It felt alive.


The tour arrived at the housing stop. They stood in the lobby of a massive, twelve-story brick tower.

"RSU guarantees housing for freshmen," Brian explained to the group. "These are traditional residence halls. Co-ed by building, but strictly separated by gender per room."

Patrick and Tom exchanged a look. Aunt Carol’s research from the night before was about to be put to the test.

While the rest of the group looked at the communal lounge, Tom approached Brian.

"Quick question about disability housing," Tom said quietly, gesturing to his children. "My son and daughter are a package deal. He's her primary care aide. For medical reasons, they need to share a room. Ideally, they need to share a bed for her nighttime spasticity management."

Brian blinked. He wasn't malicious like the administrators at Putney. He was just a twenty-year-old kid looking at a bureaucratic wall.

"Oh, wow. Uh, well," Brian stammered politely. "I know our Disability Office is great with academic stuff... but state housing code is pretty ironclad. Co-habitation for siblings of the opposite gender isn't permitted in standard dorms. And for liability reasons, all the accessible dorms use single-occupancy medical beds. We don't have double beds in the dorms at all."

Nichole heard him. Patrick’s hands tightened on her push handles, bracing for the inevitable *Turtle Mode*.

But Nichole didn't shut down.

Aunt Carol had warned them last night. *It's a state school. They run on a grid system. They don't make exceptions for anybody.* Nichole looked at the cinderblock walls of the mega-dorm. She wasn't angry this time. The betrayal at Putney had been personal—they had promised a sanctuary and delivered a prison. Raritan State was just a juggernaut. It couldn't see her as an individual because it was too big.

It was disappointing, but it wasn't a shock.

Nichole reached up and patted Patrick’s hand on the grip. She made a soft, clicking sound with her tongue. *It's okay.*

Patrick looked down at her, surprised by her calm. He let out a breath he didn't realize he'd been holding.

"No problem, man," Patrick said to the guide, patting his shoulder. "Thanks for the info."

The tour ended amicably twenty minutes later at the campus center. Nichole waved goodbye to Brian, who waved back.


They met Aunt Carol back at the parking garage.

"So?" Carol asked, leaning against the hood of her sedan.

"No go on the dorms," Ellen sighed. "Just like you thought. State policy."

"Well," Carol said, clapping her hands together, ever the pragmatist. "You saw it. Now you know. And now, you need real New Jersey food before you start the drive back to Virginia. Follow me to the diner."

The "Machine" loaded up. Patrick secured Nichole in the van, and Tom started the engine.

They pulled out of the university and onto US Highway 1.

US-1 was a massive, divided highway. On the left side of the highway sat the classic chrome-and-neon diner Aunt Carol was aiming for.

"Alright," Tom said, gripping the wheel. "Watch the master."

Instead of moving into the left lane to make the turn, Carol’s sedan stayed in the far right lane. Tom followed her.

"Why are we in the right lane?" Patrick asked, looking at the diner on their left.

"The New Jersey Jughandle," Tom grinned. "No left turns allowed on the highway. You have to exit right to go left."

Carol’s blinker flashed. She peeled off onto a small exit ramp on the right that looped around in a circle—shaped exactly like the handle of a jug—which brought them to a traffic light facing perpendicular to the highway.

When the light turned green, Carol and Tom drove straight across all the lanes of US-1 and directly into the diner parking lot.

"Genius," Patrick laughed, impressed by the brutal efficiency of the traffic design.

"If only college administrations worked like New Jersey traffic engineers," Tom muttered, putting the van in park.

They unloaded the chair. Nichole rolled toward the diner, the smell of griddle smoke and coffee in the air. She was tired, and she was disappointed that their backup plan had fallen through.

But as Patrick pushed her up the ramp into the diner, his heavy footsteps matching the roll of her wheels, she knew they still had the most important thing. They still had the machine. And they would find a place for it, even if they had to build it themselves.

## The Diner Debrief

The chrome diner was packed, smelling of coffee, Taylor pork roll, and griddle grease. The Bennetts and Aunt Carol were tucked into a large, accessible corner booth.

Nichole was finishing a plate of silver-dollar pancakes. She was in good spirits. The tour had gone well. The guide was polite. The campus was massive, flat, and paved in glorious, smooth concrete.

"I loved the buses," Patrick said, mapping out the routes on a diner napkin with a red pen. "They run every ten minutes. Hydraulic lifts on the back doors. They connect the whole university. It's a completely closed loop."

"Except for the dorms," Ellen reminded him gently. "Brian said it himself. Single-occupancy medical beds only. No co-habitation."

Patrick dropped his pen. He looked at the napkin. The perfect transit system, ruined by state housing codes.

Carol put her coffee mug down. Her eyes lit up with a sudden, fierce energy.

"Screw the dorms," Carol said.

Tom raised an eyebrow. "Excuse me?"

"You don't need the dorms," Carol said, leaning over the table. "Look, Raritan State waives the housing requirement for local commuters. Right?"

"Sure," Tom nodded. "If you live within a certain radius. But we live three hundred miles away."

"You do," Carol said. "I don't. Change their address to my house. They become New Jersey residents. Boom. Commuter status. They bypass the housing department entirely."

Patrick looked up, his engineering brain immediately running the numbers. "Wait. We live with you in Highland Park? The Number 810 bus goes right down Raritan Avenue. It crosses the bridge and drops off at the train station. We transfer from the transit bus to the campus bus right there."

Nichole made an excited, high-pitched vocalization. She tapped her screen. <aac>Aunt. Carol. House.</aac>

"Exactly!" Carol beamed at Nichole.

"Hold on, hold on," Tom said, holding his hands up to stop the runaway train. "Carol, I love you. But your house has four concrete steps up to the porch, and a bathroom the size of a phone booth. It's a historic home. We can't make it accessible without gutting the whole first floor."

"So I sell it," Carol shrugged, as if selling a house she'd owned for twenty years was as simple as ordering a second cup of coffee. "I've been thinking about downsizing anyway. I sell the Highland Park house, I buy a ground-floor condo in New Brunswick near the station, or somewhere on a better bus line. We get a place with a roll-in shower. Problem solved."

The table went quiet. The magnitude of the offer hung in the air, heavy and warm.

Nichole looked at her aunt. Her eyes filled with tears—happy ones this time. She reached across the table, pressing her hand against Carol's.

"Carol..." Ellen said, her voice choked with emotion. "You can't sell your home for us."

"Why not?" Carol asked stubbornly. "Family is family."

"Because of the logistics," Patrick said softly.

Everyone looked at Patrick. He was staring at the napkin.

"I looked at the schedule on the wall at the bus shelter during the tour," Patrick said. "The 810 bus only runs once an hour. On Sundays, it stops at 5:00 PM. If the lift is broken, or the bus is full, we miss class. The campus buses are great, but the public transit to get to them is a bottleneck."

Patrick looked at his aunt, his eyes full of deep gratitude.

"It's an amazing offer, Aunt Carol," Patrick said. "Seriously. Thank you. But we can't let you uproot your whole life for a transit system that only runs once an hour."

Nichole sighed. The logic was inescapable. She knew Patrick was right. The New Jersey plan was a beautiful dream, but it was just too heavy a lift.

Carol looked disappointed, but she nodded slowly. "The 810 is notoriously awful," she admitted. "And the lifts are always broken."

Tom reached across the table and squeezed Carol’s hand. "Thank you. For everything."

"So," Carol said, clearing her throat to push past the emotion of the moment. "What's the plan, then? If Putney is out, and Raritan State is out. What's left?"

Patrick looked at Nichole. He put his arm around the back of her wheelchair.

"We go home," Patrick said. "We go back to Norfolk and Virginia Beach. We have options right in our backyard."

He flipped the napkin over, using his red pen to draw a crude map of the Hampton Roads area.

"Look," Patrick said, pointing with the pen tip. "We have the Community College of Hampton Roads. We have Virginia Dominion University in Norfolk. If we want to look across the water on the Peninsula, there's Peninsula State University in Newport News, or even the Royal College of Virginia up in Williamsburg."

"Williamsburg?" Ellen asked. "That's an hour's drive away on a good traffic day."

"I looked at the transit maps before we left," Patrick said, his eyes lighting up with that same transit-nerd energy he’d had on the Raritan bus. "Hampton Roads has an interconnected bus grid. Even from Williamsburg. You can take the local bus to Newport News, transfer to the express bus over the bridge-tunnel to Norfolk, and catch the local bus straight back to our neighborhood in Virginia Beach."

"That would take five hours," Tom pointed out.

"But the *track* exists," Patrick emphasized. "If there's an emergency, or a long weekend, or if Nikki is just feeling homesick and you guys can't pick us up... we just roll onto the bus. It's a closed loop all the way to our front door."

Nichole looked at the red ink on the napkin. She could picture it. A long bus ride didn't scare her. A long bus ride meant she could listen to her music and watch the world go by, knowing that at the end of the line was her own bed. It was the ultimate safety net.

"I'll get an apartment near whichever campus we pick," Patrick continued. "A ground floor unit. I'll sign the lease. I'll be the landlord. I'll be the RA. I'll be the cafeteria chef. We don't need their permission."

Nichole looked up at him. She smiled. A real, genuine smile.

"Just us?" Tom asked.

"Just us," Patrick confirmed. "No dorm rules. No housing administrators. We build the machine ourselves."

Nichole tapped her tray. <aac>Home.</aac>

"Home it is," Tom said, raising his coffee mug. "To Virginia."

They clinked their mugs and glasses together over the plate of half-eaten pancakes. The Misfit Tour was officially over. They hadn't found the perfect college, but they had found the answer. They didn't need a sanctuary built by someone else. They were going to build their own.

## The Ocean State Option

The table was quiet for a moment, the Virginia plan solidifying in the diner air. Patrick was already folding up his napkin map, and Tom was reaching for the check. The tour was over.

"Wait," Carol said, putting her hand over Tom’s wallet. "Don't pay yet."

Tom paused. "Carol, we love the enthusiasm, but we've made the call. No more touring."

"I know, I know. No dorms, no administrators," Carol said rapidly, her eyes darting between Ellen and Patrick. "But we forgot about our brother."

Ellen blinked. "Mark? In Newport? Carol, he lives in a converted attic on the third floor of a Victorian. It has two flights of stairs. It is the literal opposite of accessible."

"I know that," Carol said, waving her hand dismissively. "But I talked to him last week. His lease is up at the end of August. He hates those stairs. He’s been looking to move to a ground-floor apartment anyway, but he said the rent in Newport is too high for a single guy. He told me he’d love to find roommates to split a bigger place. A three-bedroom, zero-step ranch."

Patrick stopped folding the napkin. He looked up. "Aunt Carol, you're suggesting we move in with Uncle Mark?"

"Why not?" Carol beamed. "He loves you guys. And he lives just across the bay from Braxton State University. The big one. BSU."

"I've never heard of it," Tom admitted.

"It's public. It's huge. And it sits right on the water in Braxton, directly west of Newport," Carol explained. "Mark said the whole campus is a 'Universal Design' project. Flat as a pancake. Automatic doors everywhere. No cars allowed on the main quad."

Ellen looked at Tom. "We haven't seen Mark since Christmas of ‘99. And the kids were born in Newport. We moved away when they were barely two years old."

Nichole felt a strange spark of electricity run up her spine. She had no memories of Rhode Island, but the idea of it carried a mythic weight. It was where her story started.

"Okay, but what's the transit situation?" Patrick asked, his inner engineer taking over. He wasn't swayed by nostalgia; he needed logistics. "If we get a new place with Uncle Mark in Newport, how do we get west across the bay to Braxton? There's no way I'm driving across the Newport Bridge in the winter."

Carol grinned. "You don't need a van. You take a boat."

"A boat," Patrick deadpanned.

"The Newport Flyer," Carol said triumphantly. "It's an easy ride on the OSTA bus to Gateway Center. You roll one block over to Perrotti Park, and you get on the Braxton Transit Authority harbor ferry. It goes straight west across Narragansett Bay and drops you off directly at the Narraganset Esplanade Station, right in the middle of the BSU campus. The gangways are hydraulic. It's perfectly level."

Patrick stared at his aunt. A local bus to a ferry to a campus. A completely public, accessible infrastructure loop.

Nichole’s eyes were wide. A ferry. She pictured the ocean. She pictured the wind. She pictured a campus where the doors actually opened.

She slammed her hand on the table, making the silverware jump.

Everyone looked at her. Nichole didn't hesitate. She jammed her finger onto the VocaLink.

<aac>Go.</aac>

"Nikki, hold on," Tom said gently. "We're exhausted. We were just talking about home."

Nichole shook her head fiercely. She looked at Patrick. She made a high-pitched, insistent *keening* sound and pointed north. She wanted to see where she was born. She wanted to ride the boat.

<aac>Go. Rhode. Island.</aac>

Patrick looked at his sister's face. The exhaustion of Putney and Raritan was completely gone, replaced by a fierce, blazing curiosity.

"Okay," Patrick said, holding up a hand to calm the table. "I'm in. But we need ground rules. This is not another Open House. If we tour Braxton State, we do it our way."

"Which means what?" Ellen asked.

"No housing office," Patrick said firmly. "We don't even look at the dorms. We don't ask for permission. We tour the campus as commuters. If we like it, we find an apartment with Uncle Mark, establish residency, and bypass the whole system. We operate as free agents."

"Commuter status only," Tom agreed, nodding. "I like it. Zero bureaucracy."

"I'll call him," Ellen said, already pulling the heavy cellular phone out of her purse.

She dialed the number. The table waited in tense silence, listening to the ambient noise of the diner.

"Mark? Hey, it's Ellen," she said. A pause. "No, everything's fine! Actually, we're in New Jersey with Carol... Yes, all of us. The twins too."

Ellen smiled, holding the phone slightly away from her ear so the table could hear Mark’s booming, excited voice on the other end.

*"El! Put them on speaker! Where are you guys?"* "Listen, Mark," Ellen continued. "We had a change of plans. How would you feel about some houseguests tonight? The kids want to tour Braxton State."

*"Are you kidding me?!"* Uncle Mark’s voice was loud enough to be heard over the diner clatter. *"Get up here! We'll put the twins up in a ground-floor hotel for the night, and tomorrow we can start looking at rental listings. Tell Patrick and Nichole I'm ordering the good pizza!"*

Ellen hung up, smiling at the table. "Well. The Ocean State is waiting."

Nichole let out a loud, joyous laugh—a bubbling, throaty sound that made a few patrons in the nearby booths turn and smile. She grabbed Patrick’s hand and squeezed it hard.

"Alright," Patrick grinned, putting his napkin map in his pocket. "Next stop, Narragansett Bay. Let's go see the water."
