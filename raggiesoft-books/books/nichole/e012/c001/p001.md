## The Last Funeral

The house was quiet. The kind of quiet that feels heavy, like a dust sheet draped over furniture.

Patrick wheeled Nichole into the living room. They were both wearing black. They had just come from the cemetery in Middletown. Uncle Mark was the last one. Tom had gone first in 2032 (heart attack). Ellen followed him in 2035 (cancer). Aunt Carol in 2038. And now Mark—the architect of the Fortress, the financier of the dream—was gone at 94.

Patrick stood in the center of the living room. He looked at the recliner where Mark used to sit, reading his spy novels. He looked at the spot by the window where Grandma Helen used to park her wheelchair to watch the snow. The house was empty. It smelled of old paper and the sea.

"It's just us, Nikki," Patrick said, his voice echoing slightly in the big room. "The adults are gone."

Nichole looked at him. His hair was gray now, thinning at the temples. He moved a little slower. The "spring" in his step that had powered them through the BSU tunnels was gone, replaced by a stiff, careful gait. She looked at her own hands resting on her lap tray. The skin was thin, paper-like. Her spasticity was still there, but it was less violent now—replaced by a stiffness that made cold days ache deep in her bones.

She tapped her tablet (a modern MagnaPad Pro). WE. ARE. THE. ADULTS. NOW.

Patrick let out a dry laugh. "Yeah. Scary thought."

He walked over to the hallway closet—the one Mark had converted back in 2003. He pressed the call button. *Whirrrr-Clunk.* The automated door swung open. The residential elevator light flickered on. It still worked. Mark had kept the maintenance contract paid up until the very end.

"He built this for us," Patrick whispered, touching the doorframe. "He knew we'd end up here."

## The City Problem

The winter of '43 was brutal. For thirty years, The Foundry had been their stronghold. They loved the industrial windows, the polished concrete, and the direct subway access. But the city was changing.

Braxton wasn't the gritty, manageable city of 2004 anymore. It was a "Smart City." The subway stations were crowded with autonomous delivery drones. The pace was frantic. And the infrastructure was aging.

The Incident: It happened on a Tuesday. Patrick and Nichole were coming back from a doctor's appointment. They took the Blue Line to Foundry Square. They rolled to the elevator to go up to the street level. OUT OF SERVICE.

Patrick stared at the red sign. "Okay," he muttered. "Backup plan. We use the freight elevator in the adjacent building." They rolled down the connector tunnel. OUT OF SERVICE.

Panic, cold and sharp, spiked in Patrick’s chest. Thirty years ago, he would have been angry. He would have found a security guard, demanded a key override, or even carried her up the escalator if he had to. But he was 57. His back hurt. His knees clicked. He couldn't carry her up three flights of stairs. Not anymore.

They were trapped underground.

They had to ride the train two stops back to *State Center*, transfer to a bus, ride the bus back to Foundry Square, and then navigate the icy sidewalks to their building's front door. The "20-minute trip" took three hours.

By the time they got inside Unit 404, Patrick was trembling with exhaustion. He collapsed on the sofa, rubbing his left knee—the "bad" one. "The grid is failing us, Nikki," he wheezed. "Or maybe... maybe we're failing the grid."

Nichole looked at him. She saw the gray pallor of his skin. She realized that the "Mole Lifestyle"—the endless walking, the subway transfers, the urban hustle—was a young person's game. The city demanded energy they no longer had.

She tapped her screen. WE. NEED. A. RANCH.

## The Decision

The papers were spread out on the island. The Deed: 95 West Main Road (Inherited, Mortgage Free). The Lease: The Foundry Lofts (Renewing in June).

"The rent is going up again," Patrick noted, looking at the notice. "\$4,500 a month." He looked at the deed to the Portsmouth house. "Taxes on the Fortress are \$6,000 a *year*."

Financially, it was a no-brainer. But emotionally, it was heavy. Moving back to Portsmouth meant admitting that the adventure was over. It meant leaving the center of the world and retreating to the edge.

"If we move back," Patrick said, staring at his coffee, "we're isolated. The bus runs every 30 minutes, sure. But we won't be going to art galleries on Tuesday nights. We won't be 'urban professionals' anymore."

Nichole looked out the window at the Braxton skyline. She loved this view. She loved the energy. But then she thought about the tub. The hydrotherapy tub in the Bennett Wing in Portsmouth. Deep. Hot. Jets that massaged her aching hips. The shower in the loft was great, but standing up to wash her was getting harder for Patrick. In the Portsmouth tub, he could sit on the bench next to her.

She thought about the elevator. In Portsmouth, if the elevator broke, they were just stuck on the first floor—where the kitchen, bedroom, and living room were. In the Loft, if the elevator broke, they were trapped in the sky or buried underground.

She reached for her mouse. WE. ARE. TIRED. PAT. THE. FORTRESS. IS. EASY. I. WANT. EASY.

Patrick read the words. *I want easy.* He let out a long breath. He rubbed his eyes. "Me too, Nikki. Me too."

## The Return

They didn't hire movers to pack. They hired movers to *purge*. They sold the loft furniture. They donated the office equipment. Bennett Dynamics was officially closed for new business. They were keeping the patent royalties from the *Haptic Interface*, but the consulting firm was retiring.

They drove the new van (a 2042 hybrid transport) across the Newport Bridge. They pulled into the driveway of 95 West Main. The house looked exactly the same. The brick was timeless. The ramp Uncle Mark had poured in 2003 was still smooth and solid, free of cracks.

Patrick lowered the lift. Nichole rolled out. The air smelled of salt and pine. It was quieter here. The roar of the city was gone, replaced by the sound of wind in the trees and the distant hum of the Route 60 bus.

They rolled up the ramp. Patrick unlocked the front door. They entered the hallway. It was empty, but it wasn't lonely. It felt like the house had been holding its breath, waiting for them to come back.

Patrick pushed her down the wide hallway to the Bennett Wing. He opened the double doors. The mini-apartment was exactly as they had left it after the funeral. The kitchenette. The study desks. And through the bathroom door, the gleaming white hydrotherapy tub.

"Home," Patrick whispered.

He parked her chair. He sat down on the edge of the bed—the same King bed they had bought with their "Bridge Fund" money forty years ago. He looked at Nichole. "We're the old people now, Nikki. We're Grandma Helen."

Nichole smiled. She tapped her screen. SHE. WAS. HAPPY. HERE. WE. WILL. BE. TOO.

Patrick lay back on the bed, staring at the ceiling. He didn't have to worry about the subway schedule. He didn't have to worry about the elevator breaking. He just had to be here.

"Okay," Patrick said, closing his eyes. "Fortress Mode engaged."
