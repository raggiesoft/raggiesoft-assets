## The Circuit

The classroom smelled of ozone and hot flux. To Harper Avery, it smelled like potential.

She sat at a workbench in the Electronics Assembly evaluation sector. In front of her was a schematic, a soldering iron, and a bag of resistors.

"Okay," the evaluator, Mr. Henderson, announced. "Your task is to assemble the circuit board according to the diagram. You have two hours. Accuracy is more important than speed."

Harper pulled her goggles down. She looked at the diagram.

It was a logic gate. A simple loop. Input A + Input B = Output C.

She picked up the soldering iron. Her hand, which usually had a slight, nervous tremor when she was dealing with people, was steady as a rock.

Heat the pad. Feed the solder. Lift. Cool.

For three hours, the world made sense. There was no yelling. There was no sarcasm. There was just the clean, binary reality of the circuit. If you did it right, the green LED lit up. If you did it wrong, it didn't.

Harper finished in forty-five minutes. She hooked up the 9-volt battery.

Click. The green light glowed steady and true.

"Done," Harper said, raising her hand.

Mr. Henderson walked over. He inspected the joints with a magnifying glass.

"Clean," he nodded, looking impressed. "Very clean. You have a knack for the flow, Harper."

Harper smiled. She felt like Freddie. She felt like she was part of the Machine.

This, she thought, is the Signal.

## The Shift Change

The Signal died the moment she walked out of the lab. The Static took over.

The Dining Hall was the epicenter of the chaos. It was a large, echoey room divided by an invisible timeline.

17:00 - 17:45: PERT Students (High Schoolers). 17:45 - 18:30: Adult Vocational Clients.

Harper sat at a round table near the exit, her tray guarding her space like a shield wall. She was eating mashed potatoes, trying to make herself as small as possible.

Around her, the high schoolers were vibrating with drama.

"I told you," a girl with aggressive eyeliner shrieked at a boy two tables over. "If you sit with her, we are done."

"It's just a seat, Ashley!" the boy shouted back, slamming his tray down.

Harper winced. Volume spike, she noted. Emotional regulation: Failed.

To her left, a group of girls were whispering furiously about a rumor involving a stolen MP3 player. To her right, a guy was bragging loudly about how he was going to sneak out past curfew to meet a girl from the Nursing Assistant program.

It’s inefficient, Harper thought, stabbing her green beans. It’s just noise.

She checked her watch. 17:40.

The doors opened. The adult clients began to line up in the foyer, waiting for the PERT students to clear out.

Harper watched them through the glass partition. It wasn't any better. She saw a man and a woman arguing intensely near the vending machines, their body language sharp and jagged.

Adults are just tall teenagers, Harper realized with a sinking feeling. The drama doesn't stop. It just gets louder.

She finished her food quickly. She didn't want to linger. She wanted to get back to the one place that was supposed to be safe.

***

## The PERT Lounge

The PERT Lounge was designed to be a recreational space—a place with couches, a TV, and board games where the high schoolers could relax after a day of assessments.

To Harper, it was a pressure cooker waiting to explode.

She rolled her purple chair into the corner, parking behind a bookshelf filled with battered paperbacks. She pulled out her sketchbook, trying to look busy, trying to be invisible. She was drawing a schematic for a soundboard, focusing on the clean, straight lines of the faders.

The room was packed. It was "Free Time," which meant "Chaos Time."

The door banged open.

A group of boys walked in. They were wearing black t-shirts with skulls on them. The leader was a big kid named Travis. He was a Senior, broad-shouldered and loud, with a sparse mustache that he seemed very proud of.

He scanned the room. He ignored the group of girls watching TV. His eyes landed on the corner.

On Harper.

He broke away from his pack and sauntered over. He didn't respect the perimeter. He walked right past the bookshelf, stepping into the small triangle of space Harper had claimed for herself.

"Hey, Wheels," Travis grinned, leaning his arm against the bookshelf, effectively boxing her in.

Harper gripped her pencil. "I'm working," she whispered, not looking up.

"Working?" Travis laughed. He reached out and tapped the page of her sketchbook with a thick finger. "That looks like homework. We're supposed to be chilling."

"I like homework," Harper said, her voice tight. "Please move. You're blocking the light."

Travis didn't move. He leaned in closer. He smelled of stale cafeteria chili and too much body spray.

"You're feisty," Travis said, his voice dropping to a low, suggestive register that made Harper’s skin crawl. "I like that. You know, you're pretty cute for a cripple."

Harper flinched. The slur hit her like a slap.

She looked up at him. She saw the arrogance in his eyes. He was seventeen, maybe eighteen. He was a man-sized boy who thought the world was his playground.

"Don't call me that," Harper said, her hand shaking.

"Just teasing," Travis smirked. He reached out and grabbed the handle of her wheelchair. He gave it a little jiggle, rocking her back and forth. "So, what's your deal? You got a boyfriend back home? Or are you waiting for a real man?"

Harper felt a cold spike of terror in her chest. He was touching her chair. To a wheelchair user, the chair was part of their body. He was touching her.

She ran the math in her head.

Variable: Age. He looked old. He had to be a Senior. He was probably turning eighteen any day now.

If he turns eighteen, Harper thought, panic rising, and I am sixteen... that changes the legal code. If he touches me then... is it a felony? Or just creepy?

She didn't know the specific statutes of the Virginia Code, but she knew the feeling in her gut. He was a predator. And he was getting too close.

"Let go of my chair," Harper said, her voice rising in pitch.

"Calm down," Travis laughed, leaning down so his face was inches from hers. "I'm just being nice. Why don't we go for a roll? There's a vending machine down the hall. My treat."

"No," Harper said.

"Come on," Travis insisted, his grip tightening on her handle. "Don't be a stuck-up—"

<aac>She said no!</aac>

The voice boomed across the lounge.

Mr. Henderson, the vocational evaluator who was on chaperone duty, crossed the room in three long strides. He stepped between Travis and Harper, breaking the line of sight.

"Step away, Travis," Henderson ordered, his voice hard. "Now."

Travis let go of the chair. He held up his hands, feigning innocence. "Whoa, chill. We were just talking."

"She didn't look like she was talking," Henderson said. "She looked like she was cornered. Back off. Go find your friends."

Travis glared at Henderson, then shot a look of pure venom at Harper over the teacher's shoulder. It was a look that said: You got me in trouble.

Harper shrank back into her chair, pulling her knees to her chest. She was shaking violently.

He's huge, she thought. And he's angry. And he's going to be an adult soon. He could hurt me.

This was why she couldn't stay. It wasn't just the noise. It was the vulnerability. At home, Freddie and Jessica and Paige were the wall. Here, she was just a girl in a chair in a corner.

Travis turned away, his ego bruised, his face red. He needed to reassert dominance. He needed to prove he still owned the room.

He looked at the center of the lounge.

The group of girls—the "Pop Squad"—were huddled around the TV, watching Star Search Academy. They were laughing and singing along.

Travis’s eyes narrowed.

"It's Tuesday," Travis announced loudly, marching toward them.

"So?" one of the girls asked, not looking away from the screen.

"Tuesday is Ignition," Travis grunted.

He reached out and snatched the remote control from the table.

Click.

The screen flickered. The singing competition vanished. Instead, heavy metal music blasted from the speakers. Pyrotechnics exploded on the screen. The logo flashed in chrome letters:

**AWE: TUESDAY NIGHT IGNITION**

"Hey!" the girl screamed, jumping up. "We were watching that!"

"Wrestling is on," Travis said, sitting on the sofa and putting his feet up. "Deal with it."

"Give it back!" The girl lunged for the remote.

Travis held it over his head. "Get away from me."

"You can't just take the TV!" another boy from the Pop Squad shouted, stepping up. "Change it back!"

"Make me," Travis sneered.

The shouting escalated. It wasn't just about the TV anymore. It was about dominance. It was about territory.

Harper shrank into her corner. Her heart was hammering against her ribs.

Violence imminent, her brain screamed. Evacuate.

But she was blocked in. The argument was happening between her and the door.

"Change. It. Back!"

Travis stood up. He grabbed a metal folding chair from the card table.

"I said," Travis roared, "IT'S IGNITION!"

He threw the chair.

It didn't hit anyone, but it smashed against the drywall with a deafening CRASH, leaving a dent in the plaster.

The room erupted. Screams. Shouting. People scattering.

Harper pulled her knees to her chest. She pulled her hood up tight, covering her face. She jammed her fingers into her ears, trying to block out the sound of the shouting and the AWE commentators screaming on the TV.

Static. It's all static.

Staff members rushed in, blowing whistles. "Everyone to your rooms! Now! Move!"

Harper waited until the herd dispersed. She rolled out of her corner, shaking, and fled down the hallway to Suite 200.

## The Carrier Wave

Harper slammed the door to her room. Her roommate, Kayla, wasn't there—she was probably still in the hall gossiping about the fight.

Harper was alone. But she felt unsafe. The vibration of the chair hitting the wall was still in her bones.

She pulled out her phone. Her hands were trembling so hard she almost dropped it.

She scrolled to Captain.

She hit send.

"Hello?" Freddie’s voice answered on the first ring. It was calm. It was flat. It was perfect.

"It's awful," Harper whispered, hunched over her phone in the dark room. "Freddie, it's a zoo."

"Clarify," Freddie said. "Is the facility dirty?"

"No," Harper said, tears spilling over. "The facility is fine. It’s the people. The clients. They... they scream. They fight."

She squeezed her eyes shut.

"There was a fight in the lounge. Over the wrestling show. A guy threw a chair."

"Physical violence?" Freddie asked, his voice tightening.

"He hit the wall," Harper said. "But it was loud. It was so loud."

"Ah," Freddie said. "Entropy. Disorder."

"I hate it," Harper admitted. "I like the soldering. I like the evaluation. But the unstructured time? The 'Free Time'? It makes my skin crawl. I just want to go home."

There was a pause on the line. She could hear the faint hum of the server rack in the background at Pepper Street. It sounded like the ocean.

"Harper," Freddie said. "Activate the Firewall."

"How?"

"You are a Satellite," Freddie reminded her. "Satellites do not engage with the atmosphere. They orbit above it."

He paused.

"Put on your headphones," Freddie commanded. "Play the playlist I made you. 'Orbital Mechanics'. It is instrumental. No lyrics. No drama."

"Okay," Harper sniffled.

"Do not engage with the chaos," Freddie continued. "Observe it. Catalog it. Treat it like a nature documentary. 'Here we see the humans creating unnecessary conflict.'"

Harper giggled. A wet, shaky sound. "They are loud humans."

"You are Avery," Freddie said firmly. "You thrive on clean data. If the data is corrupted, do not process it. Just filter it out."

"Filter it out," Harper repeated.

"I will stay on the line," Freddie offered. "I will not speak. I will just keep the connection open. You put the phone in your pocket. Put on the headphones. I will be the carrier wave."

"You'd do that?"

"Affirmative," Freddie said. "Leaving the line open."

Harper put her earbuds in. She plugged them into the phone. She put the phone in her hoodie pocket.

She could hear it—the faint, rhythmic breathing of her brother three hours away. A steady, white-noise presence.

She turned on her own music, low, mixing it with the open line.

She lay back on the bed. She closed her eyes.

She wasn't in Fishersville anymore. She was in the Unit.

## The Countdown

The week had been a gauntlet, but the "Freddie Protocol" had worked. Harper had worn her headphones like armor. She had eaten quickly. She had hidden in the library during free time.

Now, it was the final night.

Harper sat on her bed. Her suitcase was already packed. Every shirt was folded. Every sock was matched.

Her roommate, Kayla, was crying on the other bed.

"He isn't calling me back!" Kayla wailed, throwing her phone at the pillow. "He's probably with Jessica!"

Harper looked at Kayla. She ran a diagnostic.

Variable: Boyfriend. Status: Unreliable. Reaction: Hysteria.

Harper stood up. She walked over to Kayla’s bed. She didn't offer a hug (she reserved those for the Unit). She offered data.

"Kayla," Harper said, her voice steady.

Kayla looked up, mascara running. "What?"

"We leave in fourteen hours," Harper stated. "If he isn't calling you, he is a bad variable. You are wasting processing power on a glitch."

Kayla blinked. "A... glitch?"

"You passed your evaluation," Harper reminded her. "You got a recommendation for the Culinary program. That is real. The boy is just static."

Kayla sniffled. She wiped her eyes. "Static?"

"Noise," Harper said. "Turn it off."

She handed Kayla a tissue.

"We're going home tomorrow," Harper said. "Focus on the exit strategy."

Kayla took the tissue. She blew her nose. "You're weird, Harper."

"I know," Harper smiled, thinking of the Mega-Bed waiting for her. "I'm a Avery."

She walked back to her desk. She opened her notebook.

She drew a large square. She wrote a number inside it.

14 HOURS.

"System standby," Harper whispered. "Prepare for extraction."

## The Midnight Threshold

It was the final breakfast. The "Exit Protocol."

Harper sat at her usual defensive table near the door, her noise-canceling headphones around her neck. She wasn't eating. She was watching the clock on the wall.

08:32 AM. The family would be here in four and a half hours.

Across the room, the source of the static was holding court.

Travis was sitting on a table—not a chair, the table—surrounded by his sycophants. He was wearing a new t-shirt that said LEGALLY LETHAL.

He was louder than usual today. He was vibrating with a specific, arrogant energy.

"Tomorrow," Travis shouted, pointing a fork at a terrified freshman. "Tomorrow is the day, little man. Midnight tonight. The clock strikes twelve, and I am a free agent."

"Happy birthday, Travis," one of the girls simpered.

"It ain't just a birthday," Travis sneered, looking around the room until his eyes locked on Harper in the corner. "It’s a license. I turn eighteen tomorrow. That means I’m an adult. I can do what I want. I can sign myself out. I can go where I want."

He grinned at Harper. It was a wolfish, hungry grin.

"No more kiddie rules," Travis said, his voice dropping just enough to be terrifying. "Tomorrow, I’m a grown man. And grown men take what they want."

Harper gripped her tray. The math hit her like a physical blow.

Today is Friday the 14th. Tomorrow is Saturday the 15th. He turns 18 tomorrow.

She realized with a jolt of cold terror that she was escaping just in time. If she stayed for the weekend... if she was still here when he became a legal adult... the dynamic would shift. He wouldn't just be a bully. He would be a man targeting a child.

He thinks turning 18 gives him permission, Harper realized. He doesn't know it just gives him a prison sentence.

She stood up. She dumped her tray. She rolled out of the cafeteria without looking back.

Four hours, she told herself. Just four hours until the Unit gets here.
