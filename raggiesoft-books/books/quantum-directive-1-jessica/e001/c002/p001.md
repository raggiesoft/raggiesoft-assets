## Homecoming

The late‑summer light was already slanting low when the car turned off the main road and onto the long gravel drive. Jessica leaned her head against the window, watching the familiar silhouette of the old building rise ahead — brick and stone, broad‑shouldered, with the kind of symmetry that spoke of its past life as something official. A school, once. Now, it was theirs.

The driver eased to a stop in front of the wide steps. The front doors stood open, letting the warm air spill out along with the sound of voices — her siblings, unmistakable even at a distance.

She wheeled her suitcase inside, the scent of the place wrapping around her: wood polish, laundry soap, and the faint trace of coffee that seemed to live in the walls.

Aubrie was the first to spot her, appearing in the hallway with her hair pulled back and a smudge of something — probably from her latest web project — on her cheek. “Jess!” she called, and in a heartbeat they were hugging, the kind of hug that pressed away months of absence.

From somewhere deeper in the house came Amanda’s voice, bright and quick: “She’s here?” Footsteps pounded, and then Amanda was there too, grinning, already talking about a dozen things at once.

Elise emerged more quietly, but her smile was no less warm. She didn’t rush — she never did — but when she reached Jessica, her hand rested on her shoulder in a way that said \*welcome home\* without words.

And then Dorian appeared, rolling down the wide hallway in his chair, his eyes lighting up when he saw her. Jessica crouched to meet him, their foreheads touching for a moment in greeting. He tapped her wrist twice — their shorthand for \*I’m glad you’re here\*.

The rest of the evening unfolded in the easy rhythm of family: voices overlapping in the kitchen, the clatter of plates, the smell of something baking. They ate at the long table, the windows open to the sound of crickets, and for the first time in weeks, Jessica felt the Expo’s noise and lights fade into the background.

Here, she wasn’t the lead developer who’d stood on stage beside Stephen Marchand and William Keswick. She was just Jessica — eldest sister, home at last, surrounded by the people who knew her best.

## Part 2: Unwelcome Currents

Jessica had slept late, the kind of deep, dreamless sleep that only comes after weeks of running on adrenaline. Even so, she woke with the Expo still clinging to her — the glare of stage lights, the hum of the crowd, the weight of being *seen*.

Her laptop sat open on the desk by the window, the unread email still at the top of her inbox:

**From:** Quantum Communications\
**Subject:** Press Inquiries Protocol\
Please redirect all media requests to the Quantum press office. Do not engage directly.

She’d read it twice last night, the words both a relief and a reminder that her life had shifted in ways she hadn’t yet processed.

The kitchen was already alive when she rolled in. The *Providence Standard* — the *ProStan*, as everyone called it — was spread wide across the table, its pages curling at the edges. The big headline in the business section blared about William Keswick’s appearance at MagnaWorld Expo, complete with a grainy photo of him at the podium. There was no mention of Jessica; this was a business headline, not a tech one, and she was fine with that.

Tall glasses of Coffee Milk stood beside bowls of cereal, spoons clinking softly. Aubrie was perched sideways in her chair, scanning the arts section while absently stirring her flakes. Amanda had already abandoned her own bowl to sketch something on the back of an envelope, muttering about a new idea. Elise sat nearest the door, her spoon untouched, eyes flicking toward the driveway every so often.

The knock came mid‑morning. Not the easy tap of a neighbor, but a brisk, deliberate rhythm.

Elise was on her feet instantly. “Stay here,” she said, already moving toward the front hall.

Jessica set her spoon down and followed, the hum of her chair blending with the muffled voices outside. Through the glass of the front doors, she saw them: three people, cameras and notepads in hand, their posture already leaning forward like they could smell a story.

Elise opened the door just enough to step into the frame, her shoulders squared.

“Can I help you?”

The man with the microphone smiled like it was a formality.

“We’re with TechLine Weekly. We’d like to speak with Jessica Taylor about her appearance at MagnaWorld Expo.”

Jessica’s stomach tightened.

Elise didn’t blink.

“Any press inquiries go through Quantum’s communications office. You can find their contact information online.”

The woman with the notepad tried again.

“We just have a few questions—”

“And I just gave you the answer,” Elise said, her tone still even but edged with steel. “This is a private residence. You need to leave.”

She closed the door firmly, the latch clicking into place.

From the kitchen, Amanda called, “Who was that?”

Elise turned the deadbolt. “Nobody we’re letting in.”

Jessica lingered in the hallway, the weight of it settling in. The Expo had followed her home.

Elise’s gaze softened as she looked at her.

“You’re safe here. They don’t get past me.”

Back at the table, the *ProStan* still lay open, the Coffee Milk still cold, the cereal bowls waiting. Outside, the driveway was empty again, but Jessica knew it wouldn’t be the last knock.

## Part 3: Evening Edition

By the time the sun had dropped low over the Sakonnet, the Taylors were gathered around the long dining table. The smell of baked haddock and roasted vegetables mingled with the faint salt drifting in through the open windows.

Halfway through passing the bread basket, the familiar thump of the evening paper landing on the porch broke the rhythm. Aubrie was up in a flash, returning with the *Newport Harbor News* folded under her arm. She set it beside Jessica’s plate with a little flourish.

“Harbor News,” she said. “Straight from Newport.”

The front page was all Aquidneck Island: a feature on the upcoming harvest festival in Middletown, a report on ferry schedule changes, and a photo of the Tiverton High girls’ soccer team celebrating a win.

Flipping to the business section brought déjà vu — the same bold headline the *ProStan* had run that morning: **Keswick Draws Record Crowds at MagnaWorld Expo**. Same grainy podium photo, same focus on market impact and investor buzz.

But in the technology section, Jessica’s name was impossible to miss.

**Newport Native Shares Stage with Industry Giant**\
Beneath the headline, a candid shot from the Expo stage caught her mid‑gesture, eyes bright. The article traced her path from growing up in Newport — with time spent in Middletown and now Portsmouth — to graduating from Rogers High School, heading west to Northwest Pacific University, and joining Quantum straight out of college. It highlighted her role as lead developer and co‑architect of Aurora Presenter, praised her work in accessible design, and noted she was “home for a short visit” after the Expo.

Jessica stared at the photo for a moment, fork suspended.

Amanda leaned over. “They actually got your hometown right.”

Jessica gave a small laugh. Of course they had. On Aquidneck Island, if you were born in a maternity ward — civilian or military — you were born in Newport. It was just how the geography worked, and the *Harbor News* knew its own backyard.

Elise, without looking up from her plate, said, “Doesn’t matter. They still don’t get to come knocking.”

Jessica set the paper down, the clink of cutlery and low hum of conversation wrapping around her again. Outside, the last light faded over the bay. Inside, she was still just Jessica — eldest sister, home for dinner — even if the rest of the island was starting to take notice.

## Part 4: The \\#60 into Town

Morning broke clear and bright, the kind of late‑summer day that made the bay look like polished glass. Jessica had been home long enough now for the Expo’s noise to fade into the background, replaced by the steady rhythm of island life.

Today, they were all going into Newport for breakfast — the whole Taylor crew. Which meant, for the first time in years, Jessica and Dorian would be riding the bus together again.

At the corner stop, the green‑and‑white \#60 rumbled into view, brakes sighing as it pulled up. The driver — a man with a weathered face and a **Boston Beacons** cap pulled low — caught sight of them in the mirror, then rose from his seat and walked to the back door.

“Two lifts today,” he said with a grin, swinging the door open.

The familiar clunk and whir of the hydraulic platform filled the air as it lowered to the pavement. Jessica went first, rolling onto the metal plate, the sun warming her tires. The lift rose smoothly to meet the bus floor, and she wheeled into the securement spot near the back.

Then it was Dorian’s turn. Elise stayed close, one hand on the back of his chair as he rolled forward. The driver worked the controls with practiced ease, chatting about the weather as the platform lifted again.

Once both chairs were locked in, the rest of the family filed aboard — Aubrie with her tote bag of notebooks, Amanda already craning to see who else was on the bus, Elise taking the seat closest to Dorian.

The ride was a moving scrapbook: Portsmouth’s quiet streets giving way to Middletown’s mix of shops and clapboard houses, the bakery where Aubrie and Jessica used to split almond macaroons, the park where Amanda once tried to fly a kite in a dead calm, the stretch of road where the bay suddenly opened up in a flash of blue.

By the time they crossed into Newport, the city was waking up in earnest. Sidewalk cafés were setting out chairs, gulls wheeled over the harbor, and the brick facades along Broadway caught the morning light.

Jessica felt a grin tug at her face. She’d missed this — the easy chatter of strangers, the jolt of the bus over a pothole, the way the driver waved to every other bus they passed.

Today wasn’t about the Expo, or the headlines, or the knocks at the door. Today was about breakfast in town, the clink of coffee cups, and the comfort of being just another family on the \#60 — two wheelchairs, four sisters, one easy morning.

## Part 5: The Creamery Along the Wall

The \#60 carried them south along West Main Road, the morning sun glinting off shop windows and the occasional passing car. Jessica sat in the securement spot beside Dorian, the rest of the family scattered in nearby seats, the low murmur of conversation blending with the steady hum of the engine.

At **One Mile Corner**, the bus crossed from Middletown into Newport. West Main Road became **Broadway** — no more Route 114 designation — and the storefronts grew closer together: diners with steaming windows, corner markets, and the occasional bar still shuttered from the night before.

At the end of Broadway, the driver swung right onto **Marlborough Street**, then right again onto **America’s Cup Avenue**, the harbor flashing briefly between buildings. Moments later, the bus turned left into the **Newport Gateway Center**.

They rolled off together, the air here sharper with salt and the faint tang of diesel from idling coaches. The \#67 Bellevue Avenue was already waiting in its bay. Smaller and quieter than the \#60, it wound its way past shops, cafés, and the library’s stone façade.

When the driver glanced back in the mirror, Jessica called out without hesitation, “We’re going to the Creamery along the wall — can you drop us at Almacs?”

He grinned and nodded. No need to ask which corner she meant. The store had been gone for two years, but the label scar still clung to the building, and the name still clung to the street. In Rhode Island, some landmarks never die — they just live on in directions.

A few minutes later, the bus eased to the curb at **Memorial Boulevard** — though to old‑timers it would always be **Bath Road** — in front of the old Almacs building.

At the far corner of the shopping center, tucked along the wall, was the **Bayview Creamery**.

The windows gleamed in the morning light, and taped to the glass beside the door was yesterday’s *Harbor News* technology section — Jessica’s Expo photo staring back at her, mid‑gesture, eyes bright. Someone had even circled her name in red pen.

Inside, the air was cool and smelled faintly of coffee, syrup, and the sweet vanilla of ice cream from the cases along the counter. The booths were the same pale green she remembered, the Formica tables polished smooth by years of elbows and coffee cups.

From behind the counter, a familiar voice called out, “Well, if it isn’t Jessica Taylor!”

Her old manager — now the general manager — came around to greet them, wiping his hands on a dish towel. He looked older, sure, but the grin was the same. “I saw the paper yesterday. Didn’t think I’d get to see you in person so soon.”

Jessica smiled, feeling the years fold in on themselves. “It’s good to be back.”

He glanced at the rest of the family. “Breakfast for the whole crew? You know the drill — grab a booth, I’ll bring the menus.”

As they settled in, Jessica caught her reflection in the window — her Expo photo still taped outside, the morning sun spilling over it. Inside, though, she was just another customer at the Creamery, back in the booth where she’d once balanced trays and refilled coffee cups, the hum of conversation and clink of silverware wrapping around her like it always had.

## Part 6: Breakfast at the Creamery

The menus were the same laminated sheets Jessica remembered, the corners softened from years of use. The specials board still leaned slightly to one side, chalk letters announcing *Blueberry Pancakes — \$3.99* and *Johnnycakes with Maple Syrup*.

They slid into a corner booth — Jessica and Dorian on the aisle side, the others crowding in opposite. The tabletop was cool under her hands, the Formica pattern unchanged since the summer she’d worked here, balancing trays and refilling coffee cups for the morning regulars.

Her old manager came by with a pot of coffee and a stack of glasses. “Coffee Milk for the lot of you?” he asked, already reaching for the syrup bottle.

“You know us too well,” Elise said.

The first sip was exactly as Jessica remembered — sweet, malty, and just enough caffeine to take the edge off the morning. Plates began to arrive: steaming stacks of pancakes, eggs over easy, toast with slabs of salted butter. The clink of cutlery and the low hum of conversation filled the air.

Halfway through her johnnycakes, Jessica noticed two women at the counter glancing toward their booth. One of them nodded toward the taped‑up *Harbor News* article outside, then back at her. Jessica gave a polite smile and returned to her plate.

Her manager stopped by again, topping off coffee cups. “You still got the same smile you had when you worked here,” he said. “Only difference is now it’s in the paper. You want me to put you down for a Colossal Cooler?”

Jessica laughed. “Only if you’re ready to comp me the fourth one.”

Amanda grinned. “Wait — you’re not actually going to try the three‑Cooler challenge, are you?”

Jessica shook her head. “Not today. I’ve seen what happens when you push it. Whipped cream headaches for hours.”

From the next booth, an older man in a **Boston Beacons** cap leaned over. “Back in my day, we called that Tuesday.”

The table erupted in easy laughter, the kind that came from years of shared shorthand. Outside, the morning sun caught on the glass, lighting up her photo in the window. Inside, the Creamery smelled of coffee, syrup, and vanilla ice cream, and Jessica felt the rare, solid comfort of belonging — not as a headline, but as herself.

## The First Autograph

The shared laughter settled back into the comfortable hum of the Creamery. For a few more minutes, everything was as it should be: the scrape of forks on plates, the sweet aftertaste of Coffee Milk, the easy rhythm of family conversation. The bubble of normalcy felt solid, safe.

Then it popped.

The two women who had been watching from the counter finally stood up and approached the booth, their steps hesitant. One clutched her purse, the other a folded copy of the *Harbor News*.

“Excuse me,” the first woman said, her voice soft. “I’m so sorry to bother you. Are you Jessica Taylor?”

The question hung in the air, instantly changing the temperature at the table. Elise’s posture straightened, her expression shifting from relaxed to watchful. Amanda, however, leaned forward with a look of pure fascination.

Jessica managed a small, tight smile. “I am.”

The woman’s face broke into a relieved grin. “I thought so! My daughter, Sophie—she’s fourteen and she’s learning to code. She follows your work. When she saw the article last night, she nearly came unglued. She thinks what you’re doing with accessible design is… well, she called you a legend.”

Jessica’s throat felt dry. A legend? She was an engineer who spent her days chasing down bugs and refactoring code. The word felt like a costume that didn't fit.

“That’s… very kind of her,” Jessica said, the words feeling inadequate.

The second woman unfolded the newspaper, revealing the tech section with Jessica's picture. “We were wondering—and please say no if it’s too much—but would you be willing to sign this for her? She’d never forgive us if we didn’t ask.”

An autograph. The request was so genuine, so disconnected from the world of compilers and beta freezes, that Jessica was momentarily speechless. She wasn’t a rock star. She wasn’t an actor. She was a developer. Developers didn't sign autographs.

Elise started to open her mouth, ready to politely decline for her, but Amanda gave a tiny, excited shake of her head.

Seeing the hopeful look on the women’s faces, Jessica knew she couldn’t say no. She took the pen they offered. The cheap plastic felt foreign in her hand. For a beat, she didn’t know what to write. She settled on signing her name just above the headline, and below it, she added two simple words:

*Keep building.*

She handed it back. The women beamed, thanking her profusely before heading for the door, clutching the signed paper like a treasure.

The moment they were gone, the silence at the table was thick with unspoken thoughts.

Amanda broke it first, her voice a gleeful whisper. “You’re signing autographs now! Jess, this is officially a thing.”

“Are you alright?” Elise asked, her gaze searching Jessica’s face. “We can leave.”

“No, I’m okay,” Jessica said, though the smile she gave didn't quite reach her eyes. She looked past her family, her gaze landing on her own face in the window, circled in red pen. The girl in the photo looked confident, poised, a part of history. The woman in the booth just wanted to finish her johnnycakes.

Her old manager came by to clear their plates, a proud grin spreading across his face. “Saw that,” he said, nodding toward the door. “Breakfast is on the house today. For our local celebrity.”

The word hung there, another reminder that the quiet, anonymous life she had known was slipping away, one signature at a time.
