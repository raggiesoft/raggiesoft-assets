The humid morning air of Virginia Beach was already clinging to us by the time my father parked the family station wagon at the Newport News (NPN) transit station. It was the summer of 1995, and I was ten years old. My little sister, Casey, was eight, and she was currently vibrating with the uncontainable energy of a child who firmly believed she was about to make a massive, life-altering decision.

"I just need to see the campus," Casey insisted, marching beside my father as he pulled our rolling suitcases toward the rail platform. "I know VDU is closer, and their summer camp is great, but George Wythe has a completely different architectural profile. I need to physically walk the quad."

My mother smiled tiredly, handing me my backpack. "You are eight, Casey. You have a decade before you have to declare a major."

"It is never too early to optimize," Casey shot back, adjusting the straps of her brightly colored backpack. She was right about the summer camps, though; as soon as we got back from this open house trip, both of us were being shipped off. I had been going for a few years now, but this was going to be Casey's first time at the Virginia Dominion University summer session, and she was treating it like a serious academic fellowship.

Rolling effortlessly alongside us was my older sister, Cassandra. At seventeen, Cassandra was a rising senior at Northwood High School, and she was the actual reason we were all standing on a train platform before eight in the morning.

Cassandra was navigating her manual wheelchair with the kind of casual, practiced fluid motion that came from years of muscle memory. Her axial load injury was just a standard fact of our family's logistics now, an unquestioned baseline that Cassandra had brutally optimized long ago. 

"The ARTS Northeast Regional is on time," Cassandra announced, glancing down at a printed itinerary resting on her lap. She didn't look up as she adjusted her glasses. "We connect in Alexandria. From there, we transition to the Capital Transit bus network to Dunn Loring, catch the Orange Line, and then intercept the CCT at Vienna. It is a seamless loop."

My father sighed, looking at the massive, folding accessibility map my sister had meticulously highlighted. "Cassandra, I'm all for taking the train up to ALX. We always do. But there is absolutely no reason to subject the entire family to a surface bus transfer through Seven Corners. We could just get on the Blue Line, ride it to Rosslyn, and transfer directly to the Orange Line to Vienna."

"The Rosslyn elevator bank is a highly volatile variable," Cassandra replied instantly, her voice carrying that sharp, unyielding certainty that she used whenever someone questioned her logistics. "A hydraulic failure at a deep-level transfer station would completely sever our route. Furthermore, I need to verify the accessibility tolerances of the Capital Transit surface grid. If I am going to accept an offer from George Wythe University, I must know that I can navigate the metropolitan area autonomously even when the rail network degrades. I will not be tethered to an elevator shaft."

"You're making us ride a bus through Seven Corners," my mother muttered, rubbing her temples. Having grown up right along that stretch of US-50, she knew the geography intimately. "I hated driving through that intersection as a teenager, Cassandra. It is a chaotic nightmare on a good day."

"The CCT and Capital Transit drivers are professionals," Cassandra countered, finally locking her brakes as we reached the edge of the platform. "And the route is mathematically sound."

I looked at Cassandra. Even at seventeen, she didn't just plan trips; she executed operations. She was already treating her college tours like tactical deployments. Once this George Wythe trip was over, and Casey and I finished our VDU summer camps, the family was flying all the way to Washington State to tour the Seattle campus of Northwest Pacific University.

But for now, the distant, heavy blast of a train horn echoed down the tracks. The ARTS Northeast Regional was pulling into NPN. Cassandra unlocked her brakes, her eyes fixed sharply on the horizon. The great college expedition had officially begun.
