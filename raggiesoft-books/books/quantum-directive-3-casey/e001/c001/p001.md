## 8:00 AM — Departing NPN

The humid morning air of Virginia Beach was already clinging to us by the time my father parked the family station wagon at the Newport News (NPN) transit station. It was the summer of 1995, and I was ten years old. My little sister, Casey, was eight, and she was currently vibrating with the uncontainable energy of a child who firmly believed she was about to make a massive, life-altering decision.

"I just need to see the campus," Casey insisted, marching beside my father as he pulled our rolling suitcases toward the rail platform. "I know VDU is closer, and their summer camp is great, but George Wythe has a completely different architectural profile. I need to physically walk the quad."

My mother smiled tiredly, handing me my backpack. "You are eight, Casey. You have a decade before you have to declare a major."

"It is never too early to optimize," Casey shot back, adjusting the straps of her brightly colored backpack. She was right about the summer camps, though; as soon as we got back from this open house trip, both of us were being shipped off. I had been going for a few years now, but this was going to be Casey's first time at the Virginia Dominion University summer session, and she was treating it like a serious academic fellowship.

Rolling effortlessly alongside us was my older sister, Cassandra. At seventeen, Cassandra was a rising senior at Northwood High School, and she was the actual reason we were all standing on a train platform before eight in the morning.

Cassandra was navigating her manual wheelchair with the kind of casual, practiced fluid motion that came from years of muscle memory. Her axial load injury was just a standard fact of our family's logistics now, an unquestioned baseline that Cassandra had brutally optimized long ago. 

"ARTS *The Patriot* is on time," Cassandra announced, glancing down at a printed itinerary resting on her lap. She didn't look up as she adjusted her glasses. "We connect in Alexandria. From there, we transition to the Capital Transit bus network to Dunn Loring, catch the Orange Line, and then intercept the CCT at Vienna. It is a seamless loop."

My father sighed, looking at the massive, folding accessibility map my sister had meticulously highlighted. "Cassandra, I'm all for taking the train up to ALX. We always do. But there is absolutely no reason to subject the entire family to a surface bus transfer through Seven Corners. We could just get on the Blue Line, ride it to Rosslyn, and transfer directly to the Orange Line to Vienna."

"The Rosslyn elevator bank is a highly volatile variable," Cassandra replied instantly, her voice carrying that sharp, unyielding certainty that she used whenever someone questioned her logistics. "A hydraulic failure at a deep-level transfer station would completely sever our route. Furthermore, I need to verify the accessibility tolerances of the Capital Transit surface grid. If I am going to accept an offer from George Wythe University, I must know that I can navigate the metropolitan area autonomously even when the rail network degrades. I will not be tethered to an elevator shaft."

"You're making us ride a bus through Seven Corners," my mother muttered, rubbing her temples. Having grown up right along that stretch of US-50, she knew the geography intimately. "I hated driving through that intersection as a teenager, Cassandra. It is a chaotic nightmare on a good day."

"The CCT and Capital Transit drivers are professionals," Cassandra countered, finally locking her brakes as we reached the edge of the platform. "And the route is mathematically sound."

I looked at Cassandra. Even at seventeen, she didn't just plan trips; she executed operations. She was already treating her college tours like tactical deployments. Once this George Wythe trip was over, and Casey and I finished our VDU summer camps, the family was flying all the way to Washington State to tour the Seattle campus of Northwest Pacific University.

But for now, the distant, heavy blast of a train horn echoed down the tracks. ARTS *The Patriot* was pulling into NPN. Cassandra unlocked her brakes, her eyes fixed sharply on the horizon. The great college expedition had officially begun.

***

## 8:30 AM — The Northern Regional

The ARTS *The Patriot* train rocked gently as it cleared the limits of Newport News, settling into a smooth, high-speed cadence on the rails heading north. 

Inside the designated accessibility car, our seating arrangement was mathematically precise. Cassandra was parked securely in the open wheelchair bay, her brakes locked. Because of the orientation of the tie-downs, she was facing backward relative to the train’s movement, allowing her to look directly at Casey and me. 

My sister and I were seated side-by-side in the two fold-down companion seats facing Cassandra. Just behind us, Mom and Dad were settled into a standard pair of forward-facing seats, within earshot but thankfully out of the immediate blast radius of Casey's academic interrogation. 

"I have already analyzed the George Wythe University computer science curriculum," Casey announced, crossing her eight-year-old arms over her chest and swinging her legs. Her feet didn’t even touch the floor of the train. "It is a highly competitive program, Cassandra. But we need to look at the macro-level logistics. VDU is practically in our backyard. The Virginia Dominion campus has excellent STEM pathways. I should know, I've toured it thoroughly."

"You attended a VDU summer camp orientation," Cassandra corrected mildly, pulling a thick spiral-bound college course catalog from her messenger bag and dropping it onto her lap. "That is not a collegiate tour, Casey."

"It gave me a baseline understanding of the architecture," Casey argued without missing a beat. "And if we go to Seattle to tour NPU later this summer, you'll be dealing with severe topographical elevation changes on the Seattle campus. Washington State is not flat. Have you calculated the gradient of the walkways at Northwest Pacific?"

I leaned my head back against the window, watching the blur of the Virginia treeline. I was ten, and I was mostly just excited we were taking the train instead of driving, but Casey was acting like she was a senior admissions counselor personally tasked with evaluating Cassandra’s future. 

"The NPU campus gradients have already been factored into my mobility profile," Cassandra replied seamlessly, completely indulging our eight-year-old sister's intense line of questioning. "But George Wythe offers a distinct advantage regarding proximity to the District's tech corridor. The Dulles technology hub is expanding. A computer science degree from GWU provides immediate lateral access to federal contracting and emerging tech sectors."

"But what about the dorms?" Casey pressed, leaning forward. "We have to inspect the dorms today. You can't just look at the academic buildings. We need to verify the turning radius in the bathrooms and the desk heights."

"We will," Cassandra promised, tapping the course catalog. "We have an appointment with their disability counselor this afternoon. I intend to inspect a standard freshman housing unit."

From the seats behind us, my father leaned forward, resting his forearms on the back of my chair. "I still don't understand why we couldn't just book a room at a chain hotel in Rosslyn. Why are we staying at a motel in Vienna?"

Cassandra didn't even blink. "Because the motel in Vienna is located directly on the CCT bus loop. The CCT acts as a dedicated transit tether between the Vienna Orange Line station and the George Wythe campus. If I live on campus, I will need to use that loop constantly. Therefore, we must establish a base camp along the route to test its operational frequency."

"And she booked us two adjoining rooms," Casey added proudly, as if she had personally negotiated the hotel contract. "Mom and Dad in one room, and the three of us in the accessible room. With a connecting door."

Mom let out a soft laugh from the row behind us. "Yes, thank you, Casey. We are well aware of the rooming logistics. Just remember that tomorrow morning is going to be an early start."

"I know," Casey said, her expression turning deadly serious. "The Open House continental breakfast begins at exactly 0730 hours. I have to secure a good table before the welcoming speech."

I looked out the window again, smiling. Casey wasn't just tagging along on her older sister's college tour. In her mind, she was already enrolled.

***

## 12:45 PM — The Seven Corners Transfer

The moment ARTS *The Patriot* pulled into Alexandria Station (ALX), our family’s transition from passengers to a unified logistical unit was immediate.

"Deploy the ramp," Cassandra commanded quietly to the conductor as the doors slid open. Once her wheels hit the concrete, she took point. 

My father grabbed our two largest rolling suitcases, while Mom handled the garment bags. Casey—wearing her brightly colored backpack and clutching a printed Capital Transit schedule as if it were a classified dossier—marched right beside Cassandra. I trailed just behind them, pulling my own small suitcase.

"We need to clear the platform," Casey instructed, her eight-year-old voice trying to project authority over the din of the station. "The long ramp is ahead, which leads to the subterranean tunnel. That will route us directly to the surface-level bus terminal."

"Thank you, Casey," my mother said dryly, shifting the weight of the bags on her shoulder. "Lead the way."

Cassandra didn't need any instructions. She rolled smoothly down the long, sloping ADA ramp, conserving her momentum. We followed her through the echoing, fluorescent-lit tunnel and emerged up into the bright afternoon heat at the ALX bus terminal.

"Bay D," Cassandra stated, locking her brakes near a concrete pillar. "The 28K."

When the massive Capital Transit bus pulled up and lowered its hydraulic suspension, Cassandra wheeled herself aboard, settling into the priority bay while Dad paid the fares. For the next hour, we rumbled through the dense, crawling suburban sprawl of Northern Virginia. Just as my mother had predicted, the traffic began to severely coagulate as we approached the infamous Seven Corners intersection. 

Mom stared out the window at the chaotic, multi-lane nightmare of cars trying to merge across US-50, her jaw tight. "I haven't missed this," she muttered.

Cassandra, however, watched the gridlock with cold, analytical satisfaction. "The bus has dedicated mass-transit mass. Smaller vehicles yield. The timeline is holding."

The 28K eventually broke free of the intersection, rumbling down toward US-29. The driver pulled over at the near side of Gallows Road, and the ramp hissed down to the pavement.

"Alright, dismount," Casey ordered, hopping off the bus. "We have to cross US-29. The 1C picks up on the far side."

We waited for the crosswalk signal, a small, heavily-laden family unit crossing the blistering asphalt of the highway. Once we secured our position at the far shelter, the 1C bus arrived within five minutes. We boarded, and it was a short, highly-efficient shot directly into the Dunn Loring rail station. 

Stepping into the Dunn Loring station offered a brief respite, trading the oppressive surface heat for the blasts of air-conditioning from the idling Orange Line train waiting in the median of I-66. Cassandra effortlessly swiped through the wide ADA faregate. We took the Orange Line for exactly one stop, disembarking at Vienna Station.

"Final transfer," Cassandra announced as we navigated the open-air concrete platform to the surface bus bays. "The CCT Green Line 1."

The CCT bus was smaller than the massive Capital Transit coaches, but it featured a flawless, low-floor boarding ramp. We climbed aboard, and the bus initiated its loop out of the Vienna transit center, rolling smoothly toward the George Wythe campus. 

When the bus pulled up to the curb outside our motel, we finally disembarked. The brutal humidity hit us again, but the massive, exhausting transfer was over. The surface fleet had delivered us right to the front door.

"I'll secure the reservations," Casey announced confidently, adjusting her backpack as she marched toward the motel lobby's glass doors. "I have the confirmation numbers memorized."

"That's very helpful, sweetie," Mom said, reaching out and gently snagging Casey by the shoulder strap of her backpack before she could push the doors open. "But I think your father and I will handle the check-in. They generally require a credit card and an adult with a driver's license."

Casey sighed, clearly annoyed by the bureaucratic red tape. "Fine. But request ground-floor adjacency. Cassandra cannot navigate a motel stairwell."

"We booked the accessible suite, Casey," Dad assured her, pulling the heavy glass door open for the family. "I promise, the logistics are secured. Let's just get into the air conditioning."

## 6:30 PM — The Crown Burger Debrief

Once we unloaded our luggage into the adjoining motel rooms, my parents realized exactly how exhausted they were from the four-leg transit gauntlet. Rather than attempting to navigate a sit-down restaurant, we opted for the immediate, high-caloric efficiency of the Crown Burger located right next door in the same parking lot.

We pushed a couple of plastic tables together near the window. The air-conditioning in the fast-food dining room was blasting, providing welcome relief from the thick Virginia heat.

Casey was practically vibrating as she tore into the small cardboard box of her kids' meal, immediately extracting a cheap, plastic wind-up toy. At ten years old, I had finally decided I was too old for the kids' menu. I sat across from her, unwrapping my standard Crown Burger with a quiet sense of maturity. I felt like I was starting to outgrow the childish stuff, but as I watched Casey gleefully wind up her plastic car and send it skittering across the Formica table, I kept my mouth shut. I didn't want to spoil it for her. I wanted my kid sister to just enjoy being a kid.

"Alright, let's review the timeline for tomorrow," Casey announced between bites of French fries. She pulled a folded, slightly crumpled printed itinerary from her pocket and smoothed it out on the table. "Registration is at 0800 in the student union. The welcoming speech is at 0830. After that, we need to divide and conquer to maximize our coverage of the—"

"Casey, I appreciate the tactical support," Cassandra interrupted gently, placing her hand softly over our little sister's printed schedule. "But I have the logistics for tomorrow secured."

Casey blinked, looking up at her older sister. "You do?"

"I do," Cassandra nodded, taking a sip of her soda. "It's going to operate exactly like the VDU open house we attended last week. They're going to try and funnel everyone into massive, generalized campus tours. We are going to bypass the general tour and proceed directly to the Computer Science department. I need to verify their hardware infrastructure before I commit."

My father paused, wiping his hands with a napkin. "What exactly are you looking for, Cassandra? Doesn't every college have computers?"

"There is a massive discrepancy in departmental funding right now," Cassandra explained, her eyes lighting up with that intense, analytical fire she always got when discussing systems architecture. "The industry is shifting entirely toward object-oriented programming. C++ is the established standard, but the major Silicon Valley firms just announced a new cross-platform runtime architecture in May. The entire structure of the World Wide Web is about to fundamentally change."

Mom stared at her, completely lost. "Right. The... Web."

"If George Wythe University is still teaching archaic procedural languages on outdated mainframes, they are useless to me," Cassandra continued smoothly, ignoring the blank stares of our parents. "I need to see their advanced server labs. I need to verify that they have T1 internet backbone access for their undergraduates, not just the graduate researchers. With Quantum OS 95 launching next month, the home PC market is about to explode, and I refuse to spend four years at an institution that is technologically stagnant."

Casey nodded solemnly, despite definitely not understanding what a T1 backbone was. "So we bypass the general tour. We secure the advanced server lab."

"Exactly," Cassandra smiled, leaning back in her manual wheelchair and picking up a French fry. "Then, at 1300 hours, we meet with the disability counselor to inspect the freshman dormitories. I need to physically measure the desk clearance to ensure my custom desktop rig will fit. It has a heavy CRT monitor and a full-tower ATX case, so standard dorm furniture might require structural modification."

"I can measure the desks," Casey offered eagerly, winding up her plastic toy car again. "I'll bring the tape measure."

"You are officially assigned to measurement duty," Cassandra agreed with a serious nod, delegating the task perfectly to keep our little sister engaged. 

I took another bite of my burger, looking around the plastic tables of the Crown Burger. Tomorrow was going to be a massive, exhausting day of academic tours and ADA compliance checks. But as I watched Cassandra seamlessly manage Casey's energy and outline her ruthless technological requirements, I realized something. 

Cassandra didn't just want to attend college. She was planning to conquer it.
