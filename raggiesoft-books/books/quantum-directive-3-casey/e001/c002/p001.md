## 7:30 AM — The Continental Breakfast

The George Wythe University Student Union was a massive, echoing atrium of brutalist concrete and large glass windows, designed to look impressive on brochures but currently functioning as a highly inefficient echo chamber. 

By 7:30 AM, the space was already swarming with nervous high school upperclassmen and their overly caffeinated parents. Tables were laden with massive stainless steel coffee urns, towers of slightly stale bagels, and sweating pitchers of orange juice. 

While my parents navigated the chaotic registration tables to collect Cassandra's printed itinerary and campus map, Casey immediately took tactical control of the dining area.

"Here," Casey ordered, dropping her brightly colored backpack onto a chair at a large circular table near the perimeter of the room. "This table has an unobstructed sightline to the podium, but it's positioned close enough to the exit that we won't get bottlenecked when the welcoming speech concludes. David, secure the perimeter. I am going to procure provisions."

I sat down at the table, suppressing a laugh. I was ten, and I was entirely content to let my eight-year-old sister run point on breakfast logistics. "I'll guard the backpacks, Casey. Get me a blueberry muffin if they have them."

A few minutes later, Cassandra rolled smoothly up to the table, locking her brakes. She had a campus map resting on her lap, but she barely glanced at it. She was already scanning the room, evaluating the crowd, the faculty standing near the walls, and the overall operational flow of the event. 

"The accessibility access to this building is acceptable," Cassandra noted quietly, picking up one of the brochures my mother had brought over. "The ramp grading was within standard tolerances, and the automatic doors functioned perfectly. However, the elevator bank is undersized for a student union of this capacity."

Mom sat down, looking exhausted before the day had even officially begun. Dad returned a moment later, carrying a tray loaded with coffee, bagels, and a massive plate of fruit. 

"I have secured the high-value items," Casey announced, marching back to the table with two blueberry muffins clutched in her small hands. She dropped one onto my napkin and took her seat next to Cassandra. 

As the family settled in to eat, a microphone screeched at the front of the room. A sharply dressed university administrator stepped up to the podium, adjusting the mic stand. 

"Welcome, prospective students and families, to George Wythe University!" the administrator's voice boomed, echoing harshly off the concrete walls. "We are thrilled to have you here today. GWU is an institution that prides itself on looking to the future..."

Cassandra quietly took a bite of her bagel, her eyes fixed on the administrator. She was listening to the speech, but I could tell her processor was already running completely different calculations. She wasn't here to be sold on the university's prestige or their marketing brochures. She was here to stress-test their infrastructure. 

"When this concludes," Cassandra leaned over and whispered to Casey, completely ignoring the booming speech echoing around us, "we bypass the guided walking tour entirely. We proceed straight to the academic quad. I need to see the Computer Science building."

Casey nodded, taking a bite of her muffin. "I have the route memorized. We move on your signal."

***

## 9:45 AM — The Architecture of the Past

Despite Cassandra's earlier insistence on bypassing the general campus tour, she quickly realized that the George Wythe University campus layout was highly irregular. Because we lacked the necessary topographic data to navigate the winding footpaths and dense brick facades autonomously, she made the tactical decision to join the massive herd of prospective students for the guided academic walking tour. 

For the next hour, we slowly followed a painfully enthusiastic student ambassador walking backward and pointing out the library, the liberal arts buildings, and the various dining halls. Cassandra wheeled herself near the back of the group, conserving her energy and mentally mapping the concrete pathways, curb cuts, and elevator locations. 

When the tour finally wound its way past a low, squat, heavily-air-conditioned brick building, the guide pointed enthusiastically. "And right here is the Computer Science building, home to our advanced programming labs!"

Cassandra immediately locked her eyes on the entrance. She had the coordinates. 

When the tour eventually circled back and terminated at the student union, the massive crowd began to disperse toward various breakout sessions. Cassandra didn't hesitate. She pivoted her wheelchair sharply. "Breakaway," she ordered quietly. "We are heading back to the CS building."

Casey adjusted her backpack, marching faithfully beside her older sister's wheelchair as we navigated back across the quad. My parents followed, looking slightly bewildered as we officially went off-script.

We pushed through the heavy glass double doors of the Computer Science building, immediately hitting a wall of chilled, aggressively processed air. The lobby was lined with recruitment tables, manned by smiling faculty members handing out glossy pamphlets detailing the department's cutting-edge curriculum. 

Arranged on the tables were several massive desktop computer towers, their heavy CRT monitors humming with static electricity. 

Cassandra wheeled herself up to the closest display, adjusting her glasses as she inspected the screen. Casey leaned in right next to her, staring at the blocky, colorful interface. 

"Is this the new Quantum OS 95?" Casey whispered eagerly. 

"No," Cassandra replied, her voice perfectly flat. "It is Atlas OS/2."

A faculty member leaned over the table, smiling brightly. "That's right! We just completely overhauled the entire department's infrastructure. Every single machine in our student labs is now running Atlas OS/2, networked via a brand new Nexus NetShare topography. It is the absolute pinnacle of stable, 32-bit architecture."

Cassandra offered him a tight, incredibly polite smile. "It certainly is an investment."

She wheeled herself slowly down the line of tables, her processor working overtime. Atlas had successfully convinced the university to commit their entire departmental budget to a platform that was practically already dead. With Quantum OS 95 dropping in a month, betting the farm on Atlas OS/2 was a catastrophic failure of market foresight. 

At the very end of the row of tables, Cassandra paused. Set up on a small pedestal was a thick, beige keyboard with a massive, blocky casing. A small handwritten sign next to it read: *Where it all began!*

It was an Apex 64. 

Cassandra let out a soft breath, reaching out and gently tapping the heavy plastic chassis of the vintage machine. She remembered the simpler times. She remembered sitting in front of a glowing television screen as a kid, watching the blinking cursor of the Apex 64, learning the absolute basics of logic and syntax. It was a beautiful machine. 

But times were changing. The industry was accelerating at a terrifying, exponential rate, and nostalgia wasn't going to secure a career in the coming tech boom. 

"Is something wrong, Cass?" I asked quietly, standing next to her wheelchair. 

Cassandra kept her polite, pleasant expression firmly in place for the benefit of the faculty watching her, but her eyes were cold as she looked at the rows of Atlas OS/2 machines. 

"This department is a museum, David," she murmured, her voice so low only Casey and I could hear it. "They just don't realize it yet."

***

## 1:00 PM — The Dormitory Metric

We left the oppressive chill of the Computer Science building and made our way back across the academic quad to the Student Services center. At precisely 1300 hours, we were sitting in the small, warmly lit office of the George Wythe University Disability Services coordinator. 

The coordinator was a younger woman named Sarah. She was incredibly welcoming, pulling up campus housing blueprints on her desktop computer the moment Cassandra mentioned her desk measurements. 

"We absolutely have fully wheelchair-accessible dorms in the freshman housing blocks," Sarah assured her, turning her monitor so Cassandra could see the floor plan. "The standard suites have two Twin XL beds connecting to a second dorm room via a shared bathroom. In the accessible suites, the bathroom turning radius is expanded, the shower is a roll-in, and the desks are fully modular. We can adjust the desk height before you even arrive on move-in day."

"That is excellent," Cassandra nodded, offering a genuine smile. "I will be bringing a rather large desktop rig. It requires a heavy CRT monitor and a full-tower ATX case, so standard fixed-height dorm furniture is usually a critical failure point."

Sarah laughed, leaning back in her chair. "You're building your own rig? My brother is a computer science major out on the West Coast, and that's all he talks about." She glanced at the door before lowering her voice playfully. "He goes to Northwest Pacific University. His department has actually been beta testing the new Quantum OS 95 for months."

Cassandra sat up slightly straighter, her interest suddenly piquing. "NPU has beta access?"

"Oh, absolutely," Sarah nodded, smiling at the sudden intensity in my sister's eyes. "He said it's completely changing the way they network their labs. In fact, they just got their hands on an early developer build for Quantum's new enterprise networking software. I think they're calling the beta *Project Citadel*. It's supposed to be the 32-bit architecture that will eventually run all the university servers."

"Project Citadel," Cassandra repeated softly, committing the codename to memory. "What will the retail version be called?"

"I think he said Quantum NT 4.0," Sarah replied, shrugging. "I'm not a tech person myself, but he loves it. Which is why I always try to steer our tech-focused students carefully. Between you and me, I know our CS department here at GWU just invested heavily in Atlas OS/2."

"I saw," Cassandra said politely.

Sarah offered a sympathetic smile. "I don't have any say in what the academic departments do with their budgets, but even I know Atlas isn't the future. If you're building an ATX rig to run Quantum OS 95, you might be a little frustrated in our student labs."

Cassandra looked around the small office. The environment at GWU was undeniably inviting. The disability counselor was accommodating, the accessibility infrastructure was perfectly sound, and the campus was physically beautiful. She could very easily see herself living here, navigating the pathways and catching the CCT bus into Vienna. 

But as Casey eagerly pulled out her tape measure to double-check the desk dimensions on the blueprint, Cassandra remained quiet. She wasn't going to make her final decision today. She was going to wait. 

Because once Casey and I finished our summer camps at VDU, our family was flying to Seattle. And Cassandra suddenly needed to see exactly what Northwest Pacific University was doing with Project Citadel.

***

## 2:15 PM — Early Dismissal

We stepped out of the Student Services center and back into the humid Virginia afternoon. The Open House was technically scheduled to run until five o'clock, with more breakout sessions, a student life panel, and an evening mixer in the dining hall. 

Cassandra, however, had seen enough. 

"The tactical objective has been achieved," Cassandra announced, locking her brakes near the campus transit shelter. "We have verified the accessibility tolerances of the housing blocks, mapped the core academic pathways, and assessed the technological infrastructure of the Computer Science department. There is no mathematical reason to remain here for the mixer."

My father looked at her, wiping sweat from his forehead. "Are you sure, Cass? We came all the way up here. We don't have to rush off."

"I have the data I need," Cassandra replied simply. She wasn't angry or disappointed. She was just ruthlessly efficient. Atlas OS/2 was a terminal diagnosis for the department's future, but the disability counselor's intel regarding Northwest Pacific University had just opened an entirely new strategic pathway. She was already mentally moving on to Seattle. "The CCT Gold Line 1 arrives in six minutes. It loops directly through the City of Fairfax."

Mom sighed in relief, clearly thrilled to be skipping a two-hour student panel in an un-air-conditioned lecture hall. "Fairfax it is. I think we've earned a break."

Casey looked down at her meticulously highlighted itinerary, visibly distressed by this sudden deviation from the schedule. "But the student life panel! We haven't investigated the extracurricular clubs!"

"You can investigate the ice cream options in historic downtown Fairfax, Casey," Dad offered smoothly, which instantly neutralized our little sister's objections. 

When the CCT bus pulled up, the ramp lowered, and we piled on. Instead of taking the loop back toward the Vienna Orange Line station, we rode it south into the heart of the City of Fairfax. 

The campus gave way to the historic downtown district, a charming cluster of old brick buildings, wide sidewalks, and sprawling oak trees. We disembarked near the old courthouse and spent the rest of the afternoon acting like a normal family on a summer vacation, completely detached from the high-stress logistics of college hunting.

We found a classic diner with checkered floors and vinyl booths. Dad ordered a massive plate of fries for the table, and Casey spent twenty minutes aggressively debating the mathematical superiority of chocolate milkshakes over vanilla. Cassandra sat by the window, sipping a soda and watching the traffic roll through the historic intersection. 

I sat across from her, dipping a fry in ketchup. "Are you disappointed?" I asked quietly, so Casey wouldn't hear and start another lecture. "About the computers?"

Cassandra looked away from the window, adjusting her glasses. The intense, calculating edge she had carried all morning was gone, replaced by a quiet sense of clarity. 

"No, David," she said softly. "It's better to find out now. A system is only as strong as its foundation. If you build a house on an obsolete architecture, it doesn't matter how nice the dorms are. It's still going to collapse."

She looked back out the window, watching a Capital Transit bus rumble past the diner. 

"We just have to find a school that's building for the future," she added. "Not the past."

***

## 8:00 PM — The Evening Routine

By the time we navigated the CCT bus back to the motel in Vienna, the sun had set, and the oppressive Virginia heat had finally broken into a mild, humid evening. 

We retreated into our adjoining rooms. Dad collapsed onto one of the beds in my parents' room, grabbing the television remote, while Casey immediately unpacked her notebooks onto the small desk in our room, determined to mathematically graph the efficiency of the CCT bus loops we had just taken.

Cassandra wheeled herself to the center of our room, locking her brakes. The grueling transit rides and the long day of navigating the GWU campus had finally taken their toll. Her shoulders slumped slightly, and the sharp, unyielding energy that usually drove her had faded into physical exhaustion.

The adjoining door opened, and Mom stepped through. 

"Alright, Cass," Mom said softly, her tone shifting from the exasperated parent managing a college tour to the focused, quiet cadence she always used for the evening routine. "Let's get you ready for bed."

Casey immediately looked up from her notebooks, dropping her pencil. "I can assist," she volunteered eagerly, hopping off her chair. "I have analyzed the spatial dimensions of the accessible shower stall. If we utilize a pivot transfer—"

"No, Casey," Mom interrupted gently, placing a hand on our little sister's shoulder to stop her forward momentum. "Thank you, sweetheart, but Cassandra needs privacy. And this requires an adult."

Casey frowned, clearly offended that her logistical expertise was being rejected. "But I am part of the support team. I secured the breakfast perimeter. I measured the desk clearance."

"I know you did," Cassandra said, offering Casey a tired, affectionate smile. "And your data collection was flawless. But this isn't logistics, Casey. It's just my ADLs."

Activities of Daily Living. It was the clinical term Cassandra always used for the deeply personal, physically demanding reality of her condition—things like transferring, bathing, and changing clothes. 

Mom wheeled Cassandra into the large, accessible bathroom, pulling the heavy door shut behind them until it clicked, leaving Casey standing in the middle of the room, looking utterly defeated.

I walked over and pulled my eight-year-old sister into a hug. She was stiff for a second, still annoyed, before she finally sighed and leaned against me. 

"It's okay, Case," I told her quietly, resting my chin on top of her head. "You did a great job today. But sometimes, Cassandra just needs the adults to help her."

"It's inefficient," Casey mumbled against my shirt. "A properly integrated support team shouldn't have artificial age restrictions."

I smiled, letting her go so she could return to her transit graphs. But as I sat down on the edge of my Twin XL bed, listening to the muffled sounds of the running shower and Mom's quiet, encouraging voice guiding Cassandra through her transfers, I found myself staring at the closed bathroom door. 

Casey viewed our sister's life as a series of complex logistical equations to be solved. But for the first time, at ten years old, I was starting to recognize the profound physical vulnerability beneath Cassandra's brilliant, armored exterior. She relied on our parents to maintain her independence. 

A quiet thought sparked in the back of my mind. Our parents weren't going to be around forever to help her with her ADLs. What would happen when Cassandra went to college? Or when she got her own apartment? 

*What if it was me?* I thought to myself. *If it was just me and Cassandra... would I be strong enough to step in and help her?*

I didn't know the answer yet. But as I listened to the water running, I realized that I wanted to learn.

***

## 9:30 PM — Lights Out

When the bathroom door finally opened, Cassandra was already dressed in her pajamas. Mom helped her wheel across the carpet and execute a smooth pivot transfer into the nearest queen bed. 

Once Cassandra was settled under the covers, Mom turned off the main overhead light, leaving just the small lamp on the nightstand casting a warm glow across the room.

"Alright, you two," Mom whispered, looking over at Casey and me. "Brush your teeth. It's late."

The accessible room was equipped with two large queen beds. Since Cassandra needed a dedicated bed to ensure she had enough space to adjust her position throughout the night, Casey and I were sharing the other one. 

Once we finished in the bathroom, I climbed into the bed and pulled the heavy hotel comforter up. Casey scrambled in right behind me, immediately cuddling up against my side. For all her intense logistical planning and insistence on being treated like a tactical operative, she was still just an eight-year-old kid who liked sleeping next to her big brother when we were in a strange place.

Mom came back out of the bathroom, picking up a few stray towels. She leaned over Cassandra's bed first, kissing her forehead. "Goodnight, Cass. Call out if you need anything."

"I will," Cassandra promised, her voice thick with exhaustion. 

Mom walked over to our bed next, smoothing Casey's hair and kissing the top of her head. "Goodnight, Casey. Goodnight, David."

"Goodnight, Mom," I whispered. 

Mom walked over to the adjoining door that connected our room to theirs. Instead of shutting it, she carefully pushed it wide open until it clicked against the wall stop. In the event of an emergency, or if Cassandra needed a glass of water or help shifting her weight in the middle of the night, our parents needed to be able to hear her instantly. 

Once Mom retreated into the other room, Dad reached over and turned off the final lamp on their side, leaving our room illuminated only by the faint orange glow of the streetlights filtering through the heavy curtains.

The room was perfectly quiet, except for the hum of the air-conditioning unit under the window. 

Casey shifted against my side, resting her head on my shoulder. 

"David?" Casey whispered, her voice barely carrying across the dark room. 

"Yeah, Case?" 

"The tactical deployment tomorrow is completely different," Casey mumbled sleepily, her eyes already slipping shut. "We aren't doing any university tours."

"I know," I whispered back. 

"We're taking the Orange Line all the way into the District," Casey added, a small, genuine smile creeping onto her face in the dark. "We're going to the National Zoo."

I smiled, pulling the comforter up a little higher over her shoulders. The stress of the GWU infrastructure analysis was officially over. Tomorrow, we just got to be kids again. 

"I know, Case," I whispered. "Go to sleep. We have a lot of animals to see tomorrow."
