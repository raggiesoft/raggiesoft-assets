## 12:30 PM — The Union Station Bottleneck

We woke up the next morning feeling the cumulative ache of a three-day, high-intensity transit deployment. We ate a quiet breakfast in the motel lobby, loaded up our luggage, and boarded the CCT bus one last time to make the short trip back to the Vienna surface station. 

Our departure vector was entirely different from our arrival. 

Because we were leaving the city via Washington Union Station instead of Alexandria, the route required diving into the heart of the District. We rode the Orange Line eastbound, rattling under the city until we reached Metro Center. From the lower-level Blue/Orange platform, we hauled our luggage and Cassandra's wheelchair up the elevators to the upper-level Red Line platform, catching a train heading east. 

When the automated voice finally announced Union Station, Dad looked up at the curved concrete ceiling of the platform with a nostalgic smile. 

"I still remember when they rebranded this place for the 1976 Bicentennial," he mused as we waited for the elevator doors to open. "They called it the National Visitor Center. Put a massive slide show in the main hall. It was a complete disaster."

"It's still a disaster, Dad," Cassandra muttered tightly. 

As soon as the elevator doors opened onto the main concourse, I instantly understood why Cassandra had gone out of her way to route us through the quiet, surface-level Alexandria station two days ago. 

Union Station was massive. It was a cavernous, Beaux-Arts cathedral of echoing marble, swarming with tens of thousands of commuters, tourists, and business travelers. The acoustic reflections off the vaulted ceilings created a deafening, continuous roar of overlapping voices, train announcements, and rolling luggage wheels. 

It hit me like a physical wall. I stopped dead in my tracks, my hands instinctively coming up to cover my ears as my brain desperately tried to filter out the auditory assault. 

Casey wasn't doing much better. She was clinging tightly to the back of Cassandra's wheelchair, her eyes darting frantically around the massive, overwhelming space, her breathing shallow and fast. 

Dad paused, looking back at us. The cheerful nostalgia instantly vanished from his face. He watched me pressing my hands over my ears, and then looked at Casey's white-knuckled grip on the wheelchair. He glanced at Mom, and I saw a silent, heavy look pass between them. Mom's suspicions from the previous night had just been confirmed in broad daylight. There was no denying it anymore. Casey and I were both rapidly approaching sensory overload. 

"Okay, team," Dad said softly, stepping between us and the crowd to act as a physical buffer. "Let's push through to the food court on the lower level. It's a bit more contained."

We retreated to the basement food court, grabbing quick, overpriced meals while waiting for our train. But even sitting down didn't solve Cassandra's primary logistical grievance with the station. 

"This is the fundamental flaw of WAS," Cassandra complained, aggressively stabbing a piece of lettuce in her salad. "At Alexandria, the platform is entirely accessible from the surface. I can roll directly up to the train. Here, the boarding gates are locked. We are forced to wait in the main hall, completely reliant on the ARTS Red Cap service to manually escort us down an employee elevator to the platform. It strips me of my autonomy."

She hated being forced to wait for an escort. She knew exactly which track the 3:50 PM ARTS *The Patriot* was arriving on, and she was more than capable of navigating to it herself, but the architecture of Union Station explicitly prevented her from doing so. 

Eventually, a Red Cap agent arrived, unlocked the gate, and escorted our family down the heavy freight elevator to the damp, diesel-smelling platform below. 

The moment we boarded the quiet, climate-controlled ARTS train, the tension instantly evaporated from the family. Casey and I slumped into our seats, exhausted by the sensory gauntlet of the station. Cassandra locked her wheelchair into the ADA tie-downs, looking visibly relieved to be back in a controlled, predictable environment. 

The train pulled out of Washington, D.C. at 3:50 PM. For the next four hours, we rolled smoothly down the Mid-Atlantic corridor, watching the sprawling suburbs give way to the dense forests and rivers of southern Virginia. 

By the time the train finally pulled into the Newport News (NPN) station at 8:09 PM, it was completely dark outside. We unloaded our bags and piled exhausted into the trusty family station wagon waiting in the parking lot.

## 9:00 PM — The Monitor-Merrimac Detour

The drive from the Newport News station back to our house in Virginia Beach should have been a relatively straightforward shot down Interstate 64. But as Dad merged onto the highway, the local traffic radio station delivered a crushing logistical update. 

"...multi-vehicle collision blocking the eastbound tubes of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel," the radio announcer crackled. "Expect delays in excess of two hours. Motorists are strongly advised to seek alternate routes."

Dad let out a long, heavy sigh and aggressively hit his turn signal, diving off the I-64 ramp and swinging the heavy station wagon onto I-664 instead. 

"We're taking the long way," Dad announced grimly. "Monitor-Merrimac it is."

The Monitor-Merrimac Memorial Bridge-Tunnel was a massive detour. It bypassed the clogged HRBT, but it required swinging in a massive southern arc through Suffolk and Chesapeake before finally cutting back up into Virginia Beach. It easily added forty-five minutes to an already exhausting day. 

In the backseat, the mood rapidly deteriorated. 

Casey was exhausted, overstimulated, and rapidly losing her ability to mask her emotional dysregulation. She was practically vibrating with frantic, nervous energy, kicking the back of Dad's seat. 

"I require Leona," Casey demanded, her voice cracking as she stared into the dark interior of the car. "I need Leona right now."

Leona was a plush lion cub from the massively successful animated movie, *Monarch of the Savannah*, that had dominated theaters the previous year. Casey never slept without it. 

"Casey, sweetie, Leona is packed inside your blue duffel bag," Mom said patiently from the passenger seat, turning around to look at her. "The duffel bag is buried under Cassandra's wheelchair in the trunk. I can't get to her right now. You just have to hold on until we get home."

Casey let out a sharp, frustrated whine, pulling her knees up to her chest and rocking back and forth. The lack of deep pressure and the absence of her comfort item were sending her into a tailspin. 

To make matters worse, as we finally cleared the Monitor-Merrimac tunnel and hit the Virginia Beach city limits, the sky suddenly broke open. 

A violent, coastal thunderstorm slammed into the region. Rain hammered against the windshield so hard that the wipers couldn't keep up. But the rain wasn't the problem. The problem was the lightning. 

A massive, blinding flash of white light strobed across the dark sky, immediately followed by a booming, concussive crack of thunder that rattled the frame of the station wagon. 

Casey screamed, clamping her hands over her ears. 

I wasn't doing much better. The sudden, violent sensory shifts—pitch black to blinding white, dead silence to deafening explosions—terrified me. I ducked down in my seat, pulling my jacket over my head and squeezing my eyes shut. Storms during the day were manageable. Storms at night, where the lightning visually shattered the darkness, felt like a literal assault on my nervous system. 

By the time Dad finally pulled into our driveway, Casey and I were both in a state of absolute panic. 

We barely waited for the car to stop. As soon as Dad threw it in park, I threw my door open, grabbed Casey's hand, and we sprinted through the torrential downpour toward the front door. We burst into the house, completely ignoring Mom yelling at us to grab our bags. I pulled Casey straight into my bedroom so we could hide from the flashing windows. 

It took another twenty minutes for Dad to carry Cassandra up the ramp and for Mom to wrestle the heavy wheelchair out of the trunk in the pouring rain. Once they were inside, Mom quickly helped Cassandra change into her pajamas and get settled into her own bed. 

In my bedroom, another blinding flash of lightning illuminated the walls, followed instantly by a terrifying crash of thunder. 

Casey let out a whimper, clutching her ears. 

We sat huddled together on my floor in the dark, waiting for the flashing to stop.

## 10:00 PM — The Sibling Anchor

The storm wasn't letting up. If anything, the coastal system was intensifying, dragging heavy sheets of rain across the roof of the house. 

I sat huddled on the floor of my bedroom with Casey, our backs pressed firmly against the frame of my bed. I had my jacket pulled up over my head to block out the sudden, blinding flashes of light, and Casey had her hands clamped over her ears, her plush lion cub, Leona, squeezed tightly under her arm. Mom had managed to dig the toy out of the trunk and toss it into my room before rushing back out to help Dad with the luggage. 

Another violent flash strobed across the room, casting long, sharp shadows across my walls. A split second later, the thunder hit—a deep, concussive boom that literally rattled the floorboards under us. 

Casey let out a sharp cry, burying her face into Leona's synthetic fur. 

I couldn't take it anymore. The sensory isolation of being trapped in the dark room with the flashing lights was too much. 

"Come on," I said, grabbing Casey's arm and hauling her to her feet. "We can't stay in here."

She didn't argue. She just gripped Leona and followed me as I carefully opened my bedroom door. 

The hallway was dark, save for the flickering lightning bleeding through the windows. We crept down the corridor, passing our parents' room, and stopped dead in front of Cassandra's doorway. 

The door was cracked open. A warm, yellow light spilled out into the hall from her bedside lamp. 

We hovered nervously in the doorway, peering inside. Cassandra was already in her pajamas, lying propped up in her bed with a massive, heavy-bound textbook resting on her chest. She looked completely, utterly unfazed by the raging thunderstorm outside. The loud crashes of thunder didn't even cause her to flinch. 

She looked up from her book and saw us standing there in the hallway, trembling, wide-eyed, and terrified. 

Cassandra didn't say a word. She didn't ask us what was wrong or tell us to go back to bed. She just let out a quiet sigh, closed her textbook, and reached down. With one smooth motion, she pulled the heavy comforter back, exposing a wide swath of the mattress beside her. 

Casey and I didn't hesitate. We bolted into the room and scrambled up onto the mattress, diving completely under the heavy blankets to escape the flashing lights. 

I wrapped my arms around Casey, pulling her against me to provide the tight, deep pressure squeeze she desperately needed to regulate her nervous system. Cassandra reached over and threw the heavy comforter over both of us, sealing out the visual flashes of the storm and anchoring us firmly to the bed. 

The storm raged outside for another two hours, rattling the windowpanes and hammering the roof with torrential rain. But buried under the blankets, pressed tightly against my siblings, the chaotic, terrifying sensory overload of the world finally faded away.
