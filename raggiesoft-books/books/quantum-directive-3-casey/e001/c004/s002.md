The drive from the Newport News station back to our house in Virginia Beach should have been a relatively straightforward shot down Interstate 64. But as Dad merged onto the highway, the local traffic radio station delivered a crushing logistical update. 

"...multi-vehicle collision blocking the eastbound tubes of the Hampton Roads Bridge-Tunnel," the radio announcer crackled. "Expect delays in excess of two hours. Motorists are strongly advised to seek alternate routes."

Dad let out a long, heavy sigh and aggressively hit his turn signal, diving off the I-64 ramp and swinging the heavy station wagon onto I-664 instead. 

"We're taking the long way," Dad announced grimly. "Monitor-Merrimac it is."

The Monitor-Merrimac Memorial Bridge-Tunnel was a massive detour. It bypassed the clogged HRBT, but it required swinging in a massive southern arc through Suffolk and Chesapeake before finally cutting back up into Virginia Beach. It easily added forty-five minutes to an already exhausting day. 

In the backseat, the mood rapidly deteriorated. 

Casey was exhausted, overstimulated, and rapidly losing her ability to mask her emotional dysregulation. She was practically vibrating with frantic, nervous energy, kicking the back of Dad's seat. 

"I require Leona," Casey demanded, her voice cracking as she stared into the dark interior of the car. "I need Leona right now."

Leona was a plush lion cub from the massively successful animated movie, *Monarch of the Savannah*, that had dominated theaters the previous year. Casey never slept without it. 

"Casey, sweetie, Leona is packed inside your blue duffel bag," Mom said patiently from the passenger seat, turning around to look at her. "The duffel bag is buried under Cassandra's wheelchair in the trunk. I can't get to her right now. You just have to hold on until we get home."

Casey let out a sharp, frustrated whine, pulling her knees up to her chest and rocking back and forth. The lack of deep pressure and the absence of her comfort item were sending her into a tailspin. 

To make matters worse, as we finally cleared the Monitor-Merrimac tunnel and hit the Virginia Beach city limits, the sky suddenly broke open. 

A violent, coastal thunderstorm slammed into the region. Rain hammered against the windshield so hard that the wipers couldn't keep up. But the rain wasn't the problem. The problem was the lightning. 

A massive, blinding flash of white light strobed across the dark sky, immediately followed by a booming, concussive crack of thunder that rattled the frame of the station wagon. 

Casey screamed, clamping her hands over her ears. 

I wasn't doing much better. The sudden, violent sensory shifts—pitch black to blinding white, dead silence to deafening explosions—terrified me. I ducked down in my seat, pulling my jacket over my head and squeezing my eyes shut. Storms during the day were manageable. Storms at night, where the lightning visually shattered the darkness, felt like a literal assault on my nervous system. 

By the time Dad finally pulled into our driveway, Casey and I were both in a state of absolute panic. 

We barely waited for the car to stop. As soon as Dad threw it in park, I threw my door open, grabbed Casey's hand, and we sprinted through the torrential downpour toward the front door. We burst into the house, completely ignoring Mom yelling at us to grab our bags. I pulled Casey straight into my bedroom so we could hide from the flashing windows. 

It took another twenty minutes for Dad to carry Cassandra up the ramp and for Mom to wrestle the heavy wheelchair out of the trunk in the pouring rain. Once they were inside, Mom quickly helped Cassandra change into her pajamas and get settled into her own bed. 

In my bedroom, another blinding flash of lightning illuminated the walls, followed instantly by a terrifying crash of thunder. 

Casey let out a whimper, clutching her ears. 

We sat huddled together on my floor in the dark, waiting for the flashing to stop.
