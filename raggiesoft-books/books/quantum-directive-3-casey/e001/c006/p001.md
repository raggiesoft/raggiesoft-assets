## 9:00 AM — The Junior Cohort

The VDU Coastal Ecology Camp officially commenced on a sweltering Monday morning in late June. 

Mom drove the three of us from Virginia Beach into Norfolk, pulling the station wagon into the sprawling, heavily shaded campus of Virginia Dominion University. I grabbed our shared cooler bag from the backseat while Mom unlatched Cassandra's wheelchair from the trunk. 

"Have a good first day, you two," Mom smiled, leaning down to give Casey a hug. "David, stick with your sister. Remember your accommodations."

"I know," I said, slinging the strap of the cooler over my shoulder. 

While Casey and I headed toward the massive university recreation center for camp orientation, Mom and Cassandra headed in the opposite direction. Cassandra had attended academic camps here when she was younger, but today she had a specific, tactical objective. VDU was built entirely at sea level—making it one of the flattest, most wheelchair-friendly campuses on the East Coast. Cassandra wanted to run a real-world stress test on the campus infrastructure to see exactly how easily she could navigate it without assistance. 

While plotting their route through the student union, they unexpectedly stumbled across a massive university career fair. Since they were already there, Cassandra decided they might as well roll through the exhibit hall and see what kind of technology companies were recruiting locally. 

But for Casey and me, our focus was strictly on the Junior Cohort. 

The recreation center gymnasium was an absolute sensory nightmare. Dozens of kids between the ages of eight and ten were running around, screaming, bouncing basketballs, and echoing loudly off the polished wooden floors. 

Casey immediately shrank back against my side, her hands coming up to cover her ears. She hated unstructured social environments. 

I didn't say a word. I just wrapped my arm tightly around her shoulders, shielding her from the chaotic crowd, and guided her toward the far wall of the gymnasium, away from the screaming kids. We sat down on the bottom row of the wooden bleachers, our shoulders pressed together, creating our own isolated bubble of order amidst the chaos. 

A camp counselor with a bright yellow VDU polo shirt eventually blew a whistle, corralled us into a circle, and forced everyone to play a grueling, twenty-minute icebreaker game involving throwing a tennis ball and shouting our favorite colors. 

Casey and I participated with absolute, robotic minimalism. When the ball was thrown to Casey, she caught it, deadpanned, "Teal," and instantly threw it to me. I caught it, said, "Black," and threw it to the counselor. 

We didn't care about the icebreakers. We were just waiting for the science. 

By noon, we had survived the morning orientation and a preliminary lecture on coastal tidal patterns. The camp broke for lunch, releasing us out onto the grassy campus quad. 

Casey and I immediately retreated to the shade of a massive oak tree, far away from the loud, chaotic clumps of kids trading snacks. I unzipped the shared cooler bag and pulled out our lunch. 

Mom had packed our exact, highly-specific preferences: two peanut butter and jelly sandwiches on honey wheat bread (crusts meticulously removed), a shared family-size bag of potato chips, and two cold 20-ounce bottles of soda. 

"The instructional pacing of the morning lecture was acceptable," Casey noted, taking a precise, measured bite of her sandwich. "But the social integration protocols are highly inefficient."

"Icebreakers are a waste of time," I agreed, popping the cap off my soda. "But we only have to deal with the classroom stuff until Friday. Then we get to deploy."

Casey nodded, a rare smile appearing on her face. Field trips were always on Fridays. This week, we were scheduled to take the VDU passenger vans across the bay to Kiptopeake State Park to analyze coastal erosion. 

"I am also highly anticipating Week Four," Casey said, chewing thoughtfully. "The camp director confirmed we have been granted access to the soft opening of the Owls Creek Marsh Pavilion at the Dominion Marine Science Museum. They are using our camp cohort to stress-test the new building before it opens to the general public next year."

"And the stingrays," I reminded her. 

Casey's eyes lit up. The *Hooray for Rays* stingray touch tank was the brand new, highly publicized flagship exhibit in the main building. "Yes. The tactile interaction with cartilaginous fish will be fascinating."

I smiled, leaning back against the trunk of the oak tree. The social aspects of camp were exhausting, but as long as we were partnered up, we could endure anything. We had our perfectly structured lunch, we had a clear itinerary for the week, and we had the science to look forward to.

## 1:00 PM — The Ecology Lab

After we finished our meticulously packed lunch under the oak tree, a counselor blew a whistle, signaling the end of free time. The Junior Cohort was herded out of the humid afternoon heat and into one of VDU's sprawling undergraduate science buildings. 

The air-conditioning hit us like a physical wall, immediately bringing my sensory stress levels down. We filed into a massive biology lab outfitted with long, black soapstone tables, stainless steel sinks, and rows of high-powered microscopes. 

"Alright, junior ecologists!" the head counselor announced, clapping his hands together. "For our afternoon session, we're going to practice cataloging marine specimens! Find your assigned partner and grab a tray from the front!"

Instantly, the room devolved into chaos. Kids were shouting, fighting over who got to be partners, and scrambling to grab the best-looking trays of shells, dried starfish, and sand dollars. 

Casey didn't even flinch. She just turned, looked at me, and nodded. 

Because Mom and Dad had filed the formal accommodation, we didn't have to participate in the frantic partner-selection ritual. I calmly walked to the front of the room, grabbed a specimen tray while the other kids were distracted, and carried it back to a quiet corner station at the very back of the lab. 

As soon as I set the tray down, we went to work. 

The other kids were treating the exercise like a toy box, loudly clattering the shells together and spilling sand all over the expensive soapstone tables. We, however, treated the tray like a crime scene. Because I had severe dysgraphia—meaning my handwriting was completely illegible—Casey was in charge of all data entry. She opened my spiral notebook, her pencil poised with perfect, rigid posture. 

I carefully removed a pair of plastic tweezers from the supply kit and began meticulously sorting the specimens into perfectly straight, symmetrical grids on the soapstone based on phylum and size. 

"Bivalvia," I announced, holding up a small, ridged shell and cross-referencing it with the laminated identification chart. "*Crassostrea virginica*." 

A kid at the next table over paused from trying to balance a starfish on his nose and stared at me like I had just spoken alien code. 

"Estimated age based on calcification?" Casey asked, not even looking up from her notebook as she wrote the Latin name down in flawless, uniform script. 

"Three years," I confirmed, placing it exactly in the center of the first grid. "Next."

For the next two hours, we completely tuned out the noise of the room. We operated like a highly efficient, closed-loop machine. I handled the tactile sorting and physical categorization, while Casey handled the data entry. We communicated exclusively in taxonomic Latin, much to the confusion of the nine-year-olds sitting around us. 

We were so hyper-fixated on the task that we didn't even notice the head counselor walking up behind us. 

"Whoa," the counselor whispered, staring down at our workstation. 

I tensed up, dropping my tweezers, afraid we were in trouble. Casey froze, her pencil hovering over the notebook. 

Our station was immaculate. The specimens were arranged in flawless geometric patterns. Casey's notebook was filled with perfectly aligned columns of taxonomic data. Meanwhile, the table next to us was covered in spilled water. 

"You guys did all this?" the counselor asked, sounding genuinely bewildered. "You didn't even need my help identifying the *Crassostrea virginica*?"

"The *Crassostrea virginica*'s ridge patterns are statistically distinct from the *Mercenaria mercenaria* population," Casey explained, completely deadpan. "The identification was elementary."

The counselor blinked, looking back and forth between us. He had clearly never dealt with neurodivergent, highly analytical children before. He slowly nodded, scribbled something down on his clipboard, and backed away. "Keep up the good work, you two."

"Social interaction concluded," I muttered, picking my tweezers back up. 

"Agreed," Casey said, seamlessly returning her focus to the notebook. "Now, hand me the *Geukensia demissa*."

## 4:00 PM — The Catalyst

When camp officially concluded for the day, Mom and Cassandra were waiting for us at the edge of the sprawling university quad. 

Instead of heading straight back to the car to fight through the punishing rush hour traffic across the Southside, Mom steered us toward the massive VDU student union. We grabbed dinner at one of the quick-service food counters, sliding into a large vinyl booth in the back of the dining hall. 

I was exhausted. My social battery was completely drained, and all I wanted to do was stare at the wall. But Casey was operating on high alert. Her eyes were locked onto Cassandra. 

Cassandra was radiating a distinct, electric energy. She was trying to look nonchalant while peeling the foil off her sandwich, but there was an unmistakable, deeply smug smirk playing at the corners of her mouth. 

"You are suppressing highly sensitive information," Casey declared, pointing a french fry directly at our older sister. "Your micro-expressions indicate a successful tactical maneuver."

Mom laughed, taking a sip of her iced tea. "She's right, Cassandra. You might as well tell them. You've been practically vibrating for the last two hours."

Cassandra carefully set her sandwich down. The smug smirk finally broke into a wide, triumphant grin. 

"Quantum Corporation was at the career fair today," she said, her voice dropping to an intense, conspiratorial whisper. 

I blinked, my exhaustion momentarily forgotten. Quantum Corporation was the undisputed titan of the technology industry. They didn't typically waste their time recruiting at regional East Coast universities; they usually poached their talent straight out of Northwest Pacific University in Seattle or the Ivy League. 

"They were running a very small, highly selective booth," Cassandra continued, her eyes gleaming. "Looking for early-access talent. I rolled right up to the lead recruiter and started asking him deeply technical questions about the memory architecture in Quantum OS 95."

"Did he take you seriously?" I asked. I knew how dismissive adults could be when dealing with teenagers, let alone a teenage girl in a wheelchair. 

"He tried to brush me off at first," Cassandra said, her grin widening. "He asked if I even knew how to write a basic batch script. So, I started speaking fluent Q32API."

I grinned. *Quantum 32 API*. It was the absolute bleeding-edge architecture for Quantum's upcoming 32-bit operating systems. 

"I explained that I've been running Quantum OS for Workgroups 3.11 on my machine at home," Cassandra told us, leaning across the table. "Instead of standard 3.1. That means I have the Q32s extensions installed, which allows me to bypass the 16-bit limitations and write highly optimized, enterprise-level C++ code directly into the kernel."

"What did the recruiter do?" Casey asked, captivated. 

"His jaw practically hit the floor," Mom chuckled proudly. "He actually called his manager over from the other side of the booth just to listen to her talk."

"They were completely blown away," Cassandra said, leaning back in her chair and crossing her arms. "The manager started talking to me like I was an industry veteran. He asked how many years of professional enterprise experience I had, and requested my resume so they could fast-track me for a senior developer position. When I told them I was only seventeen and about to start my senior year of high school, they literally couldn't believe it."

"They thought you were already a software engineer?" I asked, my eyes wide. 

"They said I knew more about their own kernel architecture than half the people currently working in Redmond," Cassandra smirked. "Once they recovered from the shock that I was still in high school, they pivoted immediately."

Cassandra reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a heavy, glossy black envelope embossed with a silver Quantum Corporation logo. She slid it across the table. 

"They officially invited me to submit an entry for the Quantum Catalyst Initiative," she said, tapping the envelope. 

I stared at the silver logo. I had read about the Catalyst Initiative on early internet message boards. It was a brutal, uncompromising national coding challenge open exclusively to high school juniors and seniors. 

"There's only one national winner," I said quietly, looking up at her. 

"I know," Cassandra said, her voice entirely devoid of doubt. 

The stakes were astronomical. The sole winner of the Catalyst Initiative received the Vanguard Scholarship—a complete, all-expenses-paid full ride to Northwest Pacific University, a paid summer internship at the Quantum campus in Redmond, and a direct, guaranteed pipeline into the company upon graduation. 

"I have until March to write a program that pushes the Q32API architecture to its absolute breaking point," Cassandra said, looking around the table at us. Her eyes were burning with that familiar, intense neurodivergent hyperfixation. "And when I win, we are officially moving to Seattle."

Casey looked at the envelope, then looked up at Cassandra. "Your probability of failure is statistically zero."

Cassandra smiled. "I know."

## 6:00 PM — The Assistant Manager

The rush hour traffic on Interstate 264 was brutal, turning the drive from Norfolk back to Virginia Beach into a crawling, exhaust-choked slog. Getting off the interstate was even worse. The intersection of Independence Boulevard and Bonney Road was a gridlocked nightmare of idling cars and blaring horns. 

By the time Mom finally navigated through the chaos and pulled the station wagon into the parking lot of the Bonney Road Rocket Burger, my social battery was completely dead. I pressed my forehead against the cool glass of the window, perfectly content to stay in the car. 

Dad was just walking out the front doors, adjusting his blue short-sleeved manager shirt. He worked long, exhausting hours as the assistant manager of the fast-food restaurant, often taking on extra shifts to help cover the medical bills for Cassandra's wheelchair maintenance and my and Casey's endless array of sensory accommodations. 

He spotted the station wagon, smiled, and walked over to the passenger side window. Mom rolled it down. 

"How was the first day of camp?" Dad asked, leaning down to look past Mom and into the backseat. "Did David and Casey survive the junior cohort?"

"The instructional pacing was adequate, but the unstructured social interaction was highly inefficient," Casey reported from the backseat, her voice completely flat. 

"We logged *Crassostrea virginica* specimens," I added tiredly. "Casey did the data entry."

"That sounds like a successful deployment to me," Dad chuckled. He looked over at Cassandra, who was sitting in the front passenger seat. "And how was the stress test on the VDU campus infrastructure? Was it as flat as you remembered?"

"It was completely compliant," Cassandra said. But she wasn't looking at him. She was staring straight ahead, holding the glossy black envelope in her lap. 

Dad paused, noticing the sudden shift in the air. He looked at Mom. Mom just smiled, tapping her fingers lightly against the steering wheel. 

"Okay," Dad said, leaning a little closer to the window. "What happened?"

Cassandra took a deep breath, turned, and handed the envelope out the window to him. 

Dad took it, his brow furrowing as he looked down at the embossed silver logo. "Quantum Corporation? What is this?"

"They were running a recruiting booth at the student union," Cassandra explained, her voice steady but vibrating with that same electric intensity she had in the dining hall. "I talked to the manager. I broke down the Q32API architecture for him and explained how I'm running Quantum OS for Workgroups 3.11 to write optimized C++ kernel code."

Dad stared at her, then looked back down at the envelope. "They gave you this?"

"It's an invitation to the Quantum Catalyst Initiative," Mom said softly. "It's a national coding challenge. The winner gets a full-ride scholarship to Northwest Pacific University in Seattle and a guaranteed job at Quantum."

Dad went completely still. He looked down at his blue Rocket Burger uniform, the faint smell of fryer grease clinging to his clothes. He worked fifty hours a week managing teenagers and dealing with angry customers just to keep the lights on and the medical bills paid. He knew exactly what an opportunity like this meant. It was an escape velocity vector. It was a golden ticket out of financial instability and straight into the upper echelon of the global economy. 

"They thought she was an industry professional," I piped up from the back. "They didn't believe she was still in high school."

Dad looked back up at Cassandra. His eyes were wide, shining with a mixture of absolute awe and fierce, protective pride. 

"Seattle," Dad breathed. 

"Seattle," Cassandra nodded. "If I win, I have to move to the Pacific Northwest."

Dad didn't hesitate. He didn't bring up the logistics, or the three-thousand-mile distance, or how hard it would be to transport her wheelchair across the country. He just gripped the envelope tightly and smiled. 

"If the biggest technology company in the world wants my daughter," Dad said, his voice thick with emotion, "then we are going to make it happen. What do you need?"

"I need time," Cassandra said, her eyes locking onto his. "And I need my computer."

Dad reached through the window and squeezed her shoulder. "You've got it. We clear the schedule. You write the code. And we get you to Seattle."
