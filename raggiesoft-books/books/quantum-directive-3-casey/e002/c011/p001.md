## 1:30 PM — Burton Station Road

The calendar eventually surrendered.

On the afternoon of December 17th, the moment my final exam at the community college concluded, my mother was waiting for me in the parking lot. But Wendy Vance didn't drive us back to her house. She bypassed the highway exits for the main public terminal of Norfolk International Airport and instead navigated her standard sedan down Burton Station Road, pulling up to the discrete, heavily gated General Aviation terminal.

I sat in the passenger seat, staring through the chain-link fence at the private tarmac. With Kristine’s toxic surveillance permanently eradicated from my life, Cassandra had absolutely zero reason to hide her leverage. She had completely abandoned the commercial charade.

A sleek, twin-engine corporate jet touched down and taxied directly toward our gated perimeter.

When the cabin door opened and the hydraulic accessible ramp deployed, Casey practically bounced down to the tarmac. She was clutching her duffel bag, her eyes wide with absolute, unfiltered awe. It was her first time ever flying on a private corporate jet, and the sheer scale of the luxury had left my seventeen-year-old sister completely stunned.

Cassandra wheeled herself down the ramp right behind her, looking entirely composed and unbothered, her heavy messenger bag resting on her lap.

I jumped out of Mom's car and met them at the gate. Casey collided with me, wrapping her arms fiercely around my neck for a massive, deep-pressure hug. The physical contact sent a shockwave of relief through my system. The twenty-one days of isolation were officially over.

"This beats commercial First Class by a mile," Casey rasped into my shoulder, vibrating with excitement.

"Quantum's logistics desk actually wanted to route our flight path into PVG over in Chesapeake," Cassandra noted casually, rolling up to the car and offering Mom a warm, familiar smile. "But I told them ORF was vastly superior. PVG is too far of a drive for you, Mom, especially with the High Rise Bridge traffic. Burton Station Road is much closer to home."

"Well, I appreciate the executive override," Mom laughed, helping us load the bags into the trunk.

We drove back to Virginia Beach, the east-coast consolidation of our pack finally underway. But the perimeter wasn't quite complete yet.

## 2:00 PM — The Flight Data

The drive back to Virginia Beach operated on a completely different frequency than the tense, quiet car rides of November.

With Mom navigating the afternoon traffic and Cassandra sitting comfortably in the front passenger seat, the backseat belonged entirely to me and Casey. The moment the car doors closed, Casey leaned heavily against my shoulder, seeking her baseline physical grounding while simultaneously offloading a massive stream of rapid-fire data.

She was practically vibrating with excitement, her hands animating every single detail of the cross-country flight.

"David, you cannot even comprehend the logistical efficiency," Casey began, her voice slightly raspy but moving at lightning speed. "On Thanksgiving, we had to wait in that massive TSA line, take our shoes off, and sit at the gate with hundreds of people. The ambient noise alone was exhausting. But today? We just rolled straight up to the plane. There was zero friction."

I laughed, wrapping my arm around her and pulling her closer. "Zero friction sounds exactly like a Cass operation."

"It's entirely different architecture inside, too," Casey continued, her dark eyes wide as she twisted slightly to look at me. "Commercial First Class is just wider seats in rows. But the corporate jet has these massive, swiveling leather captain's chairs. You can actually turn around and face a mahogany table. Cass had her laptop out, doing Quantum server reviews, and I had an entire couch to myself in the back!"

From the front seat, Cassandra glanced in the rearview mirror, offering a fond, satisfied smile as she listened to her younger sister break down the executive perks.

"And the cabin pressure," Casey added, tapping my arm to emphasize the structural difference. "It's pressurized to a much lower altitude than a standard commercial airliner. My ears didn't pop once. There were no overhead announcements interrupting us, no strangers bumping into the armrests. Just total, uninterrupted quiet all the way across the country."

"Did they at least give you those tiny bags of pretzels?" I joked, leaning my head against hers.

Casey scoffed playfully, waving the idea away. "David, there was a dedicated flight attendant. For just the two of us. She brought out actual, hot catering on ceramic plates. I had a perfectly grilled chicken wrap and fresh fruit while we flew over the Rocky Mountains. It was flawless."

I just sat there, smiling and absorbing her sheer, unfiltered joy. Hearing her completely dissect the mechanical and hospitality differences between the two flights was the exact anchor my system had been starving for. She wasn't carrying an ounce of the fear or hesitation that Kristine had forced into our environment just three weeks prior.

"Well, get used to the layout, Case," Cassandra called back from the front seat, her tone completely matter-of-fact. "Because when we fly back to Washington on Monday, all four of us are getting on that exact same plane."

Casey gasped, grabbing my hand and squeezing it tightly. "You and Liz are going to love it," she promised fiercely. "It's the ultimate perimeter."

## 7:45 PM — The Honorary Sister

By the time the winter sun had fully set over Virginia Beach, the anticipation in Mom's living room had reached a fever pitch.

I was sitting on the sofa with Casey tucked securely against my side. We were watching a movie, but my internal clock was violently counting down the seconds. The twenty-one-day bridge was functionally over, but my system wouldn't fully settle until the final piece of the perimeter was physically in the room.

Suddenly, a double set of headlights swept across the living room blinds.

"She's here," I breathed, my heart hammering against my ribs.

I practically bolted off the cushions. Under any other circumstance, abruptly breaking my physical proximity without warning would have sent Casey’s processor into an immediate tailspin. She relied on me as her primary anchor, and losing that deep-pressure contact usually triggered a wave of intense distress.

But Liz Carter was the single, absolute exception to the rule.

Liz was the only person on the planet who could pull my attention entirely away from Casey without causing my little sister to panic. Because Liz wasn't a threat to the pack—she *was* the pack. Casey didn't view Liz as someone taking her brother away; she viewed Liz as an extension of our safe space. Casey simply smiled, pulling her knees to her chest as I rushed for the front door.

I threw the door open and sprinted down the concrete steps.

Liz put her parents' sedan into park just as the heavy air brakes of a rented moving truck hissed loudly behind her, signaling that her mother and father had successfully completed the convoy from the Shenandoah Valley.

Liz threw her car door open and ran across the driveway. We collided in the cold night air, her arms wrapping fiercely around my neck, pulling me into a breathless, desperate hug. The sheer relief of feeling her in my arms sent a massive, regulating shockwave through my entire system.

"I've got you," Liz whispered fiercely into my ear, burying her face in my shoulder. "I told you I was coming."

"I missed you so much," I choked out, refusing to let her go.

When we finally pulled apart, the rest of the family was waiting on the porch. The dynamic was seamless. Even though Liz shared absolutely zero blood relation with us, the Vance family treated her exactly like a sister.

Liz jogged up the steps and immediately knelt down to wrap Casey in a massive hug, which Casey happily returned. Cassandra wheeled forward, offering Liz a warm, deeply affectionate embrace, while my mother walked down the steps to greet Mr. and Mrs. Carter with open arms.

The rest of the evening was a blur of joyous, insulated family time. We hauled a small mountain of delivery pizza boxes into the kitchen to feed both families. The Carters were incredibly supportive, treating Liz's cross-country move not as a disruption, but as the natural, inevitable evolution of our lives.

As we all crowded into the living room with our paper plates, the layout of the pack naturally engaged. I sat on the floor, my back resting against the front of the sofa. Casey immediately sat down on my left side, leaning her weight against my ribs, while Liz sat on my right, resting her head on my shoulder.

I was perfectly sandwiched between my favorite girl and my best friend, eating a slice of pizza while listening to Cassandra and Mr. Carter discuss the logistics of the moving truck.

The perimeter was locked. My system was flawless. For the first time in what felt like a lifetime, I had absolutely everything I needed right in the palm of my hand.

## 9:15 PM — The Moving Logistics

With the pizza boxes cleared away, the conversation naturally shifted to the sheer mechanics of hauling Liz's life across the country. Mr. Carter pulled out a notepad, ready to outline the driving shifts for the rental truck, but Cassandra raised a hand, smoothly intercepting the planning phase.

"You don't need to drive that truck to Washington," Cassandra stated, her executive tone cutting through the logistical chatter. "I have already retained a bonded, commercial moving service. They will be here tomorrow morning to take possession of the cargo. They are hauling everything to a climate-controlled storage facility I secured near the Federal Way apartment."

Liz blinked, stunned by the frictionless solution. "Cass, you didn't have to do that."

"The apartment is a two-bedroom layout, Liz. We don't have the square footage to absorb a truckload of furniture right now," Cassandra reasoned pragmatically. "Quantum's network handled the contracting. It is already paid for."

With the immediate transit logistics resolved, the tone in the living room subtly shifted. The adults—Mom, Tom, and the Carters—were all sitting together, and Cassandra decided it was time to officially close out the operational debrief from the holiday.

Cassandra didn't raise her voice, but she commanded the absolute attention of the room as she laid out exactly what had happened on Thanksgiving Day. Being mindful of the younger siblings in the room, she kept her language clinical and precise, summarizing the deeply inappropriate, territorial display Kristine had orchestrated in the kitchen. She explained the sheer hostility of the boundary violation, how Kristine had intentionally forced a minor into a highly distressing situation just to assert dominance over the apartment.

As Cassandra spoke, I felt a heavy, residual spike of guilt hit my chest. I looked down at the floor, my shoulders slumping as the memory of the extraction washed over me.

Liz intercepted the guilt instantly.

She shifted on the floor, wrapping her arms securely around my neck, and immediately pulled Casey into the embrace from my other side. Casey didn't hesitate, burying her face against my shoulder as Liz locked us into a tight, three-way hug.

"None of that was your fault, David," Liz whispered fiercely, her voice radiating absolute certainty.

"She's right," Casey added softly, her cheek pressed against my collarbone. "I was never mad at you. I was just scared of her. But you're safe now."

The absolute, unconditional forgiveness from the two women I loved most in the world completely pulverized the lingering shame.

By the time ten o'clock rolled around, the travel exhaustion had finally caught up to all of us. Because my childhood bedroom and the room Casey had grown up in were both located on the second floor of the house, the stairs presented a hard, physical boundary. Cassandra’s severe 1999 axial load injury meant she couldn't navigate the staircase.

"I'm claiming the sofa tonight," Cassandra announced, locking the brakes on her manual chair near the coffee table. "You three go get some sleep."

I leaned down, wrapping my arms around my older sister for a long, grounding hug. Liz and Casey followed suit, exchanging warm, fiercely affectionate goodnights before the three of us headed for the stairs.

We walked down the familiar upstairs hallway, stopping right between our two bedroom doors.

Casey looked up at me, her dark eyes heavy with sleep but entirely free of the anxiety that had plagued her in November. We exchanged one last, deep-pressure hug.

"Goodnight, David. Goodnight, Liz," Casey smiled, before slipping into her childhood bedroom and softly clicking the door shut.

Liz took my hand, her fingers lacing naturally through mine, and followed me into my room.

I shut the door, enclosing us in the quiet, absolute safety of my childhood sanctuary. It was the first time I was going to share a bed with anyone since the extraction, and the fact that it was my childhood best friend completely eradicated any lingering nervous tension.

The transition into the space was seamless. Because we had spent virtually our entire lives in each other's orbits, there was zero hesitation or awkwardness. We moved around the room comfortably, getting undressed in front of each other just like we had countless times before, the vulnerability entirely natural.

Liz pulled her t-shirt over her head, her eyes catching mine across the dim room. She paused, reading the quiet, shifting frequency of my baseline.

"How are you feeling?" Liz asked softly, stepping closer.

I swallowed hard, looking down at her. "I'm okay. I just... I really missed certain things we did last summer."

Liz’s expression softened into a warm, profoundly understanding smile. She knew exactly what I meant. The deep, physical intimacy we had established before the fall semester had been a massive anchor for my system, and I had been entirely starved of that unconditional connection.

She stepped directly into my space, resting her hands gently against my chest. "I'm still on my birth control, David," she murmured, her thumb lightly tracing my collarbone. "We have all the time in the world. We can go exactly at your pace."

I nodded, a massive wave of relief and overwhelming affection crashing over me as I pulled her into a tight, grounding embrace. "My pace is right now."

Liz wrapped her arms around my torso, her cheek resting against my bare chest. She didn't press for a kiss—we had never needed that specific physical mechanic to translate how we felt. Instead, she shifted just enough to glance over my shoulder toward the second-floor window.

"Show me," Liz whispered softly, her fingers tracing a light, soothing pattern down my spine. "You haven't talked about the sky in months."

Kristine had hated my astronomy hyperfixation, constantly shutting me down or rolling her eyes whenever I tried to explain orbital mechanics or constellation shifts. She had actively trained me to repress it. But Liz was deliberately pulling it back out, inviting my true baseline back into our shared space.

I turned my head, following her gaze out the front window.

From the second floor, looking out over the intersection of Pope and Delaney, the window faced directly southeast. The massive tree in the front yard had shed all its leaves for the winter, its bare branches acting like a dark silhouette framing the crisp, cloud-speckled night sky. The temperature outside had dropped into the low forties, but inside the room, anchored entirely by Liz's proximity, the cold didn't exist.

"Orion," I murmured, the familiar, comforting data points easily flooding back into my processor as I looked at the southeast sky. "Right through the center branches. The three stars in the belt are perfectly aligned tonight. And just below it, you can barely see the faint glow... that's the Orion Nebula."

"It's beautiful," Liz said, her voice a warm, steady hum against my collarbone.

She pulled me down onto the mattress, the heavy winter blankets wrapping securely around us. As I settled into the familiar, deeply regulating weight of her body against mine, the astronomy data quietly faded into the background. I didn't need to look out the window anymore. The only thing left to process was the profound, absolute safety of my best friend, the quiet rustle of the sheets, and the undeniable reality that the isolation was finally over.
