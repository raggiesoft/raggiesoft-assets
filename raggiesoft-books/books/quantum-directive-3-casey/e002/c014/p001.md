## 8:00 AM — Pantry Logistics

The heavy wool blankets of the king-sized bed had created an absolutely flawless thermal envelope.

When I finally blinked my eyes open, the digital alarm clock on Cassandra's nightstand was completely blank. The ambient, low-level hum of the apartment’s HVAC system was entirely absent, leaving the room blanketed in a heavy, insulated silence.

I carefully shifted my weight. Casey was still curled up against my side, her breathing deep and even, while Liz was softly snoring against my shoulder. Cassandra was awake, her head propped up on a couple of pillows, quietly watching the gray morning light filter through the bedroom blinds.

"Good morning," Cass whispered, her executive baseline already calmly assessing the situation. "The grid is still down."

I gently untangled my arm from Casey, careful not to break her physical grounding too abruptly, and slipped out from under the covers. The air in the apartment had grown noticeably chillier overnight without the heater running. I walked over to the bedroom window and pulled back the blinds.

The torrential, violent downpour from last night had burned itself out, leaving behind a steady, misty Pacific Northwest drizzle. Down the street, the cause of the blackout was immediately obvious. Half a dozen utility trucks, their amber lights flashing steadily in the gray morning, were clustered around a scorched utility pole. Linemen in bright yellow rain slickers were up in bucket trucks, physically wrestling with the blackened, blown-out remains of a massive transformer.

"Utility company is already on it," I reported quietly, letting the blinds fall back into place. "Looks like they're replacing the whole transformer."

"Then we are operating on emergency reserves for the morning," Cassandra stated, sitting up and stretching her arms. "Which means strict thermal discipline for the kitchen."

By the time Liz and Casey started to stir, the apartment's operational protocols had been fully established. Because the ambient temperature inside was dropping, there was absolutely no reason to change out of our warm, oversized flannel pajamas.

Casey rubbed her eyes, looking around the massive bed. She didn't have a meltdown about the change in routine. The deep-pressure sleep she had gotten while sandwiched between me and Liz had completely stabilized her processor. She was perfectly calm.

"Is the power still out?" Casey asked, pulling a blanket around her shoulders.

"It is," Cassandra nodded, sliding smoothly from the edge of the mattress into her manual chair. "Which brings us to rule number one for the day: nobody opens the refrigerator. Every time that door opens, we lose the cold envelope, and I refuse to let our groceries spoil. We are strictly utilizing dry assets this morning."

We all shuffled out of the master bedroom and into the quiet, dim living room.

Breakfast became an exercise in pantry logistics. Liz and I rummaged through the cupboards, pulling out boxes of dry cereal, foil-wrapped Toaster Tarts, and a handful of granola bars. Since we couldn't use the microwave or open the fridge for milk, we poured glasses of room-temperature bottled water and sat in a circle on the floor around the coffee table.

It wasn't exactly a five-star Quantum corporate breakfast, but the vibe in the room was incredibly peaceful.

Casey happily munched on a dry strawberry Pop-Tart, entirely unbothered by the lack of electricity. She was wearing a massive, fluffy sweater over her pajama pants, leaning back against the sofa bed frame with her knees pulled to her chest.

"I kind of like it," Liz smiled, wrapping her hands around her glass of water. She was wearing one of my oversized t-shirts and a pair of thick sweatpants. "It forces us to just stop and exist. No schedules, no rushing."

"It is definitely a forced operational pause," Cassandra agreed dryly, taking a bite of a granola bar. "But as long as the structural integrity of the roof holds and the utility crews keep working, I suppose a pajama day is an acceptable baseline."

***

## 8:30 AM — Circadian Logistics

As we sat on the floor of the dim living room, passing around boxes of dry oat flakes and frosted toaster pastries, the reality of our new geography started to set in.

I took a bite of a generic, foil-wrapped pastry and looked across the coffee table at Casey. She was happily kicking her feet against the edge of the sofa bed, looking entirely well-rested despite the fact that she had practically lived on an airplane for the last forty-eight hours.

"I don't understand how you two aren't completely dead on your feet," I said, glancing between Casey and Cassandra. "You flew from Seattle to Virginia on Friday, turned right back around, and flew from Virginia to Seattle on Saturday. My internal clock is completely fried, and you guys act like you just took a nap."

Cassandra took a sip of her bottled water, her executive baseline effortlessly shifting to biological logistics.

"It is basic circadian math, David," Cassandra explained, setting her water bottle down. "Casey and I experienced zero actual jet lag because our internal clocks never had time to decouple from the West Coast. We left Washington after school on Friday, and we were on the ground in Virginia for less than eighteen hours before we boarded the jet to come back. Our bodies never registered the Eastern Time Zone. We are dealing with standard travel fatigue, but chronologically, we are perfectly anchored."

Liz rubbed her eyes, leaning her head against my shoulder. "That makes sense. Meanwhile, my body feels like it's almost noon, but it's not even nine in the morning here."

"Traveling East to West is inherently easier on the human body than traveling West to East, because you are extending your day rather than compressing it," Cassandra noted, her tone analytical but warm. "However, you and David have lived your entire lives on Eastern Standard Time. You crossed three distinct time zones yesterday."

"So how long until we stop feeling like we're operating on a delay?" I asked, finishing my dry breakfast.

"The standard medical metric is roughly one day of adjustment for every time zone crossed," Cassandra calculated smoothly. "Since you gained three hours, your systems will require approximately three days to fully overwrite your old baseline. You will likely wake up entirely too early tomorrow and Monday, and hit a wall of exhaustion around seven in the evening. But by Tuesday, your internal clocks will be flawlessly synchronized with the Pacific grid."

"Three days," Liz nodded, wrapping her arms around her knees. "I can handle three days. Especially since we don't have to be anywhere or do anything."

"Exactly," Cassandra smiled, leaning back in her manual chair. "Which is precisely why we executed the extraction now, during the winter academic break. You have nearly a full month to acclimate to the apartment, the time zone, and the municipal transit grid before the spring semester begins at the university."

Casey leaned over, resting her head firmly against my arm to engage her deep-pressure grounding. "And until then, we just get to hang out. No schedules. No Kristine. Just us."

I looked around the dark, quiet apartment. The power was still out, the ambient temperature was slowly dropping, and our breakfast consisted entirely of dry pantry carbohydrates. But as Casey pressed against my side, and Liz held my hand, and Cassandra watched over us with absolute, unconditional protection, I realized my younger sister was entirely correct.

The time zone didn't matter. The blackout didn't matter. The perimeter was secured, and for the next three days, my only operational objective was to adjust to being safe.

***

## 12:15 PM — The Eastside Expansion

We spent the rest of the morning in a massive, tangled cuddle puddle on the living room floor. With the ambient temperature still hovering on the chilly side, we had dragged the heavy wool blankets out from the master bedroom and created a makeshift nest around the sofa bed. Casey was completely anchored against my side, Liz was resting her head on my chest, and Cassandra sat with her back against the sofa frame, her legs stretched out under the blankets with us.

It was perfectly quiet, the rain drumming steadily outside. The Federal Way apartment had been a brilliant tactical perimeter for Cassandra and Casey, but as I looked around the tight living room, the physical limitations of the space were obvious.

"This apartment is a great temporary staging ground," Cassandra said softly, echoing my exact thoughts as she ran a hand gently through Casey's hair. "But it was only ever designed to hold two females. It was never meant to accommodate a pack of four adults."

Liz tilted her head up, looking at Cass. "Are you thinking about renting a bigger place down here?"

"No," Cassandra replied, her tone shifting into a quiet, calculated executive register. "I'm thinking about buying a house."

Before any of us could process the sheer financial magnitude of that statement, a loud, mechanical *clunk* echoed from the utility closet down the hall. A second later, the steady, rhythmic whoosh of the HVAC system kicked on. The digital clock on the microwave lit up, blinking aggressively in the kitchen.

Casey gasped happily. "The power!"

"The grid is back online," Cassandra smiled, immediately shifting into operational mode. "Liz, grab my laptop from the kitchen island, please? The battery drained completely overnight, it needs to be hardwired into the wall."

Liz untangled herself from the blankets and grabbed the silver laptop, bringing it over and plugging the heavy power brick into the nearest living room outlet. As the screen flared to life, Cassandra booted up her web browser and immediately pulled up a digital map of the Puget Sound region.

"If we are going to purchase a permanent perimeter, we need to completely overhaul our geographic footprint," Cassandra explained, rotating the laptop so we could all see the screen. "Right now, I am commuting from deep south King County all the way up to the Quantum campus. It's functional, but it's not efficient. If we are buying a house, we are looking at the Eastside."

"The Eastside?" I asked, looking at the map. "You mean across Lake Washington?"

"Exactly," Cassandra nodded, tracing a finger over the digital map.

"Cass, you're a high-level executive at one of the biggest tech companies on the planet," Liz pointed out, her eyes wide as she looked at the map's wealthy enclaves. "You could easily afford one of those massive waterfront mansions on Mercer Island. Or even Medina. Isn't that where all the tech billionaires live?"

"Medina is highly optimal from a transit perspective," Cassandra conceded, pulling up the TAPS route maps in a new tab to cross-reference the data. She tapped the screen to highlight the intersecting transit lines.

- **The Commute to Northwest Pacific University:** "If we anchored in Medina, David and Liz could simply walk to the main road and catch the 271 bus going straight across the SR-520 floating bridge. It would drop you directly on the NPU campus in Seattle."

- **The Commute to Quantum:** "At the exact same time, I could catch the 271 heading the opposite direction. It would take me to the Bellevue Transit Center, where I could easily transfer to an express route up to the Overlake Transit Center, right next to Building 33."

"That is a flawless pipeline," I admitted, my processor instantly recognizing the beauty of the transit architecture. "One bus route serves the entire pack in opposite directions."

Cassandra hummed in agreement, her fingers flying across the trackpad as she pulled up the regional real estate MLS listings. "Medina is statistically viable. But let's see what else is currently on the market—"

She stopped. Her finger hovered over the trackpad.

"Wait," Cassandra breathed, her executive baseline momentarily fracturing in pure surprise. She clicked on a newly populated listing, bringing up a photo of a sprawling, beautiful property surrounded by heavy Pacific Northwest evergreen trees.

"What is it?" Casey asked, leaning over my arm to squint at the screen.

"Look at the address," Cassandra said, tapping the screen. "It's not in Medina. It's in Redmond. It's just a few short blocks away from the Quantum campus. I wouldn't even need to take the bus to the Overlake Transit Center. I could literally roll from my front door to Building 33 in under ten minutes."

I looked at the property details, then back at Cassandra. Her eyes were already running the logistical math, calculating property taxes, square footage, and accessibility modifications.

"Should we check it out?" Liz asked, a massive grin spreading across her face.

Cassandra closed all the transit tabs, leaving only the house listing glowing on the screen. "Yes. We absolutely should."

## 1:00 PM — The Tier-One Acquisition

Cassandra didn't just browse standard public real estate websites. The moment we decided on the Eastside, she closed the public tabs and logged directly into the highly exclusive Quantum Building 33 intranet wiki.

"Standard real estate agents operate too slowly," Cassandra murmured, her eyes scanning the encrypted corporate directory. She bypassed the public real estate market entirely to locate a Tier-One real estate agent who specializes in acquisitions for high-net-worth tech executives.

She clicked on a listing the agent's internal portal had flagged. It was exactly what she was looking for: a sprawling, 3,800-square-foot Pacific Northwest rambler situated right on the edge of the Quantum campus.

"This is it," Cassandra stated, her eyes locking onto the property specs. "It fits the mandate perfectly. I require a single-family home on a flat lot, structurally suitable for commercial-grade ADA retrofitting, and located within a highly specific, transit-dense perimeter near the Overlake Transit Center in Redmond."

"Are you going to call them?" Liz asked, peering over my shoulder at the screen. "It's Sunday afternoon. The real estate office is definitely closed."

"I'll use their priority routing," Cassandra replied. She clicked an exclusive back-door email link maintained specifically for Quantum executives, designed to instantly flag the sender's corporate clearance level and bypass standard reception queues.

Her fingers flew across the keyboard, drafting a polite but highly efficient message.

> **From:** Cassandra Vance
>
> **To:** Executive Acquisitions Desk
>
> **Subject:** Priority Inquiry - Redmond Property
>
> *Good afternoon,*
>
> *I am looking to immediately view and acquire the 3,800-square-foot rambler adjacent to the Quantum campus. Please advise on your earliest availability to facilitate a private walk-through. I understand it is Sunday, so I am perfectly amenable to scheduling this for Monday morning if necessary.*
>
> *Best regards,*
>
> *Cassandra Vance*
>
She hit send and closed the mail client. "There. We'll likely hear back tomorrow morning once standard business hours resume—"

*Ping.*

Less than forty-five seconds after the email left her outbox, an alert flashed in the top right corner of Cassandra's screen. For the first time all weekend, Cassandra Vance looked genuinely shocked. Her executive baseline momentarily fractured into pure disbelief.

"Well," Cassandra said, blinking at the screen. "I stand corrected."

"Did they already reply?" Casey asked, laughing at our sister's rare moment of surprise.

"They did," Cass nodded, opening the response. "Apparently, when you use the Building 33 portal, 'weekend hours' simply do not apply. The agent is offering to meet us at the property for a private walk-through in two hours."

I looked at Liz, a massive grin breaking across my face. Our temporary Federal Way perimeter was already evolving into a permanent, sovereign fortress.

"I guess we better get out of these pajamas," I smiled, tossing the heavy wool blankets aside.

***

## 1:15 PM — The Sunday Dispatch

The sudden, chaotic scramble to get out of our flannel pajamas and into respectable daytime clothes took less than ten minutes. But as I pulled a clean sweater over my head and walked back out to the living room, Cassandra was staring at the transit maps on her laptop with a deep, frustrated frown.

"Is there a problem with the route?" I asked, grabbing my coat.

"The Sunday municipal schedule is fundamentally flawed for an emergency deployment," Cassandra stated, snapping her laptop shut. "The 565 express route to Overlake only operates on weekdays. If we utilize the bus grid today, we would have to take the 174 local crawler all the way to the airport, transfer to the 560 to Bellevue, and then catch the 230 local into Redmond. It would take well over two hours. That is an unacceptable level of operational friction."

She immediately pulled her cell phone from her purse and dialed a local dispatch number.

"Yes, I need a minivan at the apartment complex on the corner of SR-99 and South 308th," Cassandra instructed the dispatcher, her voice crisp and authoritative. "No, it does not need a mechanical lift. I have a team with me to execute a manual transfer. Just send a standard minivan with an empty trunk. Thank you."

She hung up and looked at us. "The dispatcher said fifteen minutes."

"You don't care about the wheelchair ramp?" Liz asked, knowing how fiercely Cassandra usually guarded her physical autonomy.

"Not today," Cass replied, wheeling herself toward the front door. "When it is just me traveling, I require a lift. But right now, I have my brother, my sister, and you. We can break down the chair and execute a manual transfer into a standard vehicle in under thirty seconds. We are prioritizing speed."

Exactly fifteen minutes later, a standard yellow minivan pulled up to the lobby doors. We moved with synchronized precision. I opened the passenger door, and Liz and I securely braced Cassandra under her arms as she pivoted smoothly from her chair into the front seat. While Casey hopped into the back row, I quickly collapsed the manual chair, popped the wheels off, and shoved the entire frame into the trunk.

As the cab merged onto Interstate 5 North to begin the forty-minute drive toward the Eastside, Cassandra double-checked her purse, ensuring her Quantum Blue Badge was securely clipped inside.

"I have to admit," Cassandra murmured, looking out the window as we crossed the county line, "I am genuinely surprised."

"By what?" Casey asked, leaning forward from the back seat.

"The agent," Cass replied, shaking her head slightly. "I am perfectly aware of the leverage my clearance level carries inside Building 33. But the fact that this agent was willing to completely abandon his Sunday afternoon on fifteen minutes' notice just because a Quantum executive sent an email... it is staggering."

"They know who they're dealing with," I smiled, leaning back against the seat. "They know you're not going to waste their time."

Cassandra didn't reply, but a small, sharp smile pulled at the corner of her mouth. The skyline of downtown Seattle passed by on our left as the cab banked onto the SR-520 floating bridge, carrying us across the gray, choppy waters of Lake Washington and straight toward our permanent perimeter.
