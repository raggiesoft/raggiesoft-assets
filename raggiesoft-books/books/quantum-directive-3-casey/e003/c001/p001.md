## January 10, 2005 • 7:00 PM — The Scope of Practice

The living room of Cassandra's Federal Way apartment was quiet, save for the rhythmic tapping of my keyboard and the soft hum of the rain against the glass sliding door. 

Winter Quarter at Northwest Pacific University had officially started. Liz and I were completely settled into our academic routines, taking the express bus from Federal Way directly to the NPU campus in Seattle, while Cassandra navigated her own commute toward the massive Quantum corporate campus. 

The logistics were flawless. We were comfortably anchored in the Federal Way apartment while Cassandra finalized the staggering corporate paperwork required to purchase our permanent house out in Redmond. The perimeter was entirely secure. But as I sat at the kitchen island with my laptop open, staring at the Washington State Department of Health's regulatory guidelines, I realized we had a massive, looming vulnerability.

For years, I had always assumed that just being Cassandra's brother—and being physically capable of assisting her with her Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)—would be enough to act as her primary caregiver once we were out from under our parents' roof. 

I was wrong. 

I clicked through the heavy, legalistic PDFs outlining the state's scope of practice for home care. Cassandra didn't just need someone to help push her manual wheelchair or reach high shelves. She had complex, underlying neurological needs stemming from her cerebral palsy. She required highly specific, regulated medication management, complex physical transfers, and the constant monitoring of autonomic functions that could rapidly destabilize. 

If she ever needed a specialized intervention, or if we ever needed to interface with her neurologists or secure institutional accommodations, a family member with no medical background had zero legal authority to execute clinical care. I would be legally barred from performing anything beyond basic, non-medical assistance. We would be entirely dependent on third-party home health agencies—strangers coming into our house, breaching our secure perimeter, and dictating her daily schedule. 

Cassandra would absolutely hate that. It would destroy her autonomy. 

I opened a new tab on OmniSearch and began cross-referencing the credentialing tiers. 

A Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) was the absolute baseline, but the scope of practice was far too narrow. A CNA couldn't legally administer medications, manage complex therapies, or handle acute physiological shifts. It was primarily focused on basic bathing, feeding, and mobility. It wasn't enough. 

A Registered Nurse (RN) was the gold standard, but it required a brutal, multi-year bachelor's degree that would completely derail my current academic trajectory and delay my ability to step in as her primary provider. 

And then, right in the middle of the credentialing matrix, I found the exact puzzle piece I needed: the Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN). 

The LPN scope of practice was incredibly robust. It authorized the administration of medications, the execution of complex nursing plans, the management of specialized medical equipment, and the legal ability to interface directly with physicians on a clinical level. It was the exact level of clearance required to legally, safely, and autonomously manage Cassandra's care without ever needing to rely on a third-party agency. 

I highlighted the LPN requirements on the screen, my heart accelerating. 

It wasn't an easy credential. It required intense anatomy and physiology prerequisites, hundreds of hours of clinical rotations, and passing the grueling NCLEX-PN national board exam. 

But as I pulled up the course catalog for Northwest Pacific University's College of Nursing and started mapping out the prerequisite tracks for their LPN certification program, a massive dual-track academic path suddenly crystallized. 

I wasn't going to drop my tech major—I still loved tinkering with HTML and building out local networks. But I was going to run the LPN curriculum right alongside it. I didn't plan on being a full-time career nurse. I just needed the clinical clearance. 

I was going to earn my LPN, so that the next time I sat in a room with Cassandra's primary care physicians, I wouldn't just be the "helpful brother." I would be speaking to them as a fellow medical professional, and I was going to permanently secure my sister's autonomy.

## January 10, 2005 • 7:30 PM — The Pitch

I didn't wait until morning to present my plan. 

Twenty minutes after mapping out the academic requirements, I carried my laptop into the living room. Cassandra was sitting on the sofa, her power-assist manual wheelchair parked nearby, reviewing a dense stack of Quantum logistics reports. Liz was curled up in the oversized armchair opposite her, highlighting a textbook for one of her NPU communications classes. 

"Hey," I said, setting the laptop down on the coffee table between them. "I need to run something by both of you."

Cassandra didn't look up from her paperwork, but she tapped her pen against the armrest—her universal signal that I had the floor. Liz lowered her highlighter, giving me her full attention. 

"I've been looking at the Washington State Department of Health regulations regarding home care," I started, taking a seat on the edge of the sofa. "Right now, if we ever need to bring in specialized medical equipment, or if you require an acute medical intervention, I have absolutely zero legal authority to do anything. I'm just family. I can help with basic transfers, but legally, I have no clinical standing."

"I am aware," Cassandra said, her voice dry. She finally looked up, her expression tightening slightly. "Which means if my cerebral palsy requires anything beyond basic assistance, we are legally mandated to contract a third-party home health agency. Complete strangers inside our perimeter, dictating a schedule."

"Exactly," I said. "And I know how much you would hate that."

"It's an unacceptable security vulnerability," Cassandra agreed immediately. "But it is a legal requirement we cannot bypass."

"We can bypass it," I countered. I turned the laptop around so the screen faced both of them. "I'm going to get my LPN."

Liz blinked, leaning forward to look at the screen. "Your Licensed Practical Nurse? David, I thought you were majoring in Information Technology."

"I am," I confirmed. "I'm not dropping my IT major. I still want to work with networks and web development. I'm not doing this to become a career floor nurse at a hospital. I'm doing a dual-track."

I pointed to the credentialing matrix on the screen. 

"The LPN scope of practice is the exact level of clearance I need. It gives me the legal authority to administer medications, manage complex therapies, and operate specialized medical equipment without needing a third-party agency. More importantly, it gives me a state-sanctioned medical license." I looked directly at Cassandra. "The next time we have to sit in a room with your primary care physician or a specialist, I won't just be the helpful brother sitting in the corner. I'll be a licensed medical professional. I can interface with them on a clinical level."

The living room went completely silent. 

Liz stared at me, a slow, incredibly proud smile spreading across her face. "You're getting a nursing license just to pull rank on her doctors?"

"I'm securing the perimeter," I said, borrowing Cassandra's terminology. 

Cassandra stared at the laptop screen for a long time. Her eyes tracked over the intense prerequisite requirements—the anatomy and physiology labs, the clinical hours, the board exams. She knew exactly how much extra work I was volunteering to take on. 

She looked up at me, her dark eyes completely unreadable for a moment before she gave a single, definitive nod. 

"The tactical advantage of having an in-house, licensed medical proxy is absolute," Cassandra said softly. The corporate freeze in her voice had completely melted, replaced by a profound, quiet gratitude. "It's a brilliant strategic maneuver, David."

"I'm going to the NPU academic advising office tomorrow morning," I told them, closing the laptop. "I'm officially declaring the dual-track."

## January 11, 2005 • 9:00 AM — The Academic Advisor

The Northwest Pacific University academic advising center was already packed with students frantically trying to adjust their Winter Quarter schedules, but I had booked my appointment weeks ago. 

I sat across the desk from Mrs. Higgins, my assigned academic advisor. She was a no-nonsense woman in her fifties who had been navigating NPU degree tracks for over a decade. She peered at her computer monitor, clicked her mouse a few times, and then slowly lowered her reading glasses to stare at me. 

"David, I want to make sure I am reading this correctly," she said, her voice a mix of professional skepticism and genuine confusion. "You are currently enrolled as an Information Technology major."

"Yes, ma'am," I said. 

"Your grades are excellent. You're completely on track," she continued, tapping a manicured fingernail against the edge of her desk. "But you've submitted a petition to concurrently enroll in the College of Nursing's LPN certification track."

"That's correct."

"You understand that the LPN program is not a minor," Mrs. Higgins said, leaning forward. "It is an intense, clinically demanding certification. You will be taking heavy, lab-based prerequisites—Anatomy and Physiology I and II, Microbiology, Pharmacology. And once you clear the prerequisites, you have to complete hundreds of hours of hands-on clinical rotations over at the NPU Medical Center."

She gestured out her window toward the southern end of the campus, where the massive, sprawling complex of the NPU teaching hospital sat overlooking the water. It was the in-universe equivalent of the world-renowned UW Medical Center—a massive Level I trauma and research hub. 

"I'm aware of the requirements," I said evenly. 

"You're going to be trying to pass network architecture and database management exams while simultaneously learning how to draw blood, insert catheters, and manage acute patient vitals at a world-class teaching hospital," she warned me. "It is going to be a brutal schedule. Most students who attempt a dual-track like this end up burning out and dropping one of them."

"I'm not going to drop my IT major, and I'm not going to fail the clinicals," I replied. I kept my voice completely level, holding her gaze without blinking. "I have a highly specific, personal requirement for the LPN credential. I need the clinical clearance, and I am fully prepared to handle the workload."

Mrs. Higgins studied my face for a long moment. She was looking for the typical college-student hesitation—the slight waver that usually accompanied a wildly ambitious, poorly thought-out plan. 

She didn't find any. 

She sighed, pushing her glasses back up her nose and turning back to her keyboard. "Alright, Mr. Vance. If you're absolutely certain. But we need to restructure your entire academic roadmap. You can't take Anatomy and Physiology until you've cleared the foundational biology requirement, which means we need to get you into Bio 101 this quarter."

"Whatever it takes," I said. 

For the next forty-five minutes, we completely tore down my schedule and rebuilt it from scratch. By the time I walked out of the advising office and stepped into the cold, damp Seattle morning air, my path was locked in. 

I was officially a pre-nursing IT major, and the clock had just started on securing the perimeter.

## January 11, 2005 • 10:30 AM — The Pack Conference

The Northwest Pacific University student union was a massive, cavernous building echoing with the chaotic noise of thousands of students returning for Winter Quarter. I navigated through the dense crowd to the food court, finding Liz sitting at a small corner table with two coffees. 

I dropped my backpack onto an empty chair, grabbed one of the coffees, and pulled out my flip phone. 

"Is it done?" Liz asked, taking a sip from her cup. 

"I'm officially a pre-nursing IT major," I confirmed. I navigated to my contacts, initiated a three-way conference call, and set the phone down in the center of the table on speaker. "Bio 101 starts tomorrow."

The phone rang for a few seconds before two distinct streams of background noise merged onto the line. On one end, the distinct, chaotic chatter of the Rhode Island Delaney household filtered through. On the other end, the quieter, familiar ambient noise of the Virginia Beach house. 

"Vance!" Erin's voice boomed over the speaker from Rhode Island. "Are you freezing to death in Seattle yet?"

"It's just raining," I said, leaning closer to the phone. "Is everyone on?"

"We're all here, David," Mrs. Carter’s voice replied from Virginia Beach. 

"Meredith is making us do chores, so make it quick," Kelly complained in the background, earning a sharp reprimand from her older sister. 

I smiled, instantly missing the sheer gravity of their combined presence. The week we had spent in Virginia Beach during the deployment had completely rewired my understanding of family. Being separated from them by three thousand miles felt entirely unnatural. 

"I just walked out of the academic advising office," I announced to the combined pack. "I officially altered my degree track. I'm running my IT major concurrently with the nursing prerequisites. I'm going for my LPN."

There was a brief moment of silence on the line before the Rhode Island feed erupted into cheers. 

"Oh my god, David, that's amazing!" Megan yelled over the noise of her younger sisters. 

"Wait, you're actually going to be a nurse?" Chloe asked, sounding genuinely impressed. 

"Not a full-time hospital nurse," Liz clarified, leaning toward the phone. "He's getting his clinical license so he has legal medical authority over Cassandra's care. He's locking out the third-party agencies."

The realization hit the Virginia Beach line immediately. 

"That is brilliant," Meredith said, her tone shifting to absolute, profound respect. "David, that is such an incredible way to step up for her. She must be so relieved."

"She is," I said quietly. 

Suddenly, a synthesized, mechanical voice cut clearly through the Virginia Beach audio feed. It was David Delaney's AAC device. 

*IT and nursing. You are a glutton for punishment.* 

The entire conference call burst into laughter. 

<aac>Proud of you,</aac> Kate's AAC device immediately followed up, her synthesized voice layering over the laughter. <aac>Cassandra is lucky.</aac>

"I'm the lucky one," I replied, meaning every single syllable. I took a deep breath, looking across the table at Liz, who was beaming at the phone. "I miss you guys. Seattle feels way too quiet without thirty people trying to talk over each other."

"Just survive until March, Vance," Erin ordered from Rhode Island. "Because the second Spring Break hits, both houses are flying out to Washington State. We're going to completely crush-load that massive new house Cassandra is buying in Redmond."

"I can't wait," I said, a massive surge of relief washing over me. 

The perimeter was secure, my academic path was locked, and the entire pack was backing me up. I drank my coffee, listening to the Delaneys argue over the phone, and for the first time in my life, I felt completely, utterly unstoppable.
