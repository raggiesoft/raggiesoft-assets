The jarring hiss of the air brakes violently pulled me out of a shallow, pain-induced daze. 

"Knoxville," the driver's voice crackled flatly over the PA system, devoid of any sympathy for the hour. "This bus terminates here. Everybody off. Gather all your belongings. If you're transferring westbound, check the boards inside."

It was three-thirty in the morning. The cabin lights flickered on, blindingly harsh after hours of pitch-black Appalachian darkness. A collective groan rippled through the bus as dozens of exhausted passengers were forced to abandon the tiny, cramped pockets of warmth they had managed to carve out for themselves. 

Delores unbuckled her jacket and stood up, her face tight with sleep-deprived irritation. "Get the bags," she ordered, immediately stepping into the aisle to escape the claustrophobic row. 

I tried to stand, but my legs didn't cooperate. 

After sitting nearly motionless in a vibrating, unergonomic seat for over twelve hours, my CP had completely locked up my lower body. My right knee felt like it had been filled with wet cement, and the spasticity in my lower back sent sharp, electric spikes of pain down my spine the second I put weight on my feet. I had to grip the plastic headrest of the seat in front of me just to keep from collapsing back down. 

I dragged myself into the aisle, gritting my teeth, and slowly limped down the narrow rubber steps into the freezing Tennessee night. 

The Knoxville Pathfinder terminal was a brutalist block of concrete bathed in harsh fluorescent light. Despite the ungodly hour, the waiting room was packed. Travelers were slumped across rows of hard plastic chairs or sleeping directly on the cold linoleum floor, their luggage pulled tight against their bodies. 

I dragged our two massive suitcases inside, the wheels grinding loudly across the floor, and parked them near a bank of vending machines. Delores was already at the ticket counter, aggressively confirming our transfer. 

I slumped against the glass front of a snack machine, my breathing shallow. The harsh white light of the terminal made the exhaustion behind my eyes burn. I pulled out my Kova phone, verifying the time. It was the dead of night, and we were only one state away from Virginia. The sheer, terrifying mathematics of the geography crashed over me. We had been traveling for almost an entire day, and we hadn't even crossed the Mississippi River yet. 

Delores returned, her jaw set. "Westbound boards in an hour," she snapped, finding a single empty plastic chair and sitting down, leaving me standing by the luggage. "Don't wander off."

I looked at the glowing rows of stale potato chips and shrink-wrapped pastries behind the vending machine glass. The hot breakfast in Richmond felt like it had happened in another lifetime.
