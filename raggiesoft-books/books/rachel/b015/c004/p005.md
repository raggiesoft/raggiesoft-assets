The short stop in Fort Collins was uneventful. A few passengers disembarked, a few boarded, and the heavy coach quickly pulled back out onto the road. 

By the time we cleared the city limits, the sun was dropping rapidly behind the jagged peaks of the Rocky Mountains to our west, casting long, dark shadows across the plains. 

I sat in the aisle seat, shifting my weight slightly to try and relieve the dull, persistent burn on my right hip. But as I looked out the massive windshield of the coach, my processor threw a warning flag. 

We weren't getting back on the interstate. 

Instead of the wide, brightly lit lanes of I-25 or I-70, the Pathfinder had merged onto US-287—a narrow, two-lane rural highway that carved directly into the barren, high-desert scrubland. The transition was jarring. The safety and predictability of the multi-lane interstate system completely vanished, replaced by the isolating reality of a dark, undivided road cutting through absolute nowhere. 

As the last light of the sun faded into a deep, bruising purple twilight, the psychological weight of the two-lane road began to press down on me. 

There were no streetlights. There were no gas stations. There were no other cars. The only light in the world was the sweeping beam of the bus's headlights cutting through the high-desert darkness. We were completely disconnected from the national grid, barrelling blindly through the freezing void between Colorado and Wyoming. 

Delores was fast asleep in the window seat, her head lolling slightly with the motion of the coach. 

I stared out into the pitch-black desert, the isolation amplifying the physical pain in my body. The thin, mile-high air was still starving my lungs, forcing me to take shallow, rapid breaths just to maintain baseline oxygen levels. My hip felt like it was resting on a hot coil, the strange, localized fire refusing to extinguish no matter how I adjusted my posture. 

Hours bled away in the darkness. The heavy drone of the diesel engine became a hypnotic, suffocating hum. 

Eventually, the two-lane road crested a massive ridge, and the distant, sprawling lights of Laramie, Wyoming appeared on the horizon. The bus finally slowed down, merging onto the wide, familiar concrete lanes of Interstate 80 West. 

The rural detour was over, but the brutal reality of the Wyoming crossing had just begun. We were officially on the long, dark corridor to Salt Lake City, and my biological reserves were critically low.
