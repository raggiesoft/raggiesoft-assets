The Pasco Intermodal Terminal was quiet and largely deserted when the Pathfinder coach pulled into the bay. 

The driver announced a brief rest stop over the PA system. The harsh, fluorescent lights of the cabin flickered on, abruptly ending the dark sanctuary of the overnight drive. 

"Get up," Delores ordered. 

Her voice wasn't laced with the sharp, defensive annoyance that usually accompanied her commands. It was low. Flat. Heavy with an unsettling, predatory calm. 

I gripped the armrest, pulling myself upright. The pressure sore on my right hip flared with a blinding, agonizing heat, sending a wave of nausea crashing through my stomach. I bit the inside of my cheek, dragging my leg down the rubber steps of the bus and limping into the cold morning air of Washington State. 

We walked into the terminal building. 

"We're going to the restroom," she said, her hand suddenly clamping down on my shoulder. Her grip was terrifyingly rigid. 

She didn't steer me toward the men's room. She marched me directly into the large, empty family restroom located down a quiet side corridor. The heavy wooden door swung shut behind us, cutting off the ambient noise of the terminal. 

The lock clicked into place with a sharp, metallic thud. 

The sound of that deadbolt sliding home caused my blood to run completely cold. My CP spasticity, already red-lining from the exhaustion and pain, suddenly locked my legs into a rigid, trembling stance. 

I looked at her, my breathing shallow and erratic. 

The mask of the inconvenienced, narcissistic girlfriend had completely vanished. Her eyes were dark, devoid of any warmth or basic human empathy. The psychological abuse of the last three days—the weaponized silence, the forced dehydration, the control over my finances—had merely been the prologue. 
The mask of the inconvenienced, narcissistic girlfriend had completely vanished. But it wasn't replaced by hostility or anger. 

Instead, a chilling, sickening sweetness washed over her features. The rigid tension in her shoulders vanished. She took a step toward me, closing the physical distance in the small, tiled room, and her voice dropped into a soft, alluring whisper. 

"I know you want this," she murmured, her tone completely devoid of the sharp, defensive edge she had wielded for the last three days. 

I was physically broken. I was starving, severely dehydrated, and in excruciating pain. I had no strength left to fight her off, no voice to scream for help, and the thick walls of the terminal restroom created a perfect, soundproof isolation chamber. 

She reached out, her hands crossing a boundary that shattered the last remaining fragments of my psychological stability. 

A suffocating, absolute terror crashed over me. I froze entirely, my central nervous system plunging into a state of severe, trauma-induced paralysis. I couldn't move. I couldn't process the horrific reality of what was happening. The air in the room felt impossibly thick, crushing down on my chest as the violation began to unfold.

"The bus," she muttered, her voice entirely devoid of emotion, as if she were simply reading a grocery list. "It's going to leave."

She didn't stop because she recognized the horrific depravity of her actions. She didn't stop out of a sudden surge of morality or human affection. She stopped solely because the external timeline dictated that she had to. 

She stepped back, smoothing her clothes with a chilling, robotic calmness. 

"Fix yourself," she snapped, her tone instantly reverting to its usual, hostile edge. "I am not missing this bus because of you."

She unlocked the deadbolt, pulled the heavy door open, and walked out into the terminal without looking back. 

I stood alone in the tiled restroom, my body trembling so violently I could barely remain standing. The pressure sore on my hip burned like a live coal, but the physical pain was entirely eclipsed by the devastating psychological shock of the Pasco Lock.
