## 2:30 PM — Not Proper

The heavy brass tokens eventually ran out, and Jessica signaled that our time in the quiet, mechanical sanctuary of The Neon Quarter was over.

Rachel immediately grabbed my hand again as we crossed the threshold, stepping back into the bright, deafening chaos of the food court. We navigated through the maze of sticky tables and overflowing trash cans until we spotted our family. They had claimed a large cluster of tables near the center of the room. Uncle Eddy and Joey were eating massive slices of pizza, while Mom and Aunt Janet were surrounded by heavy plastic shopping bags.

Standing a few feet away from the tables, isolated from the rest of the group, were Dad and Grandma Helen.

Grandma Helen was a very traditional woman from Newport, Rhode Island, and she carried a rigid sense of East Coast etiquette wherever she went. She was currently speaking to Dad in a hushed, sharp whisper, her posture stiff. Dad was rubbing his temples, looking entirely exhausted by the conversation.

As Jessica led us over, Dad looked up. His expression immediately tightened into that familiar, authoritative mask.

"Michael," Dad said, stepping away from Grandma Helen to intercept us. "Tonight, you are going back to your room. You're going to sleep in your own bed."

My chest instantly seized up. The panic was sharp and fast. "But Joey is in there."

"I don't care," Dad replied firmly, keeping his voice low so the other tables wouldn't hear. "Joey is your cousin. You will share the room with him."

"We weren't fighting on the bottom bunk," I argued, my voice trembling slightly. I couldn't articulate the sensory math. I just knew that the lower bunk was safe and my bedroom was compromised. Rachel squeezed my hand so hard her knuckles turned white, sensing my rising panic.

"That's not the point, Michael," Grandma Helen interjected, stepping up beside Dad. She looked down at Rachel and me with a strict, disapproving frown. "It simply isn't proper."

I stared at her, completely lost. I had no idea what the word "proper" meant in this context. We were just sleeping. We were just hiding from the noise. It wasn't a puzzle or a game; it was a logistical solution to a broken environment. There was no logic to her argument.

"Why isn't it proper?" I asked, my frustration mounting because the rules were shifting again without any logical foundation.

Dad flushed red. He opened his mouth, then closed it, looking incredibly uncomfortable. He couldn't explain it to me. He was trapped between his mother's rigid, old-world sensibilities and the impossible task of explaining those unspoken adult anxieties to an eight-year-old boy.

Beside me, Jessica let out a loud, theatrical scoff.

At thirteen, she was standing squarely on the other side of the childhood divide. She was old enough to instantly decode the weird, puritanical panic Dad and Grandma Helen were projecting onto us. She knew exactly what they were implying about a boy and a girl sharing a bed, and she clearly thought they were being ridiculous.

"They're eight and six, Dad," Jessica said, her voice dripping with teenage exasperation. "They're practically toddlers. They're just trying to—"

<aac>I want to go to the arcade!</aac>

The sudden, ear-piercing shriek echoed across the entire food court.

Everyone jumped. Rachel flinched violently, burying her face into my side as her hands locked around my arm in a terrified vice grip.

Ten feet away, Joey was standing on his plastic chair, his face red with fury. Uncle Eddy had apparently just told him no, and Joey had chosen the nuclear option. He spiked his half-empty heavy plastic soda cup directly onto the table. Ice and brown soda exploded everywhere, splashing across Mom's shopping bags and Aunt Janet's coat.

"Joey, sit down right now!" Uncle Eddy bellowed, his voice booming over the ambient mall noise.

Joey screamed again, kicking the table leg so hard the entire cluster of trays rattled.

Silence swept over our small, isolated circle. Grandma Helen was staring at the chaotic scene, her hand clutching the collar of her coat, absolutely appalled by the total lack of decorum. Dad was staring at the spilled soda, his jaw tight, his previous lecture about the rules completely derailed.

Jessica didn't miss a beat. She looked at Dad, then pointed a finger directly at Joey, who was now throwing a full-blown tantrum while Uncle Eddy tried to wrangle him off the chair.

"You want to lock Michael in a room with that?" Jessica asked flatly.

Dad closed his eyes, taking a long, deep breath. He was already exhausted, completely overwhelmed by the crowds, the noise, and now his nephew causing a massive public scene. The thought of forcing me into a small bedroom with that kind of violent, unstructured energy was suddenly more trouble than it was worth.

Grandma Helen pursed her lips, her strict East Coast sensibilities highly offended. "Perhaps... given the circumstances, Michael sleeping elsewhere is for the best."

Dad opened his eyes, defeated by the sheer logistics of his own family. He looked down at me and Rachel, who was still trembling against my side.

"Fine," Dad sighed heavily, waving his hand in surrender. "Fine. But I don't want to hear a single peep out of you two upstairs tonight."

Jessica flashed me a subtle, triumphant smirk. The adults had tried to enforce their invisible social rules, but the sheer, undeniable reality of Joey's chaos had broken the system in our favor. The lower bunk was secure.

## 3:15 PM — The Bantam Workaround

The aftermath of Joey’s food court tantrum meant the massive family shopping trip was officially aborted. The logistics of getting sixteen people back to Delaney Street required splitting up again. Mom, Uncle Eddy, and Aunt Janet took the cousins and the grandmothers in the larger sedans, leaving Dad to transport me and my sisters in the truck.

Dad’s truck was a dark red 1989 Izumi Bantam. It was a boxy, light-duty Japanese import with a manual stick shift and absolutely zero frills. The cab consisted of a single, narrow bench seat designed for two, maybe three, average-sized adults.

It was definitely not designed for an adult and three growing kids.

Dad unlocked the heavy metal doors, and the three of us automatically assumed our highly calculated, unspoken formation. Jessica, being thirteen and the tallest, climbed in first and slid all the way over to the passenger window. I climbed in next, wedging myself into the dreaded middle position directly over the transmission hump, pulling my knees tight to the left so I wouldn't block the gear shift.

Rachel climbed in last, scrambling up the running board and wedging herself tightly into the narrow gap between me and Jessica. Instead of leaning toward the passenger door, she pressed her entire left side flush against my ribs, burrowing into my shoulder, and I immediately reached over and wrapped my arm completely around her shoulders, pulling her tightly against my chest to provide that deep, grounding pressure she craved.

Dad opened the driver's side door and froze.

His face tightened, pulling into that same uncomfortable, flushed expression he had worn back at the food court with Grandma Helen. He stared at me and Rachel, pressed tightly together like a single unit in the center of the bench seat.

"Michael. Rachel," Dad sighed, rubbing his forehead. "You don't need to cling to each other like that. Let go of your sister and scoot over."

"There's nowhere left to scoot, Dad," Jessica said from the window seat, crossing her arms over her winter coat.

Dad pointed past me, gesturing toward Jessica. "If she needs to lean on somebody, why doesn't she sit over against you by the window? Then Michael wouldn't be pressed right up against my gear shift."

My chest instantly seized. If Dad pried Rachel off my side and made her lean against Jessica by the window, the entire system would collapse. Rachel wouldn't have my arm anchored around her shoulders to regulate her, and without Rachel's solid weight pressed flush against my side to pin me into the seat cushion, I was going to absorb every single vibration, bump, and loud engine noise directly into my spine. I pulled Rachel tighter against my shoulder, my breathing turning shallow.

"She can't," I blurted out, my voice tight with rising panic.

"Why not?" Dad asked, his frustration mounting. To him, it was a perfectly logical solution. Separate the two kids in the middle and give the stick shift some breathing room. "Michael, you don't need to hold onto her like that. It just... it isn't proper."

There was that word again. Proper. I stared at him from beside Rachel's head, completely baffled. The bench seat was barely four feet wide, and there were four of us in the cab. Wrapping my arm tightly around her shoulders was the safest, most logical way to anchor both of us in place. Why did he keep acting like this was a violation of some invisible rule?

Jessica let out a sharp, exasperated sigh. She saw right through his uncomfortable stuttering, and she perfectly understood the mechanical reality of my panic.

"Dad, she doesn't want to lean on me," Jessica stated flatly. "If you pry them apart, she’s going to start crying. And if she starts crying in this tiny cab, Michael is going to start screaming. Do you really want to drive forty-five minutes down the highway with both of them having a complete meltdown right next to your ear?"

Dad looked from the gear shift, to my arm locked around Rachel's shoulders, to Rachel's perfectly calm face. He pictured the sheer volume of the scenario Jessica had just laid out. He was completely out of options.

"Fine," Dad muttered, climbing into the driver's seat and slamming the heavy metal door shut. "But keep your knees out of my way, Michael."

He jammed the key into the ignition, and the Izumi Bantam roared to life, vibrating the entire cab.

Riding in the Bantam was a massive, overwhelming sensory experience. The suspension was incredibly stiff, the engine was loud, and the cab smelled sharply of old vinyl and gasoline. Under normal circumstances, being squished into the middle seat would have sent my nervous system into a complete spiral.

But with Rachel wedged tightly against my side and my arm wrapped around her shoulders, the environment was perfectly regulated.

Her solid weight pressed me securely into the seat back, while my arm provided a deep, heavy anchor against the constant bouncing of the truck. As Dad merged onto the busy highway, his right arm was constantly in motion, slamming the shifter up into first, down into second, up into third. I knew the exact rhythm of the engine's pitch. Every time he shifted, I smoothly leaned my torso and Rachel's weight slightly toward Jessica, perfectly dodging Dad's elbow without saying a word.

Dad reached out and cranked the volume dial on the center console radio. He always controlled the dial in the Bantam, and it was permanently locked to Rock 107 The Wolf.

The heavy, driving piano chord and ripping guitar riff of Arthur Sterling's "The Bitch is on the Loose" suddenly blasted through the cheap door speakers.

Rachel and I both knew it was a bad word. We knew that if we ever said it in the house, we would be in massive trouble. But we also shared the exact same logical workaround: Dad chose the radio station, and Dad turned up the volume. We weren't breaking the rules; we were just participating in the environment he created. It was an unpunishable loophole.

As Arthur Sterling belted out the chorus, I pulled Rachel tighter against my shoulder and sang the bad word at the top of my lungs, right next to Dad's ear.

Rachel didn't use the words—she couldn't—but the situational mutism didn't stop her from participating. She felt the vibration of my chest singing right against her side, and she immediately matched my energy. She tilted her head back against my shoulder and loudly, enthusiastically vocalized the exact melody of the bad word, her voice buzzing perfectly in sync with mine.

Beside us, Jessica didn't sing a single note. She just looked over at us from the passenger window with a quiet, knowing older-sister smirk—she knew the exact song that was playing, and she saw the exact loophole we were exploiting.

Dad glanced over at us with an embarrassed, sheepish smirk, his lips pressed tightly together as he tried not to laugh at the trap he had walked himself into. He couldn't yell at us, and he couldn't punish us. He had picked the radio station, which meant the bad word was officially fair game.

We rode the rest of the way home wedged side-by-side on the narrow bench seat, completely safe, yelling our sanctioned bad words over the roar of the Izumi Bantam's engine.

## 3:30 PM — The Father's Perspective

Dad kept his eyes locked on the road, his jaw tight as he steered the Izumi Bantam out of the sprawling mall parking lot, making a sharp right onto River Birch Run.

Every time he grabbed the stick shift to accelerate, his elbow brushed against Michael’s winter coat. He could see them in his peripheral vision as he banked left onto Eden Way North—Rachel wedged flush against Michael's side, Michael’s arm locked tightly around her shoulders as both of them threw their heads back and belted out the chorus. They were loud, practically screaming over the radio, completely lost in their own bizarre, shared world.

It made absolutely no sense to him.

Why would she cry if he made her lean against Jessica instead? It was a simple, logical question that gnawed at him as he waited at the traffic light to make a left onto Volvo Parkway.

If Rachel was just scared of the loud truck or the crowds, why wasn't Jessica good enough? Jessica was thirteen. She was practically a third adult. She was soft-spoken, fiercely protective, and sitting right next to the window where Rachel would be completely out of the way of the gear shift. Any normal six-year-old girl who was feeling timid would naturally gravitate toward the older sister who babied her.

Instead, Rachel clung to an eight-year-old boy.

Dad hit the clutch and turned left onto Kempsville Road, the stiff suspension of the Bantam rattling hard over the pavement. Michael wasn't exactly a warm, nurturing figure. He was a quiet, rigid kid who didn't even speak his first words until he was five. He hated when his routine was interrupted, he hated loud noises, and he hated when his cousin Joey touched his things. By all traditional playground logic, Michael should be shoving his little sister away, complaining that she was taking up his room on the bench seat, or whining that girls had cooties.

But he didn't. He held onto her like it was his job. He anchored his arm around her shoulders like a seatbelt, and she pressed back against him with a desperate intensity that made Dad profoundly uncomfortable.

It wasn't just sibling affection; it was something heavier. Something almost frantic. If Dad tried to pry them apart, Rachel wouldn't just pout—she would completely shatter. She would scream, her face turning red, gasping for air like he was physically hurting her. And Michael would instantly panic, his whole body locking up, his eyes darting around like a trapped animal until she was right back against his side.

Why? Dad wondered, his knuckles turning white on the steering wheel as he downshifted for the left turn onto Centerville Turnpike.

Grandma Helen called it "improper." Uncle Eddy called them "spoiled." Dad just thought it was weird.

He loved his kids fiercely. He would tear the world apart to keep them safe. But he didn't know how to protect them from whatever invisible thing was making them act like this. He wanted a normal son who played catch and complained about his little sister. He wanted a normal daughter who talked to her aunts and uncles instead of hiding behind her brother like a mute shadow.

He told himself it was just a phase. Rachel was just overly shy. Michael was just being a good big brother. Eventually, they would grow out of it. They had to grow out of it, because the alternative—that his kids were fundamentally, irreparably different from everyone else—was a thought he wasn't equipped to handle.

He finally made the right turn onto Brandon Boulevard, the familiar residential streets offering a brief reprieve from the heavy traffic. He shifted into third, dodging his son's knee again, letting them scream the lyrics to the radio. It wasn't proper, and it didn't make sense, but it was keeping the peace.

Dad made the final right turn onto Delaney Street. Pulling up alongside 1852, he saw Mom's sedan already parked in the driveway, meaning the rest of the loud, chaotic family was already inside.

With a heavy sigh, Dad parallel parked the Bantam against the curb, cut the engine, and let the mechanical silence wash over the cab. The ride was over, but the house was still waiting.

## 3:55 PM — The Public Radio Sentence

The problem with a really good musical hook is that my brain doesn't just turn it off when the ignition stops.

As the four of us walked up the concrete front walk of 1852 Delaney Street and stepped into the crowded foyer, the heavy piano rhythm of Arthur Sterling was still looping at full volume inside my head. Rachel was still holding tightly to the hem of my winter coat, and before I had even unzipped my jacket, I cheerfully belted the chorus one more time right in the middle of the front hallway.

Rachel immediately chimed in beside me, humming the exact five-note cadence at the top of her lungs.

The entire living room went dead silent.

Mom was standing at the threshold of the dining room with a pot of coffee in her hand, right next to her mother, Grandma Loretta. Slowly, Mom lowered the glass carafe onto the side table and stared at me, her eyes wide with sheer maternal disbelief.

"Michael Thomas Ragsdale," Mom said, her voice dropping an octave. "What did you just sing?"

I froze, my hands still on my coat zipper. Instantly, my processor ran the legal defense.

"I didn't say it on my own," I explained quickly, pointing a finger straight back toward the front door where Dad was hanging up his keys. "It was on the radio in the truck! Dad picked the station, and Dad turned up the volume!"

Every pair of adult eyes in the foyer pivoted directly onto Dad.

Dad's shoulders slumped. The sheepish smirk from the truck cab evaporated completely, replaced by the look of a man who had just realized that an eight-year-old and a six-year-old had not only outmaneuvered him in his own vehicle, they had immediately flipped on him as state's witnesses the second they walked through the door.

"They sang it the whole way down Centerville Turnpike, Mom," Jessica added helpfully as she slipped past us toward the stairs, her face a mask of innocent older-sister concern. "Dad had it on Rock 107 The Wolf."

"Tom!" Mom hissed, crossing her arms tightly over her chest. "They are six and eight years old! You are blasting songs with profanity in that tiny truck cab while they're sitting right next to you?!"

"Ellen, it just came on the radio, I didn't request it—" Dad started, holding both hands up in a desperate attempt at self-defense.

"I don't care how it came on!" Mom snapped. "You control that dial! And since you apparently can't be trusted to screen what's playing on Rock 107 when the kids are in the truck, we are changing the rules."

Beside her, Grandma Loretta adjusted her glasses and gave a slow, approving nod.

"Effective immediately," Mom declared, pointing a finger at Dad's chest, "whenever Michael, Rachel, or Jessica are in that truck with you, the radio stays tuned to 89.5 FM. WCVP."

Dad blinked, pure horror washing over his face. "Ellen... that's public radio."

"It is Coastal Virginia Public Radio," Grandma Loretta corrected him crisply from the dining room doorway. "Classical music in the afternoon and public affairs news on the hour. I listen to it every single day, Tom, and I can assure you nobody on 89.5 is singing that word."

"Exactly," Mom said, her tone final and unappealable. "If I get into that truck or ask Jessica, and that dial is anywhere other than WCVP when the kids are with you, you can walk to work. Do we understand each other?"

"Yes, Ellen," Dad muttered in total defeat.

From the bottom step of the open-slatted stairs, Jessica caught my eye and had to bite her lip to keep from laughing out loud. Rachel squeezed my hand, completely unbothered by the courtroom drama, and together we headed upstairs toward the quiet sanctuary of the lower bunk—leaving Dad in the foyer to mourn the loss of his classic rock station.
