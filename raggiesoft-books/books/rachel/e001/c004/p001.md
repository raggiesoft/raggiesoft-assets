## 8:45 AM — Curbside Drop-Off & The Two-Week Block

The morning humidity coming off the Elizabeth River already smelled like warm concrete and salt water when Mom eased our station wagon up to the drop-off curb off Hampton Boulevard.

"Have a good day, stay hydrated, and keep your water bottles in your packs," Mom called out through the open passenger window as the three of us stepped onto the wide sidewalk at the edge of Virginia Dominion University. "I'll be right back at this exact loop at four o'clock."

"We've got it, Mom," fourteen-year-old Jessica promised, adjusting her clear wire-rimmed glasses and settling her canvas backpack over both shoulders. Before the station wagon had even merged back into the Norfolk traffic, my older sister stepped straight into position on my left flank, while seven-year-old Rachel reached up with her right hand and laced her fingers tightly through mine.

We were on the second morning of a two-week, all-day block at the VDU Junior Admirals Summer Camp—running from nine o'clock in the morning until four o'clock in the afternoon, Monday through Friday. Just like [at home in the bottom bunk on Delaney Street](https://books.raggiesoft.com/rachel/the-delaney-street-years/the-lower-bunk/sanctuary-the-new-arrangement), where Rachel and I stayed side by side to keep the static quiet, the three of us operated as a single unit the second our sneakers touched the pavement.

This two-week session carried a quiet weight that all three of us understood. At fourteen, rising into her freshman year of high school, Jessica stood at the absolute ceiling of the camp's age bracket. After this two-week block ended, she would officially age out of the youth summer camp system forever. And because she was fourteen, the camp roster placed her in the Senior Division with the twelve-to-fourteen-year-old campers for all of the morning and afternoon rotations, while nine-year-old me and seven-year-old Rachel were assigned to the Youth Division.

Before Mom had ever signed the tuition check, however, Jessica had insisted on one non-negotiable condition—and Mom had made sure the camp director documented it in writing on our official registration forms. Every day from twelve o'clock to one o'clock, when all of the divisions marched into the Monarch Commons cafeteria, Jessica had written permission to leave the older campers and sit at the Youth Division tables with her younger brother and sister.

![Nine-year-old Michael, seven-year-old Rachel holding hands, and fourteen-year-old Jessica walking with their VDU Youth Summer Camp counselor and fellow campers across the concrete plaza of Dominion Mall at Virginia Dominion University in July 1994]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/rachel/b001/jul_1994_vdu_childhood_summer_camp_michael_rachel_jessica.jpg)

"I'll see you guys inside Monarch Commons at noon," Jessica told us as the Senior Division counselor blew a short whistle near the check-in tables. She gave my shoulder a quick, grounding squeeze and brushed a stray lock of hair behind Rachel's ear. "Remember the landmarks on the walk over."

As soon as Jessica stepped over to the older group, the two college-age counselors running our Youth Division—a sunburned physical-education major named Brad and an elementary-education sophomore named Kelly—walked down our line with their wooden clipboards, determined to organize us by textbook summer-camp socialization rules.

"All right, Youth Division, let's buddy up in two lines for the walk across Dominion Mall!" Counselor Brad announced cheerfully over the rush of the circular plaza fountain. He looked down at Rachel's and my locked hands, then glanced two paces behind us, where another brother and sister—eight-year-old platinum-blonde fraternal twins named [Nathaniel and Katie Prairie](https://books.raggiesoft.com/katie/early-childhood/summer-camp-at-virginia-dominion-university/the-parallel-pair-dominion-mall-the-vdu-fieldhouse)—were standing just as close together, Katie's fingers wrapped tightly around her twin brother's wrist.

Brad grinned at Kelly, convinced he had just spotted the easiest social-mixing equation of the summer.

"Look at this—we've got two brothers and two sisters right next to each other!" Brad said brightly, tapping his clipboard. "Let's mix it up today so the boys can hang out with the boys and the girls can hang out with the girls! Michael, step right back here and pair up with Nathaniel! And Rachel, you team up with Katie!"

Counselor Kelly gently guided Rachel's shoulder two steps toward Katie Prairie, while Brad motioned for eight-year-old Nathaniel—whose short platinum-blonde hair was the exact same pale shade as his twin sister's long straight hair—to step up beside me.

For about four seconds, I stood next to Nathaniel while Rachel stood next to Katie.

I looked sideways at Nathaniel. Nathaniel looked sideways at me through the morning humidity. Behind us, Katie Prairie wasn't talking to Rachel, and Rachel wasn't talking to Katie; both girls had gone completely rigid, staring straight at the backs of their brothers' camp shirts with identical expressions of silent, rising panic. Like Rachel and me, Nathaniel and Katie were autistic, and like Rachel and me, they operated as a single, indivisible pair.

Neither of us yelled, and neither of us argued with the counselors. We didn't have the vocabulary to explain to two twenty-year-old college students that trading one sister for another girl—and trading one brother for another boy—didn't satisfy the equation at all. Instead, as soon as Brad turned around and raised his wooden clipboard in the air to lead the fifteen of us out across the broad, light-gray concrete expanse of Dominion Mall, Nathaniel and I gave each other a single, silent nod of total agreement.

I took one half-step to my right, and Nathaniel took one half-step to his left. Simultaneously, Rachel slipped past Katie's elbow to reclaim my left hand, and Katie stepped straight up beside Nathaniel to lock her fingers back around his arm.

Before our group had even crossed the second concrete expansion joint past the circular fountain, both brother-and-sister pairs had completely reassembled themselves. Up at the front of the column, Counselor Kelly glanced back over her shoulder to check the headcount, saw Michael with Rachel on the left and Nathaniel with Katie on the right, and blinked in mild bewilderment.

## 9:30 AM — The Computer Lab: *Wagon Train West* & *Digit Chompers*

Our first ninety-minute rotation of the morning took us out of the rising Tidewater humidity and through the heavy steel doors of the campus computer lab.

Inside, the windowless room was chilled to sixty-eight degrees and hummed with the steady, rhythmic cooling fans of thirty beige desktop towers and curved glass CRT monitors. For a nine-year-old brain that craved order, the computer lab was paradise—until the counselors tried to separate us a second time.

"Okay, campers, listen up!" Counselor Brad called out from the instructor's desk at the front of the room, holding up a plastic storage box of 3.5-inch floppy disks. "Boys on the left wall row—we've got *Wagon Train West* and *Where on the Map Is Scarlet Devereaux?* loaded up! Girls on the right wall row for *Digit Chompers* and *PixelPaint Junior*!"

Once again, the camp staff was determined to enforce traditional playground geometry: boys with boys on one side of the room, girls with girls on the other.

Reluctantly, I let go of Rachel's hand and sat down at Terminal 4 on the left wall, while Brad guided Nathaniel Prairie into the chair right next to me at Terminal 5. Across the blue low-pile carpet, Counselor Kelly led Rachel and Katie Prairie to Terminals 19 and 20 on the right wall.

Within ninety seconds, the system began to fail on both sides of the room at once.

Two seats to my left, a loud nine-year-old boy named Tyler was aggressively slamming the mechanical *KeyMech* spacebar with both thumbs, shouting at his neighbor to buy forty boxes of ammunition instead of spare wagon axles while their pixelated ox team sank to the bottom of a river in *Wagon Train West*. My chest tightened as the overlapping shouting bounced off the cinder-block walls. Beside me at Terminal 5, Nathaniel wasn't touching his keyboard either; he was turned halfway around in his plastic chair, keeping his eyes locked across the aisle on his twin sister.

I turned my head to look across the center carpet.

At Terminals 19 and 20, the brightly colored five-by-five number grids of *Digit Chompers* were flashing on the monitors while little animated *Glitchers* crept across the squares. Neither Rachel nor Katie had touched a single key. Rachel had pulled both of her hands completely off the desk, tucking her knuckles tightly against her ribs with her chin dropped toward her royal-blue camp shirt, while Katie sat frozen beside her, twisting the hem of her shirt around her fingers and staring back across the aisle at Nathaniel.

I didn't wait for permission, and neither did Nathaniel. As soon as Counselor Brad knelt down at the front printer to clear a paper jam in the continuous-feed dot-matrix tractor, I pushed my rolling plastic chair back six inches and looked straight at Rachel, while Nathaniel tapped the empty edge of his desk at Terminal 5 and motioned to Katie.

Both girls moved at the exact same second.

Without making a sound, Rachel and Katie slid off their chairs on the girls' row, walked three quiet steps side by side across the carpeted aisle, and crossed straight over to the boys' wall. Rachel squeezed onto the right half of my chair at Terminal 4, pressing her left shoulder solidly against my right ribs, while six feet away, Katie pulled the spare rolling chair at the end of the row flush against Nathaniel's side at Terminal 5.

"Hey, girls, your computers are still running over on the right wall—" Counselor Kelly started, walking down the center aisle with a stack of floppy disks in her hand before stopping dead in her tracks as she looked at the four of us.

![Inside the 1994 Virginia Dominion University computer lab, nine-year-old Michael and seven-year-old Rachel share Terminal 4 while eight-year-old fraternal twins Nathaniel and Katie Prairie share Terminal 5 beside them in matching royal-blue VDU Summer Camp shirts]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/katie/b001/jul_1994_vdu_summer_camp_two_sibling_pairs_computer_lab.jpg)

"She's playing with me," I said quickly, my voice quiet but completely firm as I exited the noisy frontier screen and launched *Digit Chompers* from the desktop menu on my own monitor. I shifted six inches to the left so Rachel had half the seat, rested my left hand over the arrow keys, and placed Rachel's right hand directly over the wide spacebar. "I steer the square, and Rachel chomps the multiples. That's how we play."

Right beside us at Terminal 5, Nathaniel had already handed Katie the mouse for *Wagon Train West* while keeping his own hands on the keyboard, her long platinum-blonde hair brushing his shoulder as she leaned in against him.

"Katie's my twin," Nathaniel told Kelly with quiet, unbudging certainty. "We always play together."

Counselor Kelly stood in the middle of the aisle, looking from Rachel and me at Terminal 4 to Nathaniel and Katie at Terminal 5. The rigid tension had vanished from all four of us the instant we were back with our siblings. On my screen, as I tapped the right arrow key twice to move the green sprite onto the number twelve, Rachel hit the spacebar with a crisp, satisfied *clack*, clearing the square half a second before a purple *Glitcher* could reach us.

Kelly glanced back at Brad by the printer, then looked down at the clipboard tucked under her arm. Slowly, she let out a small breath and offered a gentle nod.

"Okay," Kelly said softly, pulling a second spare chair over so Rachel had her own seat next to mine. "Michael and Rachel on Terminal 4, Nathaniel and Katie on Terminal 5."

Even with her own chair right beside mine, Rachel dragged the casters until the plastic armrests clicked flush together—just like Katie had done next to Nathaniel—keeping our shoulders touching for the entire ninety-minute block as we cleared level after level of *Digit Chompers* and traded turns navigating our covered wagon across the continent in *Wagon Train West* without losing a single axle.

## 12:00 PM — Monarch Commons & The Written Exception

By noon, when we stepped back out of the science building onto Dominion Mall, the July sun was blazing straight down onto the concrete squares.

"Stay together on the diagonal path, Youth Division!" Brad called out, leading us past the flat-roofed precast-concrete and blue-glass facade of the University Library toward the wide glass double doors of Monarch Commons.

Inside the sprawling dining hall, more than two hundred campers from every age bracket—from six-year-old first-graders up to the fourteen-year-old Senior Division—were filing through the lunch lines with plastic trays of grilled cheese sandwiches, apple slices, cracker packets, and small cardboard cartons of milk.

Across the tiled floor, the Senior Division counselors were herding their fourteen-year-old campers toward a row of high-top tables near the far windows. A tall male counselor wearing a whistle around his neck stepped in front of Jessica as she came out of the lunch line with her tray.

"Senior Division is over by the glass wall, Jessica," the counselor said, gesturing toward the noisy cluster of teenagers.

Jessica didn't flinch. Adjusting her clear wire-rimmed glasses with one finger while balancing her tray on her forearm, she nodded politely toward the camp director's master clipboard resting on the check-in podium ten feet away.

"I have a written lunch exception on file," Jessica stated calmly, using that exact, unshakable big-sister authority that had dismantled Dad's holiday bedroom rules back in December. "My brother Michael and my sister Rachel are in the Youth Division at Table 6. My mom and the camp director signed off on me eating lunch with them every day."

The counselor blinked, walked three steps over to the podium, and flipped the top sheet of the master roster. Right there in the margins, highlighted in bright yellow marker above Mom's signature, was the standing directive: *Ragsdale, Jessica (Age 14, Senior Div.) — Documented permission to sit with siblings Michael (9) & Rachel (7) during 12:00–1:00 PM lunch block.*

"Got it—go right ahead," the counselor said, stepping aside.

Ten seconds later, Jessica set her red plastic tray down right across from Rachel and me at the end of Table 6, just a few seats down from where Nathaniel and Katie Prairie were quietly sharing their sandwiches shoulder-to-shoulder. While the other kids at the long folding table were loudly debating cartoons and trading foil-wrapped snack cakes, the three of us formed our own quiet perimeter at the corner. Automatically, Jessica slid her extra cellophane packet of saltine crackers across the table to me, and I traded her my cup of apple slices while Rachel leaned her hip against my leg and sipped her milk carton.

"Did they try to split you two up again this morning?" Jessica asked quietly, unwrapping her sandwich.

"Twice," I told her, crunching on a saltine and nodding slightly down the bench toward the platinum-blonde twins. "On the walk across the plaza, and in the computer lab. They tried to pair me with Nathaniel and Rachel with Katie so it would be boys with boys and girls with girls. Neither of us stayed put."

Jessica glanced down the table at Nathaniel and Katie—who were currently trading the crusts off their grilled cheese sandwiches in total, synchronized quiet—and a warm, knowing smile crossed her face.

"Good," Jessica said firmly, looking back at Rachel and me. "Don't let them talk you into splitting up just because their clipboard says boys go in one line and girls go in another. Next summer I'm going to be fifteen, which means I age out of this camp and I won't be here at noon to check on you guys. So over the next two weeks, we're making sure the two of you know every single building and shortcut on this campus by heart."

Pulling a folded paper napkin out of her tray, Jessica used the blunt tip of a pencil from her backpack to sketch the rectangular concrete grid of Dominion Mall between our milk cartons.

"Okay, quiz time," she said, tapping the center of the napkin. "We're inside Monarch Commons right now. If the heat index goes past ninety-five after lunch and your calves start getting tight on the walk back from the science building, where is the closest shaded bench with mist from the water jets?"

"Right outside the front glass doors, under the live oaks and crape myrtles on the west edge of the circular fountain," I answered immediately.

"And what's the building with the blue-tinted glass walls across the diagonal walkway?" Jessica asked, looking at Rachel.

"The library," Rachel whispered—her voice barely above a breath, reserved strictly for me and Jessica where none of the other campers could hear her. "And the arts building is on the corner."

"Exactly," Jessica smiled, folding the napkin neatly and tucking it into my pocket. "And after lunch, your group is walking down Hampton Boulevard to the VDU Fieldhouse for swimming. The Fieldhouse is where the Lady Admirals play women's basketball during the school year—the men's team plays downtown under the dome at the Norfolk Prism, so the Fieldhouse belongs to women's basketball and the indoor pool. Stay together in the shallow end, and I'll meet you both back at the pick-up curb at four."

## 1:30 PM — The VDU Fieldhouse Pool & The Buddy Board

At one-fifteen, our Youth Division marched down the concrete sidewalk toward the VDU Fieldhouse.

Compared to the glass-and-precast academic buildings lining Dominion Mall, the Fieldhouse was a massive, functional 1970s brick-and-steel athletic fortress. As we walked down the wide rubber-floored corridor past the trophy cases displaying Lady Admirals women's basketball banners, the sharp, unmistakable scent of warm chlorine and indoor humidity drifted out from the double doors of the Fieldhouse Natatorium.

Ten minutes later, after changing in the locker rooms, I stepped out onto the wet white tile of the pool deck wearing my navy swim trunks and a white camp swim wristband. My calves and heels were always stiff on bare tile, forcing me into a careful, slightly flat-footed walk along the edge of the deck. Almost immediately, the girls' locker room door on the far side of the tiled wall swung open, and Rachel and Katie Prairie stepped out in their navy one-piece swimsuits with their camp towels wrapped around their shoulders.

The acoustics inside the Fieldhouse Natatorium were ten times louder than Monarch Commons. Every splash, shout, and whistle bounced off the cinder-block walls and the high steel ceiling trusses, turning the room into a roaring echo chamber.

Neither girl even glanced at the line of seven- and eight-year-old girls waiting by the steps. Rachel walked straight across the wet tile to grab my left hand, and Katie walked right beside her to grab Nathaniel's hand on the bench next to ours.

"All right, Youth Division, line up along the wall with your swim buddies before anyone gets in the water!" Counselor Brad shouted, holding a thick black grease pencil beside a white plastic Buddy Board mounted on a metal easel. Force of habit made him try the boy-and-girl split one final time. "Every fifteen minutes when the lifeguard blows the long whistle and yells *Buddy Check*, you and your buddy have to find each other in the shallow end, grab hands, and hold them up in the air until we count every pair! Michael and Nathaniel, you guys are Pair Four! Rachel and Katie, you two are Pair Nine!"

Up on the deck, Counselor Kelly looked at the four of us standing along the tile coping and opened her mouth to nudge Rachel toward Katie.

Rachel's fingers dug into my hand so hard her knuckles turned white, and three feet to my right, Katie Prairie had both hands locked around Nathaniel's forearm as the echoing roar of thirty kids hitting the water bounced off the ceiling trusses.

I didn't let go of Rachel's hand, and Nathaniel didn't let go of Katie's. Keeping my left fingers laced tightly through my sister's, I walked carefully down the wide submerged fiberglass steps into the warm, waist-deep water of the three-foot shallow end, bringing Rachel right down into the corner lane beside me while Nathaniel and Katie waded into the shallow water four feet away.

The ninety-four-degree water of the Fieldhouse pool felt incredible against my tight calves, taking the gravity and stiffness right out of my heels. Beside me, with the warm water up to her chest and my arm anchored solidly against hers, Rachel let out a slow breath and rested her chin near my shoulder.

*TWEEEEET!*

The head lifeguard on the elevated white chair blew a long, piercing blast on his silver whistle to test the drill on our first lap in the pool.

"BUDDY CHECK!" the lifeguard barked through his red plastic megaphone. "Find your buddy and hands in the air!"

All across the shallow end, kids who had been paired up with same-age classmates scrambled and splashed wildly through the churning water, trying to remember who they had been assigned to two minutes earlier. Half the boys playing *Admirals and Anchors* pool tag in the middle of the lane couldn't find their partners at all.

Along the shallow-end wall, the four of us didn't have to move a single inch. Our hands were already locked together beneath the surface. Before the echo of the whistle had even faded from the steel rafters of the Fieldhouse, I raised my left arm with Rachel's right arm, and right beside us, Nathaniel raised his right arm with Katie's left arm—lifting two pairs of tightly clasped brother-and-sister hands straight into the air above our heads.

We were the very first two pairs up in the entire pool.

Counselor Brad stared at our raised hands, looked at the rest of the Youth Division still splashing around in confusion ten feet away, and then looked at Counselor Kelly, who was struggling not to laugh behind her clipboard. Slowly, Brad wiped a rag across Pair Four and Pair Nine on the white plastic board, picked up his black grease pencil, and wrote two new lines at the very top of the column:

`PAIR 1: MICHAEL + RACHEL`
`PAIR 2: NATHANIEL + KATIE`

For the rest of the hour-long swim block—and for every single afternoon at the VDU Fieldhouse pool across the entire two-week session—nobody tried to assign us separate swim buddies again. While the rest of the kids chased each other back and forth across the ropes playing *Admirals and Anchors*, Rachel and I stayed together in the warm shallow lane alongside Nathaniel and Katie, kicking with kickboards and raising our joined hands on the first note of every fifteen-minute whistle.

## 3:30 PM — *Four-in-a-Row Grid*, *Wild Draw*, & The 4:00 PM Loop

By three-thirty, dried off and dressed back in our royal-blue VDU Summer Camp t-shirts and sneakers, our group gathered inside the air-conditioned multipurpose room on the ground floor of the Fieldhouse for the final cool-down rotation of the day.

Long folding tables were stacked with plastic board games and card decks to keep everyone quiet until parent pick-up. Even here, the counselors casually invited Michael to join the boys' *Four-in-a-Row Grid* tournament table and invited Rachel over to the girls' circle coloring camp banners.

And just like at nine o'clock, nine-thirty, and one-thirty, the two of us quietly drifted right back to the same corner bench.

Sitting hip-to-hip at the end of the table with our damp hair still smelling faintly of the Fieldhouse pool, Rachel and I set up our own yellow-and-red plastic *Four-in-a-Row Grid* rack and dealt out a two-player hand of *Wild Draw* cards between us. We didn't need to talk out loud to play; Rachel knew every pattern I liked to build with the red plastic checkers, and I knew to save my blue color-change cards whenever her hand got low. From the doorway, Brad and Kelly watched us drop checkers into the plastic grid in total, synchronized silence, finally accepting that wherever Michael Ragsdale went, Rachel Ragsdale was permanently attached to his side.

At three-forty-five, we packed our water bottles into our backpacks and walked back out into the late-afternoon sunlight, following the diagonal concrete walkways across Dominion Mall toward the Hampton Boulevard loop.

As our sneakers clicked across the expansion joints past the circular plaza fountain and the blue-glass facade of the University Library, we weren't just walking behind the counselor's whistle anymore. Together with Jessica's lunchtime napkin quizzes, we were storing every building, every shaded bench beneath the crape myrtles, and every flat concrete path from Monarch Commons to the Fieldhouse inside our shared memory.

Up ahead at the edge of the pick-up curb, fourteen-year-old Jessica was already waiting beside the Senior Division sign, her clear wire-rimmed glasses glinting in the afternoon sun as she spotted us and stepped over to rejoin our line.

Beside me, as the sun slanted across the water jets of the circular fountain, Rachel leaned her shoulder against my arm. Her small seven-year-old fingers were still laced securely through mine.

"When we grow up and go to college," Rachel whispered, looking back across the wide concrete plaza toward the library and Monarch Commons, "we already know where all the buildings are, Michael."

"Every single one of them, Rach," I said, giving her hand a gentle squeeze as Mom's station wagon pulled into view at the end of the four o'clock pick-up loop and fourteen-year-old Jessica waved us toward the curb.
