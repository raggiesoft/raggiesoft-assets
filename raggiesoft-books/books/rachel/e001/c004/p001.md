## 8:45 AM — Curbside Drop-Off & The Two-Week Block

The morning humidity coming off the Elizabeth River already smelled like warm concrete and salt water when Mom eased our station wagon up to the drop-off curb off Hampton Boulevard.

"Have a good day, stay hydrated, and keep your water bottles in your packs," Mom called out through the open passenger window as the three of us stepped onto the wide sidewalk at the edge of Virginia Dominion University. "I'll be right back at this exact loop at four o'clock."

"We've got it, Mom," fourteen-year-old Jessica promised, adjusting her clear wire-rimmed glasses and settling her canvas backpack over both shoulders. Before the station wagon had even merged back into the Norfolk traffic, my older sister stepped straight into position on my left flank, while seven-year-old Rachel reached up with her right hand and laced her fingers tightly through mine.

We were on the second morning of a two-week, all-day block at the VDU Junior Admirals Summer Camp—running from nine o'clock in the morning until four o'clock in the afternoon, Monday through Friday. Just like [at home in the bottom bunk on Delaney Street](https://books.raggiesoft.com/rachel/the-delaney-street-years/the-lower-bunk/sanctuary-the-new-arrangement), where Rachel and I stayed side by side to keep the static quiet, the three of us operated as a single unit the second our sneakers touched the pavement.

This two-week session carried a quiet weight that all three of us understood. At fourteen, rising into her freshman year of high school, Jessica stood at the absolute ceiling of the camp's age bracket. After this two-week block ended, she would officially age out of the youth summer camp system forever. And because she was fourteen, the camp roster placed her in the Senior Division with the twelve-to-fourteen-year-old campers for all of the morning and afternoon rotations, while nine-year-old me and seven-year-old Rachel were assigned to the Youth Division.

Before Mom had ever signed the tuition check, however, Jessica had insisted on one non-negotiable condition—and Mom had made sure the camp director documented it in writing on our official registration forms. Every day from twelve o'clock to one o'clock, when all of the divisions marched into the Monarch Commons cafeteria, Jessica had written permission to leave the older campers and sit at the Youth Division tables with her younger brother and sister.

![Nine-year-old Michael, seven-year-old Rachel holding hands, and fourteen-year-old Jessica walking with their VDU Youth Summer Camp counselor and fellow campers across the concrete plaza of Dominion Mall at Virginia Dominion University in July 1994]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/rachel/b001/jul_1994_vdu_childhood_summer_camp_michael_rachel_jessica.jpg)

"I'll see you guys inside Monarch Commons at noon," Jessica told us as the Senior Division counselor blew a short whistle near the check-in tables. She gave my shoulder a quick, grounding squeeze and brushed a stray lock of hair behind Rachel's ear. "Remember the landmarks on the walk over."

As soon as Jessica stepped over to the older group, the two college-age counselors running our Youth Division—a sunburned physical-education major named Brad and an elementary-education sophomore named Kelly—walked down our line with their wooden clipboards, determined to organize us by textbook summer-camp socialization rules.

"All right, Youth Division, let's buddy up in two lines for the walk across Dominion Mall!" Counselor Brad announced cheerfully over the rush of the circular plaza fountain. He looked straight down at our locked hands and grinned, assuming we were just clinging to each other out of second-day shyness. "Let's mix it up by age group today so everybody makes a friend in their own grade! Michael, you're nine, right? Step right up here next to Tyler. And Rachel, come on back here with Katie and the seven-year-old girls!"

Tyler was a loud nine-year-old boy in a Charlotte basketball cap who was currently swinging his metal insulated thermos like a pendulum. Four paces behind us, Katie was a seven-year-old girl in pink plastic sandals who was chattering nonstop at three other girls about her gymnastics ribbons.

Counselor Kelly gently guided Rachel's shoulder two steps back toward Katie, while Brad tapped my backpack to nudge me alongside Tyler.

Neither Rachel nor I yelled, and neither of us argued with the adults. We didn't have the vocabulary to explain to two twenty-year-old college students that splitting us into a boy pair and a girl pair didn't make us "social"—it just cut our tether in half in the middle of an echoing concrete plaza. Instead, as soon as Brad turned around and raised his wooden clipboard in the air to lead the fifteen of us out across the broad, light-gray concrete expanse of Dominion Mall, I slowed my stride by half a step. Simultaneously, Rachel slipped quietly around Katie's elbow and took two quick steps forward.

Before our group had even crossed the second concrete expansion joint past the circular fountain, Rachel's small right hand slid right back into my left palm, her fingers locking tightly between mine.

Up at the front of the line, Counselor Kelly glanced back over her shoulder to check the headcount, saw the two of us walking hip-to-hip again right in the middle of the column while Tyler and Katie talked across the gap behind us, and blinked in mild bewilderment.

## 9:30 AM — The Computer Lab: *Wagon Train West* & *Digit Chompers*

Our first ninety-minute rotation of the morning took us out of the rising Tidewater humidity and through the heavy steel doors of the campus computer lab.

Inside, the windowless room was chilled to sixty-eight degrees and hummed with the steady, rhythmic cooling fans of thirty beige desktop towers and curved glass CRT monitors. For a nine-year-old brain that craved order, the computer lab was paradise—until the counselors tried to separate us a second time.

"Okay, campers, listen up!" Counselor Brad called out from the instructor's desk at the front of the room, holding up a plastic storage box of 3.5-inch floppy disks. "Boys ages nine and ten, take the left wall row—we've got *Wagon Train West* and *Where on the Map Is Scarlet Devereaux?* loaded up! Girls ages seven and eight, take the right wall row for *Digit Chompers* and *PixelPaint Junior*!"

Once again, the camp staff was operating on the standard assumption that nine-year-old boys wanted to sit with nine-year-old boys and seven-year-old girls wanted to sit with seven-year-old girls.

Reluctantly, I let go of Rachel's hand and sat down at Terminal 4 on the left wall between Tyler and another nine-year-old boy named Marcus. Across the blue low-pile carpet, Counselor Kelly led Rachel to Terminal 19 on the right wall beside Katie.

Within ninety seconds, the system began to fail.

To my left, Tyler was aggressively slamming the mechanical *KeyMech* spacebar with both thumbs, shouting at Marcus to buy forty boxes of ammunition instead of spare wagon axles while their pixelated ox team sank to the bottom of a river in *Wagon Train West*. My chest tightened as the overlapping shouting bounced off the cinder-block walls. I stared at the green-and-white supply screen on my monitor, my fingers hovering stiffly above the keyboard without typing a single number.

I turned my head to look across the center aisle.

At Terminal 19, the brightly colored five-by-five number grid of *Digit Chompers* was flashing on Rachel's screen while little animated *Glitchers* crept across the squares toward her green chomper sprite. Katie was leaning over from the next chair trying to point at the screen, and Rachel had pulled both of her hands completely off the desk, tucking her knuckles tightly against her ribs with her chin dropped toward her royal-blue camp shirt. She wasn't touching the arrow keys, and she wasn't making a sound.

I didn't wait for permission. As soon as Counselor Brad knelt down at the front printer to clear a paper jam in the continuous-feed dot-matrix tractor, I pushed my rolling plastic chair back six inches and looked straight at my sister.

Rachel caught my eye instantly. Without making a sound, she slid off her chair on the girls' row, walked three quiet steps across the carpeted aisle, and squeezed right onto the right half of my wide office chair at Terminal 4, pressing her left shoulder solidly against my right ribs.

"Hey, Rachel, honey, your computer is still running over on the girls' side with Katie—" Counselor Kelly started, walking down the center aisle with a stack of floppy disks in her hand.

"She's playing with me," I said quickly, my voice quiet but completely firm as I exited the noisy frontier screen and launched *Digit Chompers* from the desktop menu on my own monitor. I shifted six inches to the left so Rachel had half the seat, rested my left hand over the arrow keys, and placed Rachel's right hand directly over the wide spacebar. "I steer the square, and Rachel chomps the multiples. That's how we play."

Counselor Kelly stopped beside our desk, her mouth open to repeat the rule about staying in our age-and-gender rows. Then she looked down at Rachel. The rigid tension had vanished from my little sister's shoulders the exact second her side pressed against mine. Her thumb was resting calmly on the spacebar, and as I tapped the right arrow key twice to move the green sprite onto the number twelve, Rachel hit the spacebar with a crisp, satisfied *clack*, clearing the square half a second before a purple *Glitcher* could reach us.

Kelly glanced back at Brad by the printer, then looked down at the clipboard tucked under her arm where Mom's intake notes were clipped behind the roster. Slowly, she let out a small breath and offered a gentle nod.

"Okay," Kelly said softly, pulling the empty spare chair from the end of the row and setting it right up against mine so Rachel didn't have to balance on half a seat. "You two can team up on Terminal 4."

Even with her own chair right beside mine, Rachel dragged the casters until the plastic armrests clicked flush together, keeping our shoulders touching for the entire ninety-minute block as we cleared level after level of *Digit Chompers* and traded turns navigating our covered wagon all the way across the continent in *Wagon Train West* without losing a single axle.

## 12:00 PM — Monarch Commons & The Written Exception

By noon, when we stepped back out of the science building onto Dominion Mall, the July sun was blazing straight down onto the concrete squares.

"Stay together on the diagonal path, Youth Division!" Brad called out, leading us past the flat-roofed precast-concrete and blue-glass facade of the University Library toward the wide glass double doors of Monarch Commons.

Inside the sprawling dining hall, more than two hundred campers from every age bracket—from six-year-old first-graders up to the fourteen-year-old Senior Division—were filing through the lunch lines with plastic trays of grilled cheese sandwiches, apple slices, cracker packets, and small cardboard cartons of milk.

Across the tiled floor, the Senior Division counselors were herding their fourteen-year-old campers toward a row of high-top tables near the far windows. A tall male counselor wearing a whistle around his neck stepped in front of Jessica as she came out of the lunch line with her tray.

"Senior Division is over by the glass wall, Jessica," the counselor said, gesturing toward the noisy cluster of teenagers.

Jessica didn't flinch. Adjusting her clear wire-rimmed glasses with one finger while balancing her tray on her forearm, she nodded politely toward the camp director's master clipboard resting on the check-in podium ten feet away.

"I have a written lunch exception on file," Jessica stated calmly, using that exact, unshakable big-sister authority that had dismantled Dad's holiday bedroom rules back in December. "My brother Michael and my sister Rachel are in the Youth Division at Table 6. My mom and the camp director signed off on me eating lunch with them every day."

The counselor blinked, walked three steps over to the podium, and flipped the top sheet of the master roster. Right there in the margins, highlighted in bright yellow marker above Mom's signature, was the standing directive: *Ragsdale, Jessica (Age 14, Senior Div.) — Documented permission to sit with siblings Michael (9) & Rachel (7) during 12:00–1:00 PM lunch block.*

"Got it—go right ahead," the counselor said, stepping aside.

Ten seconds later, Jessica set her red plastic tray down right across from Rachel and me at the end of Table 6. While the other kids at the long folding table were loudly debating cartoons and trading foil-wrapped snack cakes, the three of us formed our own quiet perimeter at the corner. Automatically, Jessica slid her extra cellophane packet of saltine crackers across the table to me, and I traded her my cup of apple slices while Rachel leaned her hip against my leg and sipped her milk carton.

"Did they try to split you two up again this morning?" Jessica asked quietly, unwrapping her sandwich.

"Twice," I told her, crunching on a saltine. "On the walk across the plaza, and in the computer lab. They wanted me to sit with Tyler on the boys' row and Rachel to sit with Katie on the girls' row."

Rachel shook her head once and hooked her left pinky finger around my wrist on the tabletop.

"Good," Jessica said firmly, looking from me to Rachel. "Don't let them talk you into splitting up just because their clipboard says nine-year-olds go in one line and seven-year-olds go in another. Next summer I'm going to be fifteen, which means I age out of this camp and I won't be here at noon to check on you guys. So over the next two weeks, we're making sure the two of you know every single building and shortcut on this campus by heart."

Pulling a folded paper napkin out of her tray, Jessica used the blunt tip of a pencil from her backpack to sketch the rectangular concrete grid of Dominion Mall between our milk cartons.

"Okay, quiz time," she said, tapping the center of the napkin. "We're inside Monarch Commons right now. If the heat index goes past ninety-five after lunch and your calves start getting tight on the walk back from the science building, where is the closest shaded bench with mist from the water jets?"

"Right outside the front glass doors, under the live oaks and crape myrtles on the west edge of the circular fountain," I answered immediately.

"And what's the building with the blue-tinted glass walls across the diagonal walkway?" Jessica asked, looking at Rachel.

"The library," Rachel whispered—her voice barely above a breath, reserved strictly for me and Jessica where none of the other campers could hear her. "And the arts building is on the corner."

"Exactly," Jessica smiled, folding the napkin neatly and tucking it into my pocket. "And after lunch, your group is walking down Hampton Boulevard to the VDU Fieldhouse for swimming. The Fieldhouse is where the Lady Admirals play women's basketball during the school year—the men's team plays downtown under the dome at the Norfolk Prism, so the Fieldhouse belongs to women's basketball and the indoor pool. Stay together in the shallow end, and I'll meet you both back at the pick-up curb at four."

## 1:30 PM — The VDU Fieldhouse Pool & The Buddy Board

At one-fifteen, our Youth Division marched down the concrete sidewalk toward the VDU Fieldhouse.

Compared to the glass-and-precast academic buildings lining Dominion Mall, the Fieldhouse was a massive, functional 1970s brick-and-steel athletic fortress. As we walked down the wide rubber-floored corridor past the trophy cases displaying Lady Admirals women's basketball banners, the sharp, unmistakable scent of warm chlorine and indoor humidity drifted out from the double doors of the Fieldhouse Natatorium.

Ten minutes later, after changing in the locker rooms, I stepped out onto the wet white tile of the pool deck wearing my navy swim trunks and a white camp swim wristband. My calves and heels were always stiff on bare tile, forcing me into a careful, slightly flat-footed walk along the edge of the deck. Almost immediately, the locker room door on the far side of the tiled wall swung open, and Rachel stepped out in her one-piece navy swimsuit with her camp towel wrapped around her shoulders.

The acoustics inside the Fieldhouse Natatorium were ten times louder than Monarch Commons. Every splash, shout, and whistle bounced off the cinder-block walls and the high steel ceiling trusses, turning the room into a roaring echo chamber.

Rachel didn't even look at the line of seven-year-old girls waiting by the steps. She walked straight across the wet tile, dropped her towel on the bench beside mine, and grabbed my left hand with both of hers.

"All right, Youth Division, line up along the wall with your swim buddies before anyone gets in the water!" Counselor Brad shouted, holding a thick black grease pencil beside a white plastic Buddy Board mounted on a metal easel. "Every fifteen minutes when the lifeguard blows the long whistle and yells *Buddy Check*, you and your buddy have to find each other in the shallow end, grab hands, and hold them up in the air until we count every pair! Tyler, you and Michael are Pair Four! Katie, you and Rachel are Pair Nine!"

Tyler splashed down the wide fiberglass steps into the three-foot shallow end and immediately started doing underwater handstands six feet away from the wall. Up on the deck, Counselor Kelly looked at Rachel and pointed gently toward Katie, who was already sitting on the top submerged step waving at her.

"Go on, Rachel, get with Katie so I can write Pair Nine on the board," Kelly encouraged her.

Rachel's fingers dug into my hand so hard her knuckles turned white. The echoing roar of thirty kids hitting the water at once was already pushing her toward the edge of panic, and the thought of letting go of me in an echoing swimming pool was completely out of the question.

I didn't let go of her hand. Instead, keeping my left fingers laced tightly through hers, I walked carefully down the wide submerged steps into the warm, waist-deep water of the three-foot shallow end, bringing Rachel right down into the corner lane beside me.

The ninety-four-degree water of the Fieldhouse pool felt incredible against my tight calves, taking the gravity and stiffness right out of my heels. Beside me, with the warm water up to her chest and my arm anchored solidly against hers, Rachel let out a slow breath and rested her chin near my shoulder.

Up on the deck, Brad looked from Tyler—who was already halfway across the shallow end playing *Admirals and Anchors* pool tag with Marcus—over to Rachel and me standing side by side at the corner wall.

*TWEEEEET!*

The head lifeguard on the elevated white chair blew a long, piercing blast on his silver whistle to test the drill on our first lap in the pool.

"BUDDY CHECK!" the lifeguard barked through his red plastic megaphone. "Find your buddy and hands in the air!"

All across the shallow end, kids scrambled and splashed wildly through the churning water, trying to remember who they had been paired with two minutes earlier. Half the boys couldn't find their partners at all.

In the corner of the three-foot lane, Rachel and I didn't have to move a single inch. Our hands were already locked together beneath the surface. Before the echo of the whistle had even faded from the steel rafters of the Fieldhouse, I raised my left arm and Rachel raised her right arm, lifting our tightly clasped hands straight into the air above our heads.

We were the very first pair up in the entire pool.

Counselor Brad stared at our raised hands, looked at Tyler and Marcus scrambling to grab each other's wrists ten feet away, and then looked at Katie holding hands with another seven-year-old girl by the steps. Slowly, Brad wiped a rag across Pair Four and Pair Nine on the white plastic board, picked up his black grease pencil, and wrote a new line at the top of the column:

`PAIR 1: MICHAEL + RACHEL`

For the rest of the hour-long swim block—and for every single afternoon at the VDU Fieldhouse pool across the entire two-week session—nobody tried to assign us separate swim buddies again. While the rest of the kids chased each other back and forth across the ropes playing *Admirals and Anchors*, Rachel and I stayed together in the warm shallow lane, kicking side by side with kickboards and raising our joined hands on the first note of every fifteen-minute whistle.

## 3:30 PM — *Four-in-a-Row Grid*, *Wild Draw*, & The 4:00 PM Loop

By three-thirty, dried off and dressed back in our royal-blue VDU Summer Camp t-shirts and sneakers, our group gathered inside the air-conditioned multipurpose room on the ground floor of the Fieldhouse for the final cool-down rotation of the day.

Long folding tables were stacked with plastic board games and card decks to keep everyone quiet until parent pick-up. Even here, the counselors casually invited Michael to join the boys' *Four-in-a-Row Grid* tournament table and invited Rachel over to the girls' circle coloring camp banners.

And just like at nine o'clock, nine-thirty, and one-thirty, the two of us quietly drifted right back to the same corner bench.

Sitting hip-to-hip at the end of the table with our damp hair still smelling faintly of the Fieldhouse pool, Rachel and I set up our own yellow-and-red plastic *Four-in-a-Row Grid* rack and dealt out a two-player hand of *Wild Draw* cards between us. We didn't need to talk out loud to play; Rachel knew every pattern I liked to build with the red plastic checkers, and I knew to save my blue color-change cards whenever her hand got low. From the doorway, Brad and Kelly watched us drop checkers into the plastic grid in total, synchronized silence, finally accepting that wherever Michael Ragsdale went, Rachel Ragsdale was permanently attached to his side.

At three-forty-five, we packed our water bottles into our backpacks and walked back out into the late-afternoon sunlight, following the diagonal concrete walkways across Dominion Mall toward the Hampton Boulevard loop.

As our sneakers clicked across the expansion joints past the circular plaza fountain and the blue-glass facade of the University Library, we weren't just walking behind the counselor's whistle anymore. Together with Jessica's lunchtime napkin quizzes, we were storing every building, every shaded bench beneath the crape myrtles, and every flat concrete path from Monarch Commons to the Fieldhouse inside our shared memory.

Up ahead at the edge of the pick-up curb, fourteen-year-old Jessica was already waiting beside the Senior Division sign, her clear wire-rimmed glasses glinting in the afternoon sun as she spotted us and stepped over to rejoin our line.

Beside me, as the sun slanted across the water jets of the circular fountain, Rachel leaned her shoulder against my arm. Her small seven-year-old fingers were still laced securely through mine.

"When we grow up and go to college," Rachel whispered, looking back across the wide concrete plaza toward the library and Monarch Commons, "we already know where all the buildings are, Michael."

"Every single one of them, Rach," I said, giving her hand a gentle squeeze as Mom's station wagon pulled into view at the end of the four o'clock pick-up loop and fourteen-year-old Jessica waved us toward the curb.
