## Wednesday, September 17, 2003, 3:15 PM — An Empty Apartment Without Ashley & Crossing Paths With Apartment A6

![Inside a brightly lit Crown Burger near Kemps River Drive in Virginia Beach on Wednesday afternoon, September 17, 2003 as overcast gray clouds gather and the wind begins picking up in the dry trees outside the window ahead of Hurricane Isabel, eighteen-year-old Michael Ragsdale sits in a booth with his canvas backpack beside his cousin Maria over a tray of warm burgers and french fries.]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/rachel/b013/sep_2003_hurricane_isabel_kemps_river_crown_burger_michael_maria.jpg)

Unplugging the desktop computer tower in the corner of Unit A5 at three-fifteen on Wednesday afternoon, September 17, 2003, I lifted the case onto the middle of the Delaney Street pull-out sofa and stood still for a second in the quiet studio.

For the past three weeks, ever since Ashley had packed her bags at the end of August and left for her freshman semester away at college, the silence inside Unit A5 had felt like a physical weight pressing against my ribs.

Back in July and August, every square foot of this rectangular ground-floor studio had been calibrated for two people. When my Cerebral Palsy muscles tightened up in the morning, Ashley's shoulder was right there to absorb my weight. When the brick walls blocked my cell phone down to one bar, we walked out across Greenway Court to the grassy field together. Now, with Rachel and Jessica thousands of miles away in Washington State, Ashley gone to college, and Mom having driven over earlier that morning to pick up my one-eyed black Lab rescue Archie so he wouldn't be trapped in a Norfolk flood zone when Hurricane Isabel hit tomorrow, I was completely alone.

Outside the wide three-pane back window above the humming through-wall air conditioner, the September sky was already turning the color of wet slate as the outer bands of Hurricane Isabel pushed toward the Virginia coast. On the radio, Coastal Virginia Transit Authority had just announced that all bus service across Hampton Roads would be suspended starting tonight—and if I didn't walk up to Hampton Boulevard and catch the southbound Route 2 bus in the next ten minutes, I wouldn't make my transfers out to Kemps River Drive before the buses stopped running.

I zipped three changes of clothes and my phone charger into my canvas backpack, slung the straps over my shoulders, stepped out into the first-floor hallway, and turned the heavy brass deadbolt of Unit A5.

At that exact moment, the wooden door to Apartment A6—directly across the narrow back corridor from mine—opened, and [eighteen-year-old Nathaniel and Katie Prairie rolled out into the hallway in their compact black SwiftChair power wheelchairs with their father, Mr. Prairie](https://books.raggiesoft.com/katie/seventeen-senior-year-greenway-court/greenway-court-apartment-a6-the-square-closet-sanctuary/part-3-hurricane-isabel-two-layers-of-sandbags-at-greenway-court-two-weeks-at-dad-s-house-on-arvin-road), carrying a blue insulated cooler and a rolled-up navy-blue sleeping bag behind them.

Both Katie and Nathaniel looked up at me at once.

I didn't have to say a word about Ashley being gone. Both of them had seen Ashley leave at the end of August, and both of them knew what it meant to depend on one safe person to keep your nervous system from spinning out of control. As Katie reached her right hand across the gap between their wheelchairs and laced her fingers tightly through her brother's left hand, I could see the quiet, unmistakable sympathy in both of their faces.

Just like me,Katie and Nathaniel operated on strict sensory boundaries. They never liked being touched by anyone they didn't know—they gladly held each other's hands, hugged each other, and felt completely comfortable when their father rested his hands on their wheelchair push-handles to help maneuver their chairs around tight corners, while keeping a polite physical buffer from everyone else. They couldn't fix the hollow ache in my chest over Ashley any more than I could have fixed it if the two of them had ever been separated.

"Hey, Nathaniel. Hey, Katie, Mr. Prairie," I said quietly, shifting the strap of my backpack. "Are you guys heading out to your dad's house in Virginia Beach before the wind picks up?"

"Just locked up A6 and lifted the computer onto the bed," Nathaniel said warmly from his chair while Katie lifted her free hand in a gentle, sympathetic wave from her armrest. "Dad's taking us over to Arvin Road for the storm. Are you getting out of West Ghent before CVTA shuts the buses down tonight?"

"Yeah," I nodded. "I'm walking up to Hampton Boulevard right now to catch the Route 2 bus downtown, transferring to the Route 6, and then taking the Route 12 out to Kemps River Drive. My cousin Maria is meeting me there—she's going to take me to Crown Burger to grab food before everything closes, and then I'm staying at her house in the downstairs bedroom until the storm passes."

Right then, footsteps clattered down the interior staircase from the second floor, and the guy who lived in Apartment B5—directly above my unit—squeezed past us carrying two bags of ice and a case of beer back up toward the second floor.

"You first-floor folks are evacuating already?" the neighbor from B5 called down over the railing. "A bunch of us up on the second floor are throwing a hurricane party tonight! We've got candles, flashlights, and three coolers on the landing!"

Mr. Prairie shook his head with a dry smile as the upstairs door shut. "A hurricane party two blocks from the Elizabeth River with an eight-foot storm surge in the forecast. Come on—let's get down the ramp while the streets are still dry."

When the four of us reached the end of the first-floor hallway at the top of the lobby ramp, our landlords—Dave and Deb, the husband-and-wife couple who ran the building through their LLC—were working side by side in canvas gloves inside the front door, stacking two full layers of heavy burlap sandbags right across the top of the lobby ramp and the three shallow rubber-coated steps above the mailboxes.

While Dave left a narrow gap in the center of the sandbags at the top of the ramp for Mr. Prairie to guide Nathaniel's and Katie's power wheelchairs down the modular ramp past the lobby mailboxes and out the front door, I stepped carefully over the double layer of burlap sacks, walked down the three lobby steps, and pushed out through the glass-and-aluminum front door onto the dry sidewalk of Greenway Court.

"Stay safe at your cousin's house, Michael!" Katie called out, waving with Nathaniel from beside their dad's silver wheelchair-accessible minivan.

"You two stay safe at your dad's place too!" I called back, lifting a hand before turning up Greenway Court toward Redgate Avenue and Hampton Boulevard.

## Wednesday, September 17, 2003, 3:35 PM — CVTA Route 2 to Route 6 to Route 12, Crown Burger on Kemps River Drive, & Sharing Maria's Twin Bed

By the time I walked up to the bus stop on Hampton Boulevard, a high ceiling of gray clouds had spread across the sky and the northeast breeze coming off the Elizabeth River was starting to pick up, rustling the dry leaves along the curb and making the overhead traffic signals sway gently on their cables. The outer rainbands were still hours away to the south—they wouldn't roll in until overnight—but you could already feel the barometric pressure dropping in the air.

Five minutes later, the southbound Coastal Virginia Transit Authority Route 2 bus hissed to the curb. I stepped up into the warm diesel cabin, dropped my fare into the box, and took a window seat halfway back as the bus rolled south past the brick towers of Tidewater General Hospital toward Downtown Norfolk.

Because half the city was scrambling to get home before CVTA pulled its fleet off the streets for the hurricane, every transfer hub felt tense and hurried. Downtown, I stepped off the Route 2 and walked straight across the transfer plaza to board the outbound CVTA Route 6 bus, watching through the window as hardware store crews nailed sheets of plywood over storefront glass along the dry streets. Thirty-five minutes later, at the transfer point along Military Highway, I made my third and final connection of the afternoon—climbing aboard the outbound CVTA Route 12 bus heading down Indian River Road toward Kempsville.

As the Route 12 bus turned toward Kemps River Drive beneath the thickening gray clouds and stiffening wind, I pulled the stop cord and stepped down onto the dry sidewalk just as my cousin Maria pulled up to the curb in her sedan and flashed her headlights.

"Hop in before the wind gets any stronger!" Maria called out, popping the passenger door lock as I climbed into the front seat and set my backpack on the floorboard between my sneakers. "You look exhausted, Michael. Have you eaten anything since lunch?"

"Not yet," I admitted, leaning my head back against the headrest as the tension of the three-bus dash slowly began to ebb out of my legs.

"That's what I figured," Maria said warmly, putting the car in gear and steering across the dry shopping center lot toward the glowing red-and-gold sign of Crown Burger—one of the last fast-food spots near Kemps River Drive that hadn't taped a hurricane closure sign to its doors yet. "Come on, let's get you a double cheeseburger and hot fries while the fryers are still on, and then we'll be settled in at the house long before the rainbands hit tonight."

Ten minutes later, sitting across from Maria in a vinyl booth inside Crown Burger with a tray of hot burgers, salted french fries, and two large sodas between us while the rising pre-storm wind swayed the trees outside the dry plate-glass windows, I finally felt my shoulders drop an inch. Maria didn't press me when I got quiet halfway through my fries; she already knew Ashley had left for college three weeks ago, and she knew how quiet Unit A5 had been without her.

Later that night, well after Maria and I had arrived safely at her house and unpacked my backpack in the downstairs bedroom, the first heavy outer rainbands of Hurricane Isabel finally swept in off the Atlantic and began lashing against the dark windowpanes. All through Wednesday night and Thursday, September 18—when the full force of the hurricane slammed into Hampton Roads, toppling massive trees across Virginia Beach and knocking out electricity across the entire region—I stayed in the downstairs bedroom, sharing a narrow twin bed with my cousin Maria.

It wasn't the Delaney Street sleeper sofa in Unit A5 with Ashley, and my chest still ached every time I thought about how far away she was—but lying shoulder to shoulder beside Maria on that twin mattress in the dark downstairs bedroom while the hurricane gusts howled against the siding kept the worst of the isolation at bay until we finally fell asleep.

## The Two-Week Aftermath on Greenway Court: The Sandbags at the Top of the Lobby Ramp Held, An Island in the Surge, & The Midtown Tunnel Power Grid

Bus service across Norfolk and Virginia Beach stayed completely suspended for days after Hurricane Isabel passed, with streets blocked by downed century-old oaks and snapped utility poles from Kempsville all the way to the Elizabeth River.

When the roads finally cleared enough for us to drive into West Ghent and check on Greenway Court, I found out just how close a call our building had had.

Inside the front glass door, a thick brown line of dried river mud and debris marked the walls of the lower entrance lobby, and water had even gotten into the lobby mailboxes. During Thursday afternoon's peak storm surge, water—and a lot of it—had poured through the front entrance door into the lower lobby bay. Yet right at the top of the lobby ramp and the three rubber-coated steps, the two layers of heavy sandbags that Dave and Deb had stacked inside the doorway had held fast, stopping the floodwater cold and preventing a single drop from reaching the first-floor hallway or any of the downstairs apartments. Both Unit A5 and the Prairies' Apartment A6 across the hall were completely dry.

Up on the second-floor walkway, my upstairs neighbor from Apartment B5—who had ridden out the storm throwing that hurricane party with a few other upstairs tenants—leaned over the iron railing and told me what Thursday afternoon had looked like from the second floor.

"If you had stayed in the building, Michael, you would've been on an island," he told me, pointing out across Greenway Court and the grassy field where high-water drift lines still ringed the tree trunks. "At high tide, the river came all the way up over the street and surrounded the whole building on all four sides!"

Even though Unit A5 was dry, however, I couldn't move back in yet.

We lost power on Greenway Court for two full weeks.

Every evening as dusk fell over West Ghent, you could look right across the way and see Tidewater General Hospital—the region's only Level 1 Trauma Center—blazing with electricity because its priority grid had either stayed online or been restored immediately. Meanwhile, our side of Greenway Court sat pitch-black night after night. I could never officially confirm the engineering reason, but the word passed along by the utility crews and landlords was that our neighborhood's power grid was physically attached to the flooded Midtown Tunnel—which had filled with forty-four million gallons of Elizabeth River storm surge when its floodgate failed to close—and that it took Commonwealth Power crews two full weeks to figure out how to cut and isolate our grid so that we were separated from the Midtown Tunnel before the lights in Unit A5 and Apartment A6 finally came back on.
