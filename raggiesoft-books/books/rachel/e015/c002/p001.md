## 8:30 AM — The Deception at Churchland Square

The heavy rear liftgate of Robyn's dark blue Holt minivan popped open with a metallic clank. 

I stood in the damp, gray morning air of the Churchland Square parking lot, shivering as the coastal Virginia wind cut through my light jacket. I reached into the trunk and grabbed the heavy nylon straps of the two massive rolling suitcases, hauling them over the bumper and letting them hit the asphalt with a solid, unforgiving thud. 

The physical effort immediately sent a sharp, vibrating spike of spasticity down my right leg. I locked my knee to keep from buckling, my Cerebral Palsy instantly protesting the sudden, heavy load.

"Are you sure you both have everything you need?" Robyn asked from the driver's seat, leaning over to look out the passenger window. Delores's mother had a warm, unsuspecting smile on her face. "That's an awful lot of luggage just to go spend a few days with Ellen in Virginia Beach."

Delores didn't even blink. She stood on the curb, her arms crossed tight against the November chill, spinning a seamless, practiced lie to her own mother. 

"We just have some extra things Michael needs to drop off at Ellen's house," Delores lied smoothly, her voice utterly devoid of friction or guilt. "Don't worry about it. Just go. The CVTA bus is going to be here in a few minutes, and I don't want you sitting here idling in the cold."

"Alright," Robyn smiled, completely oblivious to the sheer scale of the deception happening right in front of her. "You kids have fun in Virginia Beach. Tell Ellen I said hello."

"I will," I managed to say, my throat tight. The words felt like swallowing glass. 

Robyn put the minivan in drive and pulled away, the red taillights fading as she merged onto High Street. 

I stood frozen next to the heavy luggage, watching the car disappear. The isolation crashed over me in a suffocating wave. Robyn had been our absolute last tether to reality. She genuinely believed she had just dropped us off to catch the local CVTA Route 47 bus so we could take a regional transfer down to the Virginia Beach oceanfront. 

She had no idea that we weren't going to Virginia Beach. 

She had no idea that Delores was actually dragging me on a grueling, cross-country Pathfinder bus to Seattle, Washington under the guise of a "vacation" to visit my sisters. 

"Stop staring at the street and grab the handles," Delores snapped, her voice instantly dropping the warm, familial facade the exact second her mother's car was out of sight. The venom was back, sharp and absolute. "The 47 is coming. We need to get downtown and transfer to Norfolk."

I looked down at the massive, heavy suitcases. My leg was already throbbing, and my chest felt impossibly tight. 

"Delores," I whispered, the sheer gravity of what was happening finally breaking through my frozen executive function. "Please. It's not too late. We can just call her back. We don't have to do this."

"Pick up the bags, Michael," she ordered, her eyes narrowing into cold, unyielding slits. "We are going to Washington. And you are going to carry the bags."

I didn't argue. The Gray Area Freeze locked my vocal cords, paralyzing my ability to fight back. I gripped the handles of the heavy luggage, my knuckles turning white, and dragged the crushing weight toward the CVTA bus shelter, resigning myself to the nightmare.

## 9:15 AM — The Monticello Arena Transfer

The CVTA Route 47 lumbered down High Street, a slow, thirty-minute crawl toward Downtown Portsmouth. I stared blankly out the vibrating window, my right leg burning from the earlier strain of the luggage. Delores sat rigidly next to me, her silence hanging heavy in the damp morning air. 

At the Downtown Portsmouth transit center, we hauled our massive suitcases off the 47 and immediately transferred to the Route 45. The diesel engine roared as the bus descended into the claustrophobic, yellow-tiled tube of the Downtown Tunnel, officially crossing beneath the Elizabeth River and surfacing into the sprawling concrete grid of Downtown Norfolk.

The ride was mercifully uneventful. The bus navigated the heavy downtown traffic and finally pulled up to the stop on Monticello Avenue, hissing to a halt directly in front of the massive, domed concrete architecture of The Monticello Arena.

We didn't get off. We stayed in our hard plastic seats as the driver adjusted the digital destination marquee on the front of the bus. 

With a soft mechanical click, the internal display at the front of the cabin shifted. The route through-routed. The 45 had become the Route 2. 

My breath caught in my throat.

The Route 2. It was my old bus route. It was the exact route I used to take every single day when I lived in West Ghent with my honorary sister, Ashley Raybourn. 

A sudden, violent wave of homesickness crashed over my processor. The phantom memory of Ashley's warm, fiercely protective presence hit me so hard my chest physically ached. I missed her desperately. I missed the safety of her apartment, the quiet evenings, and the absolute certainty that someone in this city actually cared if I survived the night. 

I swallowed hard, forcing my eyes to stay locked on the window, burying the emotion deep beneath a thick layer of encryption. I couldn't say a word. The cardinal rule of surviving Delores's psychological perimeter was that I was absolutely forbidden from expressing affection for another female. Her narcissistic jealousy was a hair-trigger. If I even hinted that I missed Ashley—that I was grieving the loss of my honorary sister—Delores would detonate. She would weaponize it, turning the rest of this grueling trip into a relentless, vindictive punishment. 

So I sat in silence, letting the Route 2 carry me through the damp morning. 

The bus finally hissed to a stop at the corner of Olney Road and Granby Street. 

"Get the bags," Delores ordered flatly, already standing up. 

I hauled the heavy suitcases off the bus, my arms aching as the rubber wheels hit the wet pavement. The massive, brutalist structure of the Norfolk Pathfinder terminal loomed just one block away. 

We dragged our luggage across Olney Road, the cold wind whipping off the Elizabeth River as we marched toward the heavy glass doors of the terminal. The trap had officially sprung. We were crossing the threshold into the 96-hour exodus.

## 9:30 AM — The Terminal Counter

The heavy glass doors of the Norfolk Pathfinder terminal slid open, hitting me with a wall of stale, climate-controlled air that smelled faintly of industrial floor wax and heavy diesel exhaust.

I dragged the two massive rolling suitcases across the threshold, my shoulder muscles burning from the sheer physical effort of navigating the uneven pavement on Olney Road. I pulled the bags toward a cluster of hard, unforgiving plastic chairs bolted to the scuffed linoleum floor and let the handles drop. I slumped down into one of the seats, rubbing my right thigh in a desperate attempt to coax the spasticity out of the muscle. 

The terminal was cavernous and deeply institutional, echoing with the ambient murmur of exhausted travelers and the occasional distorted crackle of the PA system announcing departures. 

"Stay here and watch the bags," Delores commanded flatly. She didn't look back at me. She marched straight toward the illuminated ticket counter at the far end of the room, her posture rigid and unyielding. 

I leaned forward, resting my elbows on my knees, and watched her approach the thick plexiglass partition. 

"I need two one-way tickets," Delores told the agent behind the glass, her voice carrying sharply across the relatively quiet terminal. "From Norfolk to Seattle, Washington."

The agent didn't look up, his fingers flying across a heavy, mechanical keyboard. "Two adults. Norfolk to Seattle. That'll be on the cross-country local route." 

"How much?" Delores asked, opening her purse. 

"Ninety-nine dollars each," the agent replied mechanically. "Total is one hundred and ninety-eight dollars."

I stared at the scuffed linoleum floor, my chest tightening. Ninety-nine dollars. 

For ninety-nine dollars, Delores had chosen to subject us to a nearly three-thousand-mile overland nightmare. Under the guise of taking a "vacation" to visit my sisters in Seattle, she was dragging me across the continent on the absolute cheapest, lowest-tier commercial transit network available. There was no consideration for the physical toll the rigid seats would take on my chassis, or the sheer agonizing duration of spending four days in a vibrating metal tube. The only variable that mattered in her psychological perimeter was the financial cost. She was securing total control over the itinerary for less than two hundred dollars. 

Delores slid the cash under the plexiglass partition. A few moments later, a loud dot-matrix printer ground to life behind the counter, loudly ratcheting out two impossibly long, perforated paper ticket sleeves. 

The agent tore the sleeves off the printer and slid them under the glass. "Bus boards at Gate 4. Keep your transfer slips attached. Enjoy your trip."

Delores snatched the tickets, shoved them into her purse, and turned away from the counter. She walked back toward me, her eyes already scanning the digital departure board hanging from the ceiling. 

"Get up," she said coldly, stopping next to the luggage. "We board in twenty minutes. Grab the handles and get to Gate 4."

## 9:50 AM — Gate 4

Gate 4 was nothing more than a heavy metal door at the back of the terminal, opening directly onto the concrete boarding lanes. 

I dragged our heavy luggage through the doorway, squinting against the harsh, overcast morning glare. The massive silver-and-blue Pathfinder coach idled aggressively in the bay, its diesel engine vibrating the pavement beneath my shoes. The sharp, toxic smell of exhaust mixed heavily with the damp coastal air. 

"Put those in the undercarriage," Delores directed, pointing toward the open cargo bays on the side of the bus. She didn't offer to help. She simply handed the driver her ticket and stepped aboard, leaving me to handle the physical labor. 

My right leg was shaking with spasticity as I swung the massive suitcases into the dark cargo hold. The impact echoed hollowly inside the metal bay. 

I handed my ticket to the driver and climbed the steep, narrow rubber steps onto the bus. 

The interior was suffocating. The air was a stagnant, climate-controlled mixture of chemical toilet deodorizer, stale fabric, and body heat. The narrow aisle was already cramped with passengers awkwardly shoving duffel bags into overhead racks. 

I found Delores sitting in the middle of the bus, having already claimed the window seat. I collapsed into the aisle seat next to her, my knees immediately pressing against the hard plastic back of the row in front of me. The legroom was nonexistent. My CP was already screaming for relief, and the bus hadn't even shifted into gear. 

I pulled my Kova 3310 out of my jacket pocket and stared at the blank screen. It was on Ellen's family plan—she paid the bill every month just to make sure I had a lifeline. 

*Jessica.*

My thumb hovered over the keypad. All I had to do was press a few buttons, and I could tell my sisters that we were coming. I could tell Jessica that Delores had bought tickets, that we were on our way, and that we would be in Seattle in four days. 

But the thick, suffocating fog of Delores's psychological conditioning instantly clouded my processor. 

"Put that away," Delores snapped, noticing the phone. "We aren't telling them. It's going to be a surprise."

*A surprise.* 

The word felt like a tactical restriction. Delores thrived on controlling the flow of information. If Jessica and Rachel knew we were coming, they could prepare. They could assert their own boundaries or establish a logistical perimeter that Delores couldn't breach. By keeping it a secret, Delores ensured that she held all the cards. She was intentionally orchestrating a blind collision, dropping us into their lives without warning to maximize her control over the environment. 

And because her programming ran so deep in my own operating system, I couldn't override it. The fear of triggering her rage right here in the cramped, inescapable cabin of the bus was too high. 

I locked the phone and slid it back into my pocket, entirely severing my communication with the West Coast. 

The pneumatic doors hissed shut at the front of the bus. The air brakes released with a loud, violent sigh. 

The heavy coach lurched forward, pulling out of the Norfolk transit terminal and merging onto the gray pavement of the local streets. I leaned my head back against the rigid, unyielding fabric of the seat, staring blankly at the ceiling. 

The ninety-six-hour clock had officially started.

## 12:15 PM — The Last Hot Meal

The two-hour drone up Interstate 64 was a grim preview of the physical toll the next four days would demand. The heavy vibration of the Pathfinder chassis translated directly into the floorboards, radiating up through my boots and settling deep into my joints. Every time the bus hit a seam in the concrete, my spine compressed against the rigid, unyielding fabric of the seat. 

By the time the air brakes hissed and we pulled into the Richmond transit terminal, my CP spasticity was flaring in my lower back, tight and burning. 

"We have a layover," Delores announced, standing up immediately and blocking the narrow aisle. "We change buses here for the westbound route. Get the carry-on bags and meet me inside."

I waited for the aisle to clear before forcing myself upright, my knee buckling slightly as the blood flow rushed back into my cramped legs. I grabbed our small duffel bags from the overhead rack and limped down the rubber steps into the cold midday air. 

The Richmond terminal was older and significantly more crowded than Norfolk. We immediately separated to use the restrooms—Delores marching off toward the women's room while I slipped into the men's room to splash freezing water on my face. Staring at my pale reflection in the scratched mirror, the sheer, crushing scale of what we were doing finally began to set in. We were only two hours into a ninety-six-hour marathon, and my systems were already starting to lag. 

When I walked back out into the main concourse, Delores was standing near the entrance of the small, brightly lit cafeteria attached to the terminal. She was clutching her purse, staring at the laminated menus taped to the glass with a tight, frustrated expression. 

I walked over, the heavy duffel bags cutting into my shoulder. "Are you hungry?"

"I don't have the money to eat at a restaurant," she said sharply, her tone defensive. She only had a few loose dollars to her name, a fact that always manifested as a prickly, hostile insecurity. 

"I'll pay for it," I offered quietly, pulling my wallet from my pocket. "Let's just sit down and eat a real lunch. We have the time before the westbound bus boards."

Her posture relaxed slightly, the tension bleeding out of her shoulders as she accepted the financial bailout. She didn't say thank you. She just pushed through the glass doors and claimed a small formica booth near the window. 

I ordered us two plates of hot food. For myself, I got a cheeseburger—no onions, no tomato—and a side of fries. When I reached into the glass cooler by the register to grab a carton of milk, Delores immediately shot her hand out and slapped my wrist. 

"Put that back," she snapped, glaring at the carton. "I am lactose intolerant."

I stared at her, my processor freezing for a split second. I was paying for my own drink, with my own money, but her medical restrictions were somehow a universal law that governed my diet as well. Arguing was pointless. I quietly returned the milk to the shelf and pulled out a plastic bottle of apple juice instead, paying for the entire meal with the Quantum payroll money she knew nothing about. 
I stared at her, my processor freezing for a split second. I was paying for my own drink, with my own debit card, but her medical restrictions were somehow a universal law that governed my diet as well. Arguing was pointless. I quietly returned the milk to the shelf and pulled out a plastic bottle of apple juice instead, swiping my debit card to pay for the entire meal with the Quantum payroll money she knew nothing about. 

We sat in relative silence, the clatter of silverware and the low hum of the cafeteria masking the tension between us. The food was greasy and heavily salted, but it was hot. 

I chewed the bitter burger, staring out the cafeteria window at the massive silver buses idling in the boarding lanes. We were only a few hours into the trip, and my lower back was already locked in a dull, throbbing ache. Delores ate quickly, completely unbothered by the fact that I had just subsidized her transit experience. 

I washed the food down with the apple juice, silently calculating how many more times I could safely tap into the Quantum payroll deposits before she started asking questions. The westbound bus would be boarding soon, and the hardest miles were still ahead of us.

## 10:30 PM — Interstate 81

The transition from daylight to darkness was agonizingly slow. 

After boarding the westbound coach in Richmond, the Pathfinder had steadily carved its way through the center of Virginia, making tedious, repetitive stops in Charlottesville, Staunton, and Roanoke. Every time the heavy air brakes hissed and the pneumatic doors folded open, the cold November air would momentarily flood the cabin, offering a brief, frigid gasp of oxygen before the stale, diesel-choked air sealed us back in. 

By ten-thirty at night, we were grinding down the desolate, pitch-black corridor of Interstate 81. 

The cabin was entirely dark, save for the faint, ambient glow of the dashboard instrumentation at the front of the bus and the occasional sweep of oncoming headlights cutting through the windshield. Most of the passengers had succumbed to the heavy, vibrating exhaustion of the road. 

Delores was slouched against the cold window, a thin jacket pulled tight around her shoulders. Her eyes were closed, her breathing slow and even. She had successfully retreated into sleep, escaping the physical friction of the environment. 

I couldn't sleep. 

My Cerebral Palsy was completely redlining. The rigid, unyielding geometry of the cheap Pathfinder seat offered absolutely zero lumbar support, and the constant, high-frequency vibration of the chassis rolling over the I-81 asphalt was slowly tearing my nervous system apart. The muscles in my lower back were locked in a vice of spasticity, and my right knee throbbed with a dull, sickening heat. 

I stared straight ahead at the dark silhouette of the seat in front of me, listening to the heavy drone of the diesel engine. 

We were deep in the Appalachian mountains now, somewhere past Wytheville and barrelling toward the Federal Trade Corps stop in Marion. The absolute isolation of the geography mirrored the isolation of my reality. I was trapped in a vibrating metal tube, entirely cut off from the outside world, bleeding secret money just to survive the whims of a narcissist who was dragging me across the continent for a "vacation."

The digital clock on my Kova, glowing faintly in my pocket, silently clicked over to midnight. 

Sunday, November 12th. 

We had survived the first day, but the hardest, most punishing terrain was still waiting for us in the dark.
