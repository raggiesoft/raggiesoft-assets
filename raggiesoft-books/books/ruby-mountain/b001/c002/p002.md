# Part 2: The Standoff (11:00)

The antique grandfather clock in the corner of the Great Hall chimed exactly eleven times.

Victoria Vance stood on the Persian rug, her eyes shifting from the brass pendulum to the heavy, encrypted satellite phone resting on the mahogany reception desk. It pulsed with a single, silent green light.

"The WSDOT barricades are open," Victoria announced, her voice echoing calmly into the vaulted, 1890s timber ceiling. "The Whatcom County Sheriff’s Deputies have just crossed the threshold at Milepost 134. They are exactly twenty-two minutes away."

Outside, idling on the cobblestone portico, the line of luxury SUVs rumbled, their exhaust pluming thick and white in the crisp spring air. The trunks were packed. The doors were unlocked.

But Richard Vance did not step through the threshold.

Instead, he turned his back to the open doors, walked three steps back into the Great Hall, and dropped his heavy leather duffel bag onto the floor with a defiant thud.

"Turn the engines off," Richard commanded, looking at his brothers and sisters.

The older generation hesitated, looking nervously out at the snowbanks, but Richard’s arrogant, patriarchal confidence anchored them. They stepped away from the doors, crossing their arms and standing behind him.

"We aren't leaving," Richard sneered, his face flushed as he glared across the room at Victoria. "You really think you can bluff us, Victoria? You expect me to believe you’re actually going to let county police march into our lobby and put your own flesh and blood in handcuffs? You wouldn't destroy the family name like that."

Victoria didn't argue. She didn't raise her voice, and she didn't try to convince him. She simply folded her hands in front of her.

"Twenty-one minutes," Victoria stated flatly.

The silence that fell over the Great Hall was suffocating.

Up on the sweeping, switchback staircase, the sixteen cousins watched the mutiny unfold. None of them spoke. Morgan, twenty-three, stood at the front of the pack, her jaw locked in absolute disgust. Tucked safely behind her, fourteen-year-old Amanda gripped the handles of Johnny’s manual wheelchair.

Johnny rested his hands on his lap desk. He watched his uncle Richard pacing near the fireplace, trying to project dominance, but Johnny’s highly analytical mind saw right through it. Richard was sweating. The older generation kept checking their watches, shifting their weight, waiting for Victoria to crack. They expected her to offer a negotiation, a buyout, or a compromise.

Victoria gave them absolutely nothing. She stood like a statue carved from the mountain itself.

*11:10 AM.* The idling engines of the SUVs outside were the only sound in the room. Richard ran a hand through his hair, his confident sneer beginning to fracture into genuine anxiety.

*11:18 AM.* One of the aunts quietly picked up her purse, her eyes darting between the open front doors and Victoria’s unyielding posture. "Richard... maybe we should just get in the cars. If she actually called them—"

"She didn't call them!" Richard barked, though his voice cracked slightly. "She's trying to scare us!"

*11:21 AM.* Victoria finally moved. She walked over to the mahogany desk, picked up the thick, stamped dossier from the Whatcom County Superior Court, and turned back to face the doors.

The heavy, rhythmic crunch of specialized snow tires on cobblestone echoed through the thick exterior walls.

Richard froze. The color drained entirely from his face.

Through the massive, frosted windowpanes of the Great Hall, the harsh, strobing flashes of red and blue light cut through the morning shadows, reflecting violently off the 100-year-old chandeliers. Three black-and-white Whatcom County Sheriff's cruisers—Holt Sentinels—pulled directly onto the portico, boxing in the idling luxury SUVs.

Victoria looked at her uncle, her eyes devoid of any remaining mercy.

"Your time is up, Richard," Victoria whispered.
