# Part 2: The Coffee Shop Contingency (09:05)

The brass bell above the door jingled as Johnny pushed his way into the small, harbor-front coffee shop. A blast of warm air carrying the heavy scent of roasted espresso and baked sugar washed over him, chasing away the biting chill of the October morning.

He navigated his wheelchair toward the counter, moving with a fluid, practiced precision that Vanessa had always tried to micromanage. He didn't need her to order for him. He didn't need her to speak for him.

The barista, a teenager in a flour-dusted apron, looked up and smiled. "Morning! What can I get for you?"

Johnny’s hands danced across his custom keyboard.

"I would like a large hot chocolate, a bacon, egg, and cheese biscuit, and one hashbrown, please," his text-to-speech software projected in its steady, digitized voice.

"You got it. That'll be \$8.45."

Johnny pulled the Trust-issued debit card from his jacket pocket and handed it up to the counter. As the barista swiped it, Johnny quickly typed his next command.

"Please print the receipt."

He knew Sarah wouldn't care if he spent a hundred dollars on breakfast, but Victoria had drilled forensic accounting into all of them. Trust fund money required a paper trail, even during an escape. When the barista handed back his card and the slip of paper, Johnny meticulously folded the receipt and tucked it into his wallet. It was a small, bureaucratic action, but it felt incredibly grounding. He was back in his family's system. He was back to being a Vance-Whitaker.

He collected his tray and rolled over to a small, circular table directly beside the front window. It offered a perfect, unobstructed view of the street, the ferry terminal's toll booths, and the deep, steel-grey water of the harbor.

He unpacked his food, wrapping his cold hands around the cardboard cup of hot chocolate, letting the heat sink into his palms.

It was 9:12 AM. He had over an hour and a half before the 10:55 boat blew its horn.

He took a bite of the biscuit, chewing slowly, his eyes continuously scanning the sidewalk outside. He knew Vanessa would realize he was gone the moment she stepped out of the shower. She would see his missing backpack. She might even notice the RJ-11 cable was slightly out of place. And she would immediately know where he went.

Johnny felt a spike of adrenaline, but he forced his breathing to slow down. He wasn't trapped in her apartment anymore. He was in public.

He ran through his contingencies. If she burst through the coffee shop doors, he wasn't going to cower, and he wasn't going to let her wheel him out. He had two plans.

Plan A: Play dead. If she marched up to his table demanding he leave, he would simply stare blankly at her, hit a pre-programmed macro on his keyboard that said, *"I do not know who you are. Please leave me alone,"* and let the sheer awkwardness force the barista or a local customer to intervene.

Plan B: The nuclear option. If she physically laid hands on his wheelchair, he would trigger the emergency alarm programmed into his XN laptop—a shrieking, 120-decibel siren designed to alert the cousins on the mountain if he ever fell or got stuck. In a tiny, echoing coffee shop, it would blow out everyone's eardrums and instantly summon the San Juan County Sheriff.

He took another sip of his hot chocolate, his eyes locked on the street. Let her come, he thought. He wasn't her project anymore. He was a passenger, and he was going home.
