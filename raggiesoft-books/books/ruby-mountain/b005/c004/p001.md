# Part 1: Logistical Triage (08:00)

The silence inside The Prospector's Keep was absolute.

For the last five months, the Great Hall had hummed with the ambient, wealthy energy of peak-season tourism. There had been the clinking of fine china, the murmur of four hundred guests, and the constant, choreographed movement of the hospitality staff.

Now, three days after the Washington State Department of Transportation prematurely dropped the steel barricades at Milepost 134, the hotel was empty. The guests had been evacuated. The massive front doors were locked. The only sound in the colossal, four-story lobby was the steady, rhythmic *clack-clack* of Johnny Vance’s keyboard.

Victoria Vance had commandeered the heavy, twelve-foot oak dining table nearest to the roaring walk-in fireplace, turning it into the Vanguard’s winter war room. Spread across the polished wood were WSDOT topographical maps, commercial vendor ledgers, and inventory manifests.

Sitting directly at her right hand was Johnny. His heavy Quantum XN laptop was open, a thick Ethernet cable snaking from the back of the machine directly into a copper wall-port that fed down into his subterranean server racks. He was completely equalized to the 7,000-foot altitude. His breathing was slow and deep, his posture relaxed, and his mind perfectly, flawlessly engaged. He was in his element.

"We are three weeks ahead of schedule," Victoria said quietly, running her gold fountain pen down a printed ledger. "The mountain forced the state's hand, and WSDOT dropped the steel early. That means every commercial delivery we had scheduled for the Lower Motorpool through the second week of November is currently driving blindly into a closed avalanche zone."

"I'm already rewriting the routing tables," Johnny said, his eyes scanning three different terminal windows on his screen. "I'm intercepting the Aero-Transit, Pro-Serve Foodways, and medical supply manifests. I'm scrubbing the Milepost 138 Lower Motorpool coordinates from their GPS routing and replacing them with the WSDOT gate at the Ross Dam Trailhead Parking—Milepost 134."

Victoria nodded approvingly. "Make sure you include the automated text warning for the dispatchers. They need to know their drivers cannot cross the steel. Emma and the girls will meet them at the gate with the Kodiak for the cross-loads."

"Done," Johnny confirmed, hitting the enter key. "It's live."

The heavy wooden doors leading to the kitchen corridor swung open. Diana Whitaker stepped into the Great Hall, wiping her hands on a flour-dusted apron. At twenty-five, she looked less like a chef and more like a quartermaster preparing for a siege.

"If we're updating the logistics," Diana said, pulling a folded piece of paper from her pocket and dropping it onto the oak table, "I need an emergency bulk drop. The early closure means I'm short on winter baseline calories."

Victoria picked up the list. "This is a massive requisition, Diana. Four hundred pounds of Monarch Mills, sixty gallons of high-smoke-point cooking oil, and two hundred pounds of root vegetables?"

"Food is morale, Victoria, and right now, the mountain is running twenty degrees colder than last October," Diana said, her tone absolutely uncompromising. "I have enough luxury truffles and summer berries in the walk-ins to feed a royal wedding, but that won't keep the heavy operations crew alive when they are burning six thousand calories a day shoveling Cascades Concrete. I need density. Complex carbs and stable fats. If Emma is going to be running the Kodiak in sub-zero wind shear, she needs fuel, not fine dining."

"Understood," Victoria said, immediately signing off on the requisition. She slid the paper over to Johnny. "Get this to the Seattle suppliers immediately. Tell them we need it on a flatbed to the Ross Dam Trailhead by Thursday."

"I'll prioritize it," Johnny said, his fingers flying across the keys.

"There's an issue of space, though," Diana added, crossing her arms. "To safely store that much bulk root vegetable without it rotting, I need the secondary subterranean cellar. But it’s currently full of your summer inventory."

Victoria sighed, rubbing her temples. "The vintage Bordeaux and the summer champagne."

"Fifty cases of it," Diana confirmed. "And it’s sitting right against the outer bedrock wall. If the ambient temperature down there drops below freezing because Morgan has to reroute the boiler heat to the primary living quarters, those bottles will freeze, pop their corks, and we lose forty thousand dollars in liquid assets."

"It has to go down the mountain," Victoria decided instantly. "We cannot afford to heat a cellar just to babysit wine, and I won't let it freeze. We need to extract it to the secure climate-controlled storage facility in Mount Vernon."

Before Victoria could even outline the extraction plan, the heavy iron front doors of the Keep opened.

A blast of freezing, brutally dry alpine air swept into the Great Hall, carrying the sharp scent of ozone and diesel exhaust. Morgan Foster stepped inside, kicking the snow off her heavy work boots. The twenty-seven-year-old Lead Industrial Engineer unzipped her high-vis parka, her face flushed from the cold.

"If you're moving fragile cargo down the mountain," Morgan announced, walking over to the war room table and dropping her heavy, leather-bound meteorological logbook next to Victoria's ledgers, "you have exactly a four-day window to do it."

Victoria looked up. "I thought the current high-pressure system was supposed to give us a week of clear skies."

"The lowland forecasts say a week," Morgan corrected, tapping her logbook. "But the telemetry I just pulled off our roof sensors says otherwise. The barometric pressure is already steadily dropping. The Snow Water Equivalent pillows up on Liberty Bell are logging highly unstable density. We've got a Gulf of Alaska low-pressure system banking right toward the Skagit gorge. It's going to hit by Friday night."

Morgan leaned over the table, looking directly at Victoria. "By Saturday morning, the trench Emma carved to the Ross Dam Trailhead is going to be buried under another six feet of snow. The highway won't just be closed; it will be physically impassable for anything short of a military tank."

Victoria didn't flinch. She simply processed the data. The mountain had spoken, and the Vanguard would engineer a solution.

"Alright," Victoria said, her voice dropping into the cold, surgical cadence of the matriarch. She looked around the table at her nephew and her nieces. "We execute a massive, synchronized extraction and resupply on Thursday morning. We use the 50-ton Kodiak to break the trail, and we run the heavy Ridgeback vans in its slipstream."

She turned to Diana. "We load your bulk survival rations into the vans at the Ross Dam Trailhead."

She looked at Morgan. "We load the fifty cases of vintage wine into the empty vans and send them straight down to the Mount Vernon storage facility."

Finally, Victoria looked at Johnny. "I need you to coordinate the exact timing of the lowland delivery trucks. They cannot be waiting in that freezing trailhead parking lot for more than ten minutes. You time their arrival with Emma’s Kodiak down to the absolute minute."

Johnny smiled, his hands already hovering over his keyboard. "Routing the matrix now. I'll lock the delivery windows."

Victoria leaned back in her leather chair, looking at the fire roaring in the hearth.

The early closure wasn't a crisis; it was simply a shift in the operational parameters. For the last five months, they had been a luxury hospitality empire, beholden to the schedules of four hundred wealthy tourists and the demands of the lowland economy. It was exhausting, public-facing work.

But the moment WSDOT dropped that steel gate at Milepost 134, the hospitality season officially ended. The general public was gone. The liabilities were gone.

Victoria took a sip of her dark-roast coffee, listening to the deep, rhythmic vibration of Morgan’s 1903 boiler echoing up through the massive stone floorboards. She looked at her family—Diana mapping out the winter calories, Johnny silently re-coding the commercial supply chain, and Morgan calculating the shear-strength of the incoming blizzard.

They weren't hoteliers anymore. The mountain had closed the door, and the Vanguard was simply locking the vault.
