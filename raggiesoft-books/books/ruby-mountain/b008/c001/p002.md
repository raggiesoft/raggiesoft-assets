# Part 2: The Gridmaster (14:15)

The cab of the Alpine-Cat was loud, but it was warm, and the rich, spiced scent of Diana Whitaker’s venison chili completely masked the smell of ozone and wet wool.

Henderson piloted the Alpine-Cat with one hand, his eyes locked on the massive, sheer wall of the Kodiak’s rear bumper directly in front of them. Emma Bennett was pushing the fifty-ton plow at a steady thirty miles per hour. A flawless, fifty-foot geyser of pulverized ice arched over the right side of the highway, leaving a pristine trench of black asphalt in its wake.

Barrett sat in the passenger seat, tearing a piece of the honey-jalapeño cornbread. He was quiet for the first mile, watching the teenage girl navigate the deadly switchbacks with terrifying, mechanical perfection.

Finally, Barrett swallowed a mouthful of chili and looked over at Henderson.

"So, this Morgan," Barrett said, latching onto the name Clara had shouted over the wind. He sounded relieved. "He's the foreman up there? The guy calling the shots for the weather and the perimeter?"

Henderson didn't take his eyes off the road. He just let out a low, rumbling laugh.

"Barrett," Henderson said, shaking his head slowly. "Do you ever actually listen to anything I tell you, or does it all just bounce off that lowland skull of yours?"

Barrett frowned, his cornbread halfway to his mouth. "What? Clara said 'Morgan says you have three hours before the whiteout.' Morgan is a guy's name. I'm just saying, it makes sense there’s a guy up there running the heavy logistics."

"Morgan Foster is twenty-seven years old," Henderson corrected, shifting the Alpine-Cat's gears to match Emma's pace. "And *she* is the Lead Industrial Engineer of The Prospector's Keep."

Barrett stared at him. "Another woman?"

"Another Vanguard," Henderson corrected sharply. "And you better hope you never have to cross her. Morgan doesn't just read the weather, Barrett. She dictates it. You know the Northwest Avalanche Center forecasts we get over the radio every morning? The ones that tell us if the dam is going to get buried?"

"Yeah," Barrett nodded. "From the National Weather Service in Seattle."

"Where do you think Seattle gets their high-altitude data?" Henderson asked, glancing sideways at the rookie. "Standard equipment freezes at 7,000 feet. The federal government has a blind spot up here. So, Victoria Vance bought a military-grade Automated Weather Station and bolted it to her roof. Morgan Foster holds a master's degree in atmospheric physics and is a Certified Consulting Meteorologist. She built a subterranean server grid that fires encrypted telemetry—wind shear, barometric pressure, snow-water equivalent—directly to WSDOT and NWAC every fifteen minutes."

Barrett lowered his food. "Wait. You're telling me the state government's avalanche warnings..."

"...are entirely dependent on a twenty-seven-year-old woman keeping her servers online," Henderson finished for him. "When Morgan Foster tells the National Weather Service a storm is hitting, they don't double-check her math. They treat her telemetry as absolute, federally defensible law."

Barrett looked back through the windshield at the roaring orange Kodiak. The scale of the family's sovereignty was finally beginning to penetrate his ego. "Who runs their power? To keep a server farm like that running in a blizzard, you'd need industrial steam."

"They have a 1903 high-pressure steam boiler plant," Henderson said, a note of deep, professional reverence in his voice. "And Morgan holds a Washington State Grade I Commercial Boiler Operating License. She's got a higher mechanical certification than the Chief Engineer of Ross Dam. On top of that, she's a Journeyman Electrician and an avalanche forecaster with the legal authority to deploy high-explosive ordnance."

Henderson let the silence stretch over the roar of the engines, allowing the sheer weight of Morgan Foster's resume to crush the last remnants of Barrett's pride.

"There are no 'foremen' up there, rookie," Henderson said softly. "It is a matriarchy. They are the apex predators of this mountain. You either respect the rules they lay down, or the ice buries you."

Barrett didn't say another word. He just quietly went back to eating his chili, watching the massive, spiked augers of the Kodiak clear their path.

Two miles later, the WSDOT mile marker for 134 appeared through the swirling snow.

Henderson flashed the Alpine-Cat’s high-beams twice.

Immediately, the deep, rhythmic double-blast of the Kodiak’s air horn echoed off the granite walls of the Skagit gorge.

Henderson banked the Alpine-Cat hard to the right. The Alpine-Cat’s heavy triangular treads crunched off the asphalt and bit into the steep, descending access road that plunged down toward the Ross Lake hydroelectric dam.

Emma Bennett did not slow down.

Through the Alpine-Cat’s side window, Barrett watched the massive orange plow surge forward. Emma pushed the 500-horsepower diesel engine harder, driving deeper into the high-altitude isolation, preparing to turn up the hidden, brutal switchbacks of Prospector Road to return the Vanguard to their fortress.

The SCL engineers were safe. The grid was secure. And the mountain belonged entirely to the girls.
