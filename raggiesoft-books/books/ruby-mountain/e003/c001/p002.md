## The Wrong Schedule

The water shut off, and the sudden silence in the apartment was heavy. Vanessa stepped out of the bathroom, aggressively rubbing her hair with a towel. The steam billowed out into the hallway behind her.

"Alright, Johnny," she called out, her voice dripping with artificial authority. "I hope you spent the last twenty minutes thinking about your attitude, because I am not doing this today."

Silence.

She walked into the living room, tossing the towel onto the cheap sofa. She stopped. The space next to the kitchen counter was empty.

"Johnny?"

She spun around. The bedroom door was open. She took three steps and looked inside. The bed was made, but the space underneath it, where he kept his black canvas backpack, was empty.

A cold spike of genuine terror hit Vanessa’s stomach. She rushed back into the kitchen. The landline phone sat perfectly in its cradle, the RJ-11 cord resting innocuously against the wall exactly where it always was. She didn't give it a second glance. Her eyes flew to the front door. The deadbolt was turned to the vertical, unlocked position.

*No. He wouldn't.*

He did.

Vanessa’s mind went entirely blank for a fraction of a second before the reality of the situation crashed down on her. If Johnny got on a boat, he would call his family. If he called his family, Victoria Vance would realize he wasn't in Anacortes. And if Victoria Vance realized she had lied and trapped a legally incapacitated dependent on an island to milk his Trust and state benefits...

"She is going to kill me," Vanessa whispered to the empty room. "She is going to put me in federal prison."

Panic fully took the wheel. She lunged for the kitchen drawer, ripping it open and pulling out a crumpled, tri-fold **Evergreen Marine** paper schedule. Her hands were shaking so badly she could barely track the columns. She flipped it to the Fall timetable, her eyes darting across the grid.

*9:35 AM.*

She saw the numbers and her heart hammered against her ribs. She didn't realize, in her blind panic, that her finger was tracing the *Westbound* schedule for the Anacortes-to-Lopez run. She just saw the time. She looked at the microwave clock.

*09:23 AM.*

"Dammit!"

She threw the schedule on the counter, grabbed a pair of sweatpants, shoved her bare feet into her sneakers, and bolted out the front door.


Vanessa sprinted the last block down to the waterfront, her lungs burning, her wet hair plastering to the side of her face in the freezing October wind. She cut across the crosswalk, ignoring a honking delivery truck, and vaulted onto the wooden planks of the ferry terminal.

She stopped, chest heaving, scanning the water.

Nothing.

The massive wooden pylons of the slip were empty. There was no giant green-and-white vessel loading cars. The staging lanes were mostly empty, save for a few local pickup trucks parked with their engines off. The toll booth agent was reading a newspaper.

Vanessa paced frantically in a tight circle, her hands pulling at the roots of her hair. *Where is it? Did it leave early? Did he make it on?* She stared out at the grey, choppy waters of the Salish Sea, completely blind to the fact that the actual Eastbound boat wasn't due for another hour and twenty minutes.

Directly across the street, sitting in the warm, espresso-scented air of the corner coffee shop, Johnny watched her.

His arms and shoulders carried a dull ache from the physical exertion of pushing his heavy manual wheelchair up the town's coastal inclines, but sitting here now, the burn felt like a trophy. He took another slow bite of his hashbrown.

Through the plate-glass window, he watched Vanessa pacing on the dock like a trapped animal. She looked wildly up and down the empty staging lanes, interrogating the horizon. Not once did she turn around. Not once did she think to walk across the street and check the local businesses. She was so convinced of her own superiority, so certain that Johnny was just a helpless, panicked kid trying to flee, that it never occurred to her he might be executing a methodical, calculated wait right behind her back.

Johnny picked up his cup of hot chocolate, took a sip, and let a small, defiant smile touch the corners of his mouth.

*Checkmate,* he thought.

## The Free Passage

For an hour, Johnny sat in the warmth of the coffee shop, watching the frantic, erratic movements of his abuser through the plate-glass window.

Around 9:50 AM, Vanessa had finally walked up to the toll booth and spoken to the agent. Johnny watched her shoulders slump as she clearly received the news about the actual 10:55 AM departure time. Realizing she had an hour to kill and assuming Johnny was still somewhere in the town trying to navigate the hills, she turned her back on the water. She sprinted up Spring Street, disappearing into the town to frantically search the library and the local streets, completely abandoning her post at the dock.

She had cleared his path.

Johnny waited until 10:35 AM. Evergreen Marine usually began boarding walk-on and roll-on passengers about fifteen minutes before departure, and he wanted to be at the front of the line, minimizing his time exposed on the street.

He tossed his empty cup into the trash, exited the coffee shop, and pushed his way across the crosswalk toward the terminal. The freezing October wind whipped off the Salish Sea, stinging his face, but he didn't stop. He bypassed the massive, empty staging lanes meant for vehicles and followed the blue painted lines curving to the left, directing pedestrian traffic toward the sheltered walk-on entrance.

A small line of locals with backpacks and commuting gear had formed at the ticket window. Johnny took his place in the queue, his heart hammering a steady, nervous rhythm against his ribs. He pulled his Trust-issued debit card from his wallet, resting his thumb over the embossed numbers.

He was so close. The massive green-and-white Evergreen Marine vessel, the *M/V Yakima*, was already pulling into the slip, its massive diesel engines rumbling so deeply he could feel the vibrations in the frame of his wheelchair.

The line moved quickly. When it was his turn, Johnny rolled up to the glass window.

His fingers flew across his keyboard. "One ticket to Anacortes, please," the text-to-speech software announced clearly. He held up the debit card.

The ticket agent, a woman in a heavy fleece Evergreen Marine jacket, looked down from her booth and offered a warm, easy smile. She didn't reach for the card.

"Put your money away, sweetheart," she said, waving a hand. "You don't need a ticket."

Johnny froze, his brow furrowing in confusion. He quickly typed, "I do not understand. I am going to the mainland."

"I know," the agent chuckled kindly. "But Evergreen Marine only charges fares Westbound. Getting onto the island costs money. Leaving the islands to go back to Anacortes is totally free for passengers. You just walk right on through."

She reached out and pressed a button inside her booth. The heavy metal turnstile clicked, the locking mechanism disengaging.

Johnny stared at the open gate, the sheer weight of the moment washing over him. Vanessa had built a prison of lies. She had made him believe every single aspect of his life, his care, and his movement required her permission, her management, and a transaction. She had made the island feel like an inescapable fortress.

But the fortress had an open door. The state wasn't keeping him here. The water wasn't a wall. It was completely free to leave.

He slipped his debit card back into his wallet.

"Thank you," his computer spoke, though the digitized voice couldn't capture the overwhelming swell of relief in his chest.

"Safe travels," she replied.

Johnny pushed the wheels of his chair forward, rolling smoothly through the turnstile and out onto the covered wooden walkway leading to the ferry's passenger deck. Behind him, the turnstile clicked shut. He didn't look back at the town. He kept his eyes locked straight ahead on the massive steel boat that was about to take him home.

## The Widening Gap

The *M/V Yakima* let out a deep, bone-rattling blast from its massive air horn. Deep in the hull, the heavy diesel engines shifted, sending a powerful vibration up through the steel decks and right into the frame of Johnny’s wheelchair.

He was parked at a large viewing window in the forward passenger lounge, his hands resting lightly on his wheels.

Slowly, the massive green-and-white vessel began to reverse out of the wooden slip. The water below churned into a violent, frothy white as the propellers bit into the Salish Sea.

Movement on the dock caught his eye.

Sprinting past the toll booth, completely ignoring the pedestrian walkway, was Vanessa. Her hair was wild, her clothes thrown on haphazardly. She reached the edge of the boarding gate just as the deckhands secured the heavy steel chains.

She was too late. The ferry was already fifty feet out, the gap of dark, freezing water widening by the second.

Even through the thick, salt-streaked glass of the lounge, Johnny could see the absolute rage and despair on her face. She grabbed the chain-link fence, staring at the departing boat. He watched her pull that crumpled Evergreen Marine schedule from her pocket—the one she had misread in the apartment. He watched the exact moment her eyes found the correct column for the Eastbound sailings, and the realization hit her: The next direct boat to Anacortes wasn't until 1:55 PM.

She was stranded.

Johnny let out a long, shaky breath. For the first time in months, the iron-tight knot of tension between his shoulder blades completely dissolved. He leaned back against the canvas support of his chair. The geographical moat that Vanessa had used to imprison him was now his ultimate shield. She couldn't touch him.

He had exactly an hour until the boat docked on the mainland.

He had been on Evergreen Marine ferries before with Amanda and the older cousins for summer day-trips down to Island County, but he had never navigated one alone. He took a few minutes just to revel in the sheer autonomy of it. He rolled away from the window, maneuvering easily through the wide, ADA-compliant aisles of the passenger cabin. He took the elevator up to the sun deck, braving the freezing, howling October wind for just a minute to feel the sea spray on his face and watch Friday Harbor shrink into a tiny, insignificant speck against the shoreline.

When the cold became too much, he retreated back into the heated cabin and found a quiet booth in the corner.

He opened his backpack and pulled out his Quantum XN laptop. He didn't have a dial-up connection out here on the water, but he didn't need one. He opened a blank word processing document and placed his hands over his custom keyboard.

He had made a promise to himself: He was going to tell them *everything*.

He knew how his twin sister operated. Amanda was fiercely protective, and when she saw him, she was going to be an emotional wreck. If he tried to explain the last few months using his text-to-speech software in real-time, it would take hours, and they would constantly interrupt him with questions and tears.

So, he typed.

For forty-five minutes, as the ferry cruised smoothly through the San Juan archipelago, Johnny documented the entire nightmare. He typed out the truth about the phantom "Anacortes facility," the subsidized apartment, the isolation, the gaslighting, and Vanessa's aggressive control over his communications. He laid out the entire timeline of her fraud.

By the time the captain's voice crackled over the overhead PA system, announcing their final approach into Anacortes, Johnny had a three-page document saved to his desktop.

He closed the laptop, slid it back into his bag, and rolled toward the front passenger doors. Through the forward windows, the mainland of Washington State was growing larger, the dark timber of the Cascade foothills looming in the distance.

He was ready.

## The Terminal

The heavy tires of the Holt Workmaster van crunched against the gravel as Ashleigh bypassed the staging lanes and threw the vehicle into a temporary loading zone directly adjacent to the Anacortes terminal's main passenger exit. She shoved the gearshift into park and killed the engine.

It was 11:54 AM. They had beaten the boat by nine minutes.

Inside the cavernous van, the silence of the stopped engine was immediately filled with the tense, chaotic energy of the cousins trying to unravel the sheer scale of the fraud.

"I still don't understand the logistics," Sarah said, leaning over the middle row of seats, her hands nervously twisting the fabric of her coat. "If she was living in public housing on the island, what was the address on her state caregiver filings? How did she intercept the mail from the Trust?"

"She probably set up a ghost P.O. Box in Anacortes and forwarded it," Ashleigh replied, her eyes locked on the empty terminal doors. "She used the mainland address to keep us away, then just rode the Evergreen ferry back and forth to collect the checks."

In the third row, Victoria ignored the speculation. Her Quantum laptop was closed, and she was meticulously reading over the freshly printed, four-page *ex parte* restraining order she had faxed to a Whatcom County judge from a rest stop twenty minutes ago. The ink was barely dry, but the legal trap was fully set. She pulled a black fountain pen from her lapel, made one final, aggressive underline beneath Vanessa's name, and snapped the legal binder shut.

In the passenger seat, Amanda wasn't listening to Sarah's theories or watching Victoria's legal preparations. She was staring blankly out the windshield at the deep, grey waters of the Salish Sea, and she was crying.

The tears fell silently at first, but as the clock on the dashboard ticked to 12:00 PM, a jagged sob broke through her chest. She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. She didn't care about the forged documents. She didn't care about the state checks. She was terrified. Johnny was her fraternal twin; they shared a bond that superseded everything else. The thought of him trapped in a small apartment, stripped of his communication, and forced to navigate a maritime escape entirely on his own was tearing her apart.

"Hey," Ashleigh said softly, reaching across the center console to grip Amanda's shoulder. "He got himself to the boat. He's safe."

"I just need to see him," Amanda choked out, her voice trembling. "I need to see him right now."

At 12:03 PM, a blast from a massive air horn shook the windows of the van.

The *M/V Yakima* glided around the edge of the breakwater. The sheer size of the Evergreen Marine vessel was staggering, its green-and-white superstructure blocking out the horizon as the captain expertly reversed the massive engines, churning the water white to guide the boat into the wooden slip.

"How long?" Amanda asked, wiping her face, her hand already gripping the door handle.

"Three to five minutes," Victoria stated with calm authority from the back seat. "They have to secure the mooring lines and lower the overhead pedestrian ramp. Walk-on passengers disembark first, before the car deck is cleared."

The waiting was agonizing. The cousins sat in the van, watching the deckhands in high-visibility jackets throw the heavy ropes over the massive steel cleats. The hydraulic whine of the passenger ramp lowering into place echoed across the parking lot.

At 12:07 PM, the glass doors of the terminal building finally slid open.

A wave of commuters, tourists, and locals poured out onto the pavement. Ashleigh, Sarah, and Amanda held their breath, their eyes frantically scanning the crowd.

And then, the crowd parted slightly.

Moving smoothly down the center of the concrete ramp, his black canvas backpack slung over the handles of his manual wheelchair, was Johnny. He looked exhausted, the freezing wind whipping at his jacket, but the moment he cleared the terminal doors and saw the massive Holt Workmaster van idling in the loading zone, he stopped.

He didn't hit his text-to-speech keyboard. He didn't wave. He just looked at the van, and his shoulders completely dropped in a silent, overwhelming exhale of relief.

Amanda threw the passenger door open and hit the pavement running.
