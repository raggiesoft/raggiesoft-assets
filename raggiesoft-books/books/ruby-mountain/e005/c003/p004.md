## The SS7 Layer

Johnny finally released his fierce grip on Morgan’s heavy canvas coveralls. He leaned back in his manual wheelchair, letting out a long, ragged exhale. The sheer emotional weight of the server log had momentarily overwhelmed him, but as his nervous system resettled, his brilliant, analytical mind zeroed right back in on the data on the screen.

He looked at the unblockable trace. *360-378-4092*.

He knew that phone number. It was the landline in Vanessa’s apartment. But he also knew that Vanessa was deeply, clinically paranoid. She had physically installed a Caller ID blocking module on the wall jack, and she had paid the local telecom extra to ensure her number was unlisted and permanently blocked from outbound transmission.

Johnny placed his hands on his lap desk.

*"Vanessa’s outbound Caller ID was permanently blocked at the hardware and account level,"* the text-to-speech engine stated, echoing slightly in the glass-enclosed server room. *"How did the server bypass the block to grab the ANI?"*

Morgan smirked, pushing herself off the antistatic floor and leaning against the humming rack of Quantum OS servers.

"Because Caller ID is a polite suggestion, boss," Morgan explained, crossing her arms. "But ANI is the absolute law."

Amanda leaned against the steel desk, crossing her ankles. She loved watching Morgan talk shop.

"Think about it like network architecture," Morgan continued, pointing to the blinking lights of the server rack. "Caller ID is just a consumer-level feature. It's essentially surface metadata. If you punch \*67 before you dial, or if you have a hardware block, you are just telling the local phone switch, 'Hey, please hide my name string.' It's like masking your browser's User-Agent. It's easy to spoof, and it's easy to block."

Johnny nodded immediately. The backend logic made perfect sense.

"But Automatic Number Identification—the ANI—doesn't operate at the consumer level," Morgan said, her eyes gleaming with absolute technical reverence. "It operates at the SS7 layer. Signaling System 7. That is the foundational routing architecture of the global telecom grid. You can't mask it, because the SS7 layer doesn't care about your privacy. It only cares about billing."

Johnny’s fingers hovered over his keyboard. *"Because it was a toll-free number."*

"Exactly," Morgan beamed, snapping her fingers. "You didn't dial a local Seattle number. You dialed the Trust's 1-800 enterprise remote access line. When you call a toll-free number, the receiving party—us—pays the phone bill for the connection. And because we are paying for it, the telecom switch *must* transmit the true originating hardware number to our PBX for billing verification. The SS7 layer rips the Caller ID block right off the packet and hands the true physical address directly to my server."

Morgan walked over to the console and tapped the heavy CRT monitor.

"Honestly, it wouldn't have mattered if you had missed the remote access server entirely," Morgan told him. "If you had just managed to dial the Keep's public 1-800 room reservation line, I would have caught you."

Johnny smiled. He knew exactly what she meant.

The Prospector's Keep public reservation line was a standard automated greeting for VIP guests. But Morgan had hardcoded a hidden, localized PBX bypass into it years ago. If you called the reservation line, bypassed the greeting by pressing '1', and punched in a specific seven-digit authentication passcode, the system instantly dropped you into the hidden family extensions.

<aac>"The hidden menu,"</aac> Johnny typed, the synthetic voice carrying a tone of deep satisfaction. <aac>"If I had entered the family passcode, your PBX would have routed me to the private suites."</aac>

"Yep," Morgan nodded. "The second you hit that passcode, the PBX authenticates you as Vance blood. It would have routed your call directly to Victoria's desk, and the SS7 layer would have handed me Vanessa's unblockable ANI on a silver platter exactly the same way. There was no scenario where you reached this mountain and I didn't get your exact geographic coordinates."

Amanda looked at the server rack, shaking her head in sheer disbelief at the watertight engineering.

"Vanessa thought she was a mastermind because she bought a plastic blocker from an electronics store," Amanda scoffed, resting her hand on Johnny's shoulder. "She had no idea she was picking a fight with a digital fortress."

Morgan pushed herself off the server rack, walking back over to the wheelchair.

"She brought a plastic toy to a gunfight," Morgan agreed, giving Johnny's shoulder a light, affectionate shove. "Alright, command center. You've seen the firebox, and you've seen the tripwire. What's next on the agenda? Because if we stay down here any longer, Diana is going to start paging us for dinner prep."

## The Legacy Grid

Amanda looked back at the glowing CRT monitor, shaking her head. "I just can't believe they still let us keep those original 1-800 blocks. Most companies lost those years ago."

Morgan reached out and affectionately patted the heavy metal casing of the Quantum OS server rack.

"The telecom companies tried to force us onto an 877 or 866 block during the '99 renovation," Morgan said, a smug edge to her voice. "Victoria threatened to bury them in Trust litigation for the next decade. We kept the 1980s routing blocks."

Johnny smiled, his fingers resting on his lap desk. He shared Morgan's exact philosophy when it came to digital architecture.

*"There is no replacement for legacy stability,"* Johnny's text-to-speech engine announced into the cold, sixty-degree air of the server room. *"You do not abandon a perfectly stable operating kernel or a copper-wire PBX switch just because it's aging. You preserve it. The older the architecture, the fewer consumer-level exploits exist. It is pure structural integrity."*

Morgan snapped her fingers, pointing directly at him. "Exactly. You preserve the iron that works. The 1903 boiler shell, the 1980s telecom switches, the 2000-era server environment. We gut the vulnerable parts and we build a fortress out of the legacy tech that refuses to die."

Suddenly, the heavy analog phone mounted on the wall outside the glass server room began to ring. It was a harsh, mechanical trill.

Morgan rolled her eyes, pushing herself away from the server console. "And speaking of legacy tech that refuses to die... that is the kitchen intercom. Diana is officially losing her patience with us."

Morgan walked out of the glass enclosure and snatched the heavy handset off the wall.

"Engineering bay," Morgan answered crisply.

"Your core temperatures are stabilized, the boiler is firing, and the server hasn't melted," Diana's voice crackled through the speaker, carrying the absolute authority of the Head Chef. "It is 15:00 hours. The heavy ops team needs to be back upstairs for hydration protocols, and I need Amanda to prep the dining room. Bring the command center upstairs."

"Yes, Chef," Morgan laughed, hanging up the phone.

Morgan turned back to Amanda and Johnny, gesturing toward the heavy industrial freight elevator.

"Alright, you two. Playtime is over," Morgan said, her eyes fiercely warm. "Let's get back upstairs before Diana decides to put all three of us on deep-clean dish duty for the rest of the month."

Amanda stepped behind Johnny’s wheelchair, unlocking the brakes. "Come on, kid. Let's go see what she has planned for our first official lockdown dinner. I am not scrubbing pots tonight."

As Amanda pushed him out of the engineering bay, Johnny took one last look at the massive, 1903 iron boiler. It was roaring quietly in the background, a contained, industrial inferno fighting a war against the freezing mountain above them. He could feel the deep, seismic rumble of the steam pressure vibrating through the floorboards.

He had his twin sister pushing his chair, the undisputed master of the Keep's mechanics walking beside him, and a digital tripwire protecting his back.

The siege of Ruby Mountain had begun, and Johnny Vance was exactly where he belonged.
