## December 10, 1990 (`23:42 EST`) — Mile Marker 98 (Interstate 81 Northbound, Newbern, VA)

Fourteen months after that night backstage at **The Midwest Horizon Arena** in Chicago—having kept his vow to Cassidy through every remaining tour stop of 1989 and 1990 by flatly refusing to put *"If You Walked Away"* back on the setlist despite every angry memo from Los Angeles—twenty-six-year-old **Ryan O'Connell** had both hands locked at ten-and-two on the steering wheel of the family's **1988 Holt Freighter** touring van, heading north on **Interstate 81** after a small club gig in **Wytheville, Virginia**.

When they had loaded out of the venue in Wytheville an hour earlier, the regional weather report had only called for light, passing snow flurries—nothing that would slow down a forty-five-mile drive home to Blacksburg. Had the forecast warned of a major winter storm, Ryan never would have put the van on the highway; they would have checked straight into a hotel in Wytheville for the night and waited out the inevitable **Winter Weather Driving Ban**, using the emergency hotel cash that **Aunt Martha and Uncle Jack** (Evan and Tyler's parents) and **Sarah and Daniel O'Connell** (Ryan, Cassidy, and Holly's parents) always insisted they keep tucked inside the glovebox for exactly that reason.

Instead, twenty minutes north of Wytheville on the high mountain grade toward Pulaski County, the sky had collapsed without warning. The light flurries turned into a blinding whiteout that dropped visibility under fifty feet, glazing the asphalt in black ice as the dashboard radio crackled with an emergency bulletin announcing that the **Virginia Department of Transportation** was preparing to close Interstate 81 under a **Winter Weather Driving Ban**.

Ryan wouldn't let anyone else take the wheel. He knew the tractor-trailers on I-81, and he knew the short mountain merges. He was doing everything right—holding a steady `40 MPH` in the right-hand lane with the four-way hazard flashers clicking rhythmically (`tick-tock, tick-tock`) against the dashboard while the windshield wipers fought the heavy wet snow.

Behind him, under the dim amber glow of the middle bench's map light, twenty-three-year-old **Cassidy** and twenty-one-year-old **Holly** were looking through the courier packet from **Apex Records** that had been waiting for them before the show.

Spread across their laps were the two newest insults from Sunset Boulevard. The first was a promotional sleeve of [***Live in Chicago***](https://music.raggiesoft.com/the-stardust-engine/1990-live-in-chicago): not only had Apex pressed the lopsided, synth-buried Chicago Front-of-House board tape without letting Ryan or Cassidy near the mixing console, their Los Angeles art department had commissioned a hand-airbrushed pastel-pink and electric-cyan illustration for the cover where Evan and Tyler were reduced to nameless dark silhouettes in pink dry-ice fog while Ryan and Cassidy—dressed in a pastel color-block windbreaker and an acid-wash denim jacket they didn't even own—were painted beneath three glowing neon hearts like a married couple singing a romantic power ballad.

The second item in the folder explained why Apex had forced that cover onto them. Stamped **`SPRING 1991 PROMOTIONAL ROUTING — REGIONAL SHOPPING CENTER ATRIUM SERIES`**, Arthur Pembrook's newest mandate proposed pulling the four **Commonwealth Polytechnic Institute** graduates off concert stages and sending them on a thirty-city **suburban shopping-mall food-court tour**—playing twenty-minute Saturday afternoon sets over a pre-recorded DAT backing tape between the indoor fountain and the escalator while Evan and Tyler weren't even allowed to bring live drums or bass amplifiers into the retail concourse.

In the middle bench seat, looking down at that grotesque pastel cover and the list of echoing, fluorescent shopping-mall atriums, Cassidy was crying quietly.

Holly had both arms wrapped tightly around Cassidy's shoulders, holding her sister against her coat and comforting her as best as she could—even though Holly knew, with an ache in her chest, that the person Cassidy really wanted to hold her and anchor her right now was Ryan. From the rear bench seat right behind them, twenty-two-year-old twins **Evan** and **Tyler** leaned forward over the seatback, whispering steady, gentle words of comfort to their cousin.

"Don't cry, Cass, we're not letting them put you in a single one of those malls," Evan murmured softly over the hum of the heater.

"Not a chance," Tyler whispered, resting a warm hand on her shoulder beside Holly's. "We're almost home."

Glancing up into the rearview mirror for a split second between the sweeps of the wiper blades, Ryan saw the tears shining on Cassidy's cheeks and the way Holly was trying with all her strength to mimic his deep-pressure squeeze. His hands ached to let go of the wheel, unbuckle his seatbelt, and climb into the back bench to wrap his sister in his arms—but on a sheet of black ice with less than fifty feet of visibility, he couldn't take his hands off the steering wheel for a single second.

"Just ten more minutes to the Christiansburg exit, Cass," Ryan called back softly over his shoulder, keeping his eyes locked on the icy right lane ahead. "As soon as we're in the driveway on Progress Street and the van's in park, I've got you, okay? We're never doing that mall tour."

At `11:42 PM`, just as the green reflective sign for **Mile Marker 98** (`Exit 98, Newbern`) flashed past the right window, a pair of blinding high-beams materialized out of the swirling snow in Ryan's driver's-side mirror—a car barreling up the icy left passing lane far too fast for the mountain grade.

Before Ryan could even breathe a warning, the passing car slewed sideways across the sheet of black ice and slammed into the Holt Freighter's rear quarter panel.

On black ice, physics was unforgiving. Ryan counter-steered with everything he had as the rear tires broke loose, the highway guardrail vanished in a blur of spinning headlights, and the van plunged off the shoulder—rolling twice down the steep snowy embankment in a deafening roar of tearing steel and shattering glass.

When the violently tumbling frame finally slammed to a halt in the deep drift at the bottom of the ravine, the engine died in a hiss of steam. Up front in the crushed cab, snow blew through the shattered windshield across Ryan's face. A white-hot snap burned through the middle of his spine, and below his ribs, there was nothing at all. Pinned tight behind the collapsed steering column, he heard the rear doors wrench open behind him and the frantic voices of Evan, Tyler, and Holly scrambling out of the back with Cassidy and shouting his name through the dark—and then Ryan's vision went black beneath the falling snow.

## Winter 1991 — Star City General & Protocol T-10

When Ryan woke up under the fluorescent lights of the surgical intensive care unit at **Star City General Hospital**, the orthopedic trauma surgeon didn't sugarcoat the MRI scans: a complete **T10 thoracic spinal cord injury**. Everything above his navel—his arms, his hands, his fretboard calluses, his lungs, his voice—was 100% intact. Below his tenth thoracic vertebra, he would never walk again.

For forty-eight hours, lying flat on his back listening to the rhythmic hiss of the hospital monitors, Ryan stared at the acoustic guitar case leaning against the corner wall and wondered if **The Stardust Engine** was over.

His family didn't let that thought survive past the third morning.

At `08:00 AM` on his fourth day in the step-down ward, **Cassidy**, twenty-one-year-old **Holly** (now in her first year of law school), and twenty-three-year-old twins **Evan** and **Tyler** walked into his hospital room carrying a portable four-track cassette deck, a pair of studio headphones, a carpenter's tape measure, and a thick three-ring binder labeled **`PROTOCOL T-10`**.

"Don't even think about saying it, Ry," Cassidy said fiercely, sitting on the edge of his hospital mattress and wrapping her arms around his shoulders so tightly he could feel her heartbeat against his collarbone—giving him the exact deep-pressure anchor he had given her backstage in Chicago. "Your hands are right here. Your voice is right here. You are my brother, and we don't play a single note without you."

Beside the bed, Evan and Tyler held up the carpenter's tape measure.

"We already measured every doorway in Aunt Martha's house on Progress Street NE," Evan said, his voice steady and matter-of-fact. "Thirty-six inches clear from the front porch through the kitchen, the hallway, and the first-floor bathroom. Aunt Martha already cleared out the downstairs front room for you and Cass so you don't have to touch the stairs, Ty and I spent all day yesterday building a twelve-foot pressure-treated timber wheelchair ramp over the front porch steps at a 1:12 grade, and we lowered your workbench and amp heads in the garage so every knob sits at forty-two inches off the floor."

"And as soon as you're cleared for outpatient rehab," Tyler added, tapping a commercial driver's manual tucked under his arm, "since you had your hands on that steering wheel getting us home through the storm, Evan, Holly, and I are all testing for our **Class A Commercial Driver's Licenses** with Passenger and Airbrake endorsements—and we're installing mechanical hand-controls in the family vehicle so you can drive whenever you want to, and we can spell you on the highway whenever we tour."

When Ryan was discharged from Star City General in his first ultralight manual wheelchair that spring and rolled up the wooden ramp onto the porch of **Aunt Martha's** farmhouse on **Progress Street NE** in Blacksburg, he learned everything from the ground up: fifteen-minute weight-shift pressure reliefs, transferring from his chair to the studio bench, and reverse-climbing steep curb cuts so his front casters wouldn't catch. Every time they encountered a building in town without a ramp, Evan and Tyler locked hands beneath Ryan's frame—his "Human Ramp"—and whenever the dark nights after the crash made his chest tighten, Cassidy was right there beside him on the first floor as his human guardrail.

In Blacksburg, surrounded by his sisters, his cousins, and Montgomery County neighbors who treated him like the exact same CPI kid who had lived on the block since 1985, Ryan healed.

Twelve hundred miles to the west, however, **Apex Records** was waiting for the third album owed under their contract—and the executives in Los Angeles had no intention of letting the band stay in Virginia.
