## 8:30 AM — Petty Fights, Day-Of Whims, and Keeping My Plan to Myself

By eight-thirty on Tuesday morning inside Tammy's smoke-stained single-wide trailer at Pinecrest Court, we had already fought twice before coffee—once because my manual wheelchair tire left a damp mark two inches inside the linoleum doorway, and ten minutes later because I asked where my clean navy t-shirts had gone.

For weeks now, every single day with Tammy had been a minefield of petty, exhausting arguments over nothing at all: which shelf of the fridge my work lunch touched, how loud my Lofstrand forearm crutches clicked when I stood up at night, how many minutes I spent on the corded wall phone checking on seventeen-year-old Gwen and dying Grandpa Walter over near Emerald Creek Mall. Nine months after letting her talk me into moving out of Grandpa's house, the fog had finally burned off inside my twenty-three-year-old head. I was completely done. I had already decided I was breaking up with her—the only question was how to get myself, my wheelchair, and my crutches safely out of an isolated trailer park off Route 13 without Tammy blocking the narrow aluminum doorway or smashing my things in a rage.

Then, at quarter past eight that very morning, Tammy had slammed her bedroom closet open, hauled out an overstuffed rolling suitcase, and announced on pure day-of impulse that we were catching the city bus to the Norfolk Pathfinder station right now to buy two ninety-nine-dollar walk-up cross-country tickets to Seattle, Washington.

Sitting quietly in my manual wheelchair with my battered olive duffel across my thighs and both of my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches strapped snug in the vertical holder tubes on the back of my backrest, I hadn't argued a single word.

Inside my own head, two thoughts clicked into place with crystal clarity.

First, I had zero intention of setting foot on any Pathfinder bus. Gwen was turning eighteen in two days, Grandpa Walter's heart was down to its final borrowed days, and wild horses couldn't drag me out of Chesapeake.

Second, anybody who actually used a wheelchair in the year two thousand knew Pathfinder's intercity accessibility rule backward and forward: if you needed an over-the-road coach equipped with a hydraulic wheelchair lift—not just leaving Norfolk, either, across every transfer hub from Richmond and Roanoke down Interstate 81 to Knoxville, Nashville, St. Louis, Kansas, Denver, Wyoming, Idaho, and Pasco—you had to call Pathfinder at least forty-eight hours in advance so dispatch could assign lift-equipped coaches along the route. Deciding on a three-thousand-mile trip the morning of departure without giving two seconds of thought to whether her boyfriend in a wheelchair could physically board the bus was Tammy in a nutshell. I was ninety-nine percent sure she had never called, and I sure hadn't reminded her or dialed their number myself—because I already knew I wasn't going.

Even better, letting her march me out to the bus stop meant getting my wheelchair, my crutches, and a duffel bag of my clothes out of Pinecrest Court in broad daylight, straight into a public transit terminal full of witnesses and city buses heading back toward Emerald Creek.

This was my chance to escape.

"Quit sitting there daydreaming and roll out on the porch so I can lock the deadbolt," Tammy snapped, shoving an envelope of crumpled twenties into her purse.

Out on the rutted gravel lane past sagging trailer skirting, weeds, and overflowing dumpsters—easy rolling distance from where the buses met up along Robert Hall Boulevard—getting into Downtown Norfolk meant catching CVTA Route 6 or Route 13, whichever showed up first. When we reached the cracked curb along the Route 13 corridor, a forty-foot high-floor Beacon V coach wearing old nineties white, navy, and teal Tidewater stripes with a rectangular white CVTA decal beside the front doors rumbled up first on Route 13.

The operator pulled the air brake, flipped a dashboard toggle so the three steep front stairwell steps flattened into the horizontal steel platform of the Elevate-U hydraulic wheelchair lift, and lowered it smoothly to the concrete. Tammy let out an exaggerated, put-upon sigh right behind my push-handles the entire thirty seconds it took the hydraulic arms to raise my wheelchair three feet up to the cabin floor and lock the floor straps in place.

"Thirteen-oh-two to Dispatch," the operator called into his handset as Tammy dropped coins impatiently into the farebox.

<pa>Dispatch, go ahead thirteen-oh-two.</pa>

"Dispatch, I've picked up one ten-sixteen on Route 13 inbound."

<pa>Ten-four.</pa>

Tammy marched halfway down the aisle by herself so she wouldn't have to sit beside the wheelchair bay. Watching scrap yards and river bridges roll past the window with my silver forearm crutches rattling softly in the holder tubes behind my shoulders, my pulse beat fast and steady against my ribs. Just get to Downtown Norfolk. Get inside the terminal doors. End it in public where she can't lock me in.

## 9:45 AM — Before the Walk-Up Ticket Counter

Near the concrete dome of the Monticello Arena in Downtown Norfolk, our Route 13 operator cycled the front-stairwell Elevate-U lift back down to the sidewalk. Crossing over to the northbound stop to catch CVTA Route 2 or Route 11—whichever showed up first—for the short hop up to the intercity station, a high-floor Route 11 MetroCoach beat a trailing Route 2 coach through the traffic signal by half a block, lifted my chair aboard, and set us down five minutes later a block from the brutalist concrete facade of the Norfolk Pathfinder Terminal.

Heavy automatic sliding glass doors parted onto a wave of industrial floor wax, fryer grease, and diesel exhaust drifting in from the rear coach bays.

Even at quarter to ten on a weekday morning, the waiting room was busy—thirty or forty travelers sitting along bolted rows of hard plastic chairs beneath buzzing fluorescent tubes, two uniformed security guards chatting near the vending machines, and a line of three walk-up customers ahead of the scratched plexiglass ticket counter at the far wall.

Tammy didn't even glance down to check if my caster wheels had cleared the rubber entry mat. Shifting her rolling suitcase into her left hand and pulling the cash envelope halfway out of her purse with her right, she started striding across the middle of the waiting room straight toward the walk-up ticket line.

"Wait right here by the pillar while I go buy our Seattle tickets," Tammy tossed back over her shoulder.

Before she could take two steps toward that ticket counter, I snapped both steel wheel locks down hard on my manual wheelchair tires right in the middle of the public concourse.

"Save your money, Tammy," I said clearly.

She stopped mid-stride and turned halfway around, eyebrows pinching together in annoyance. "What?"

My heart hammered against my breastbone, yet looking up from my wheelchair with thirty witnesses around us instead of four thin trailer walls, the words came out steady and final.

"Don't buy a ticket for me," I said. "First off, you decided on this trip two hours ago, and you have to call Pathfinder forty-eight hours in advance just to get a wheelchair lift on an intercity bus—which you didn't even bother to think about. Second, Gwen turns eighteen in two days and my grandfather is dying. I'm not going across the country with you, I'm not coming back to the trailer, and we're breaking up right here."

For three dead-silent seconds Tammy stared at me with her hand frozen on her purse envelope, realizing all at once that I had let her ride two city buses into Norfolk just to walk out on her in public. Every petty argument of the past two months detonated behind her eyes into ugly, blotchy red fury.

"You sneaky little bastard!" Tammy shrieked, dropping her suitcase handle so loud heads whipped around in every row of seats as she lunged three fast steps straight back to the front of my wheelchair. "You let me haul my bags all the way into Norfolk just to dump me in a bus station?! Nobody cares about your forty-eight-hour cripple excuses or your six-foot freak of a sister—you're coming up to that counter with me right now!"

"No, Tammy," I said, gripping both wheel rims tight while my throat knotted up. "Go wherever you want. We're done."

Tammy didn't say another word. Towering straight over my seated frame in the middle of the public waiting area, she swung her right arm back and violently smacked me flat across the left side of my face with all her weight.

The crack rang off the tile walls like a board snapping in half.

My head jerked hard against my right shoulder, knocking my neck stiff while blinding white heat bloomed across my cheek and ear. Sitting locked in my manual wheelchair with nowhere to back up, nine straight months of walking on eggshells, petty daily cruelty, being cut off from Gwen and Grandpa Walter, and suddenly getting struck across the face in front of an entire bus terminal broke the dam inside my chest completely apart.

My breath hitched in a ragged, broken gasp, and I started crying right there in my wheelchair in the middle of the waiting room—heavy, chest-shaking sobs pouring out of me before I could stop them, hot tears spilling down my stinging face onto my jacket collar while I pressed both palms over my eyes.

Tammy raised her hand again, teeth bared over my chair. "Stop crying like a pathetic little—"

Fellow passengers moved faster than she could swing.

Plastic chairs scraped loud across the terrazzo as a broad-shouldered shipyard worker in a navy canvas coat lunged straight between Tammy and my footrests, catching her forearm and shoving her firmly backward toward her dropped suitcase while a middle-aged woman in teal hospital scrubs and a young guy in a culinary jacket stepped in shoulder-to-shoulder around my wheelchair.

"Get back off him right now—don't you dare touch him again!" the shipyard worker barked, pushing Tammy back another full pace when she tried to claw past his chest while two more passengers yelled for the uniformed guards by the vending bank. "Security! Right here—this woman just hauled off and smacked a man sitting in a wheelchair!"

"Stay out of my business!" Tammy screamed, face purple, clutching her unspent cash envelope as the two security officers jogged up fast to flank her elbows.

"Out the front doors right now, ma'am, or Norfolk PD puts cuffs on you," the older guard ordered flatly while the culinary student rolled her suitcase out behind her heels so she couldn't step back within ten feet of my chair.

My whole chest kept heaving in jagged, humiliating sobs I couldn't turn off. Kneeling down to eye level beside my right wheel so she wasn't towering over me, the woman in teal scrubs pressed a clean stack of folded paper napkins gently into my hand and spoke in the kindest, steadying voice imaginable.

"You're completely safe now, sweetheart—she's out on the sidewalk with security and she can't get back in," she said softly. "Just breathe. Where's home for you? Have you got family waiting?"

Wiping furiously at my wet eyes and burning left cheek with the napkins, choking through heavy sobs that broke every sentence in half, I managed to get the words out.

"I just—I just want to go home," I sobbed, gripping my push-rims with shaking hands. "I just want to go home to the Emerald Creek area of Chesapeake... my little sister turns eighteen in two days and my grandpa's dying, and I just want to go home to them."

Nobody looked down on me for crying. An older gray-haired retired chief carrying a canvas tool bag stepped up beside the nurse, made sure the straps holding my silver forearm crutches on the back of my wheelchair hadn't loosened, and nodded calmly toward the side exit away from the front curb.

"Southbound Route 15 runs straight down Military Highway to Emerald Creek Mall, son," the retired chief said warmly. "Come out the side doors with me—I'll ride the two bus down to the fifteen transfer right beside your chair so she doesn't get near you."

## 12:30 PM — Grandpa Walter's Porch and Getting Affairs in Order

When southbound CVTA Route 15 swung off eight-lane Military Highway into the Emerald Creek Mall transfer loop and lowered its front-stairwell Elevate-U lift to let me off, the late-morning air smelled like clean autumn maples instead of mildew and cigarette ash.

Rolling the three quiet residential blocks up Grandpa Walter's front walk, I locked my wheelchair brakes at the foot of the three wooden porch steps, unstrapped my matte-silver Lofstrand forearm crutches from the back of my chair, hoisted my olive duffel onto the boards, and climbed up onto the covered front porch just as seventeen-year-old Gwen pushed open the storm door on her tall matte-charcoal crutches.

Six-foot-one Gwen took one look at my red-rimmed hazel eyes, the faint pink mark fading on my left cheek, and my wheelchair sitting parked at the bottom of the steps—and the second I told her hoarsely that I had broken up with Tammy at the Norfolk bus station for good, pure sunshine broke across my little sister's face. Propping both of her charcoal crutches cleanly beside my silver pair against the wooden porch railing, Gwen wrapped both long arms fiercely around my neck and hugged me with all her strength.

"You dumped her—you actually dumped her!" Gwen gasped, laughing through happy tears against my hair on Grandpa's porch. "Oh, Nolan, I missed you so much! You're home for my birthday!"

Inside the warm kitchen, though, seventy-eight-year-old Grandpa Walter sat propped in his worn recliner beneath his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket, a thick manila accordion folder tied with string resting across his thin knees. His eyes shone proud and deeply relieved to have me back under his roof, yet the shallow rattle in his breathing made it plain he knew his time was almost up—holding on by sheer willpower so Gwen would turn eighteen on Thursday free of any county child-welfare claim.

While Gwen went down the hall to make up my old bed, Grandpa untied the manila folder and quietly walked me through the last of his earthly affairs: his notarized two-page will naming me executor, his veterans discharge and modest burial policy, and the ninety-day payoff statement on the home-equity medical loan he had borrowed five years back to keep our roof patched and wheelchairs rolling—meaning once he passed, the house would have to be sold.

"I'm making Thursday, boy," Grandpa Walter whispered with a faint, steady smile, squeezing my wrist. "I'm seeing your sister turn eighteen. After that, Nolan... make sure she's got a home right beside her brother."

That very afternoon—after a Chesapeake sheriff's deputy stood ten minutes of civil standby outside Pinecrest Court while my Blue Tech coworker loaded my full-sized bed, oak dresser, nineteen-inch television, silver VCR and DVD combo unit, and Quantum OS 2000 Professional laptop into his pickup before Tammy even straggled back from Norfolk—I rolled into a walk-up leasing office four blocks from Emerald Creek Mall and Blue Tech and signed a twelve-month lease on Studio 2B.

It was a tiny rectangular box built for one person. Come Thursday evening over birthday cake, I couldn't wait to hand my sister the second key.
