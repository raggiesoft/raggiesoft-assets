## 10:15 AM — Two Smooth Wheelchairs and One Pair of Canvas Sneakers

There is no word in English big enough for the feeling of pushing both palms forward on your manual wheelchair's push-rims and discovering an entire town where nobody forgot to pour the curb cuts.

At ten-fifteen on our fourth morning under Vaeloria's sun—after an unhurried breakfast of warm honey-oat porridge at Keepers' Hall's seated copper counter—Gwen and I rolled side by side across the Star-Court with all four of our single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches tucked neatly upright inside our rear backrest pockets, while Mallory laced up the dry canvas sneakers she had retrieved from Studio 205 and slung her canvas notebook bag across her forest-green cardigan.

"Listen to that front right fork, shortstop," Gwen beamed beside me, giving her push-rims one light flick so her chair glided twenty feet across the satin-smooth rose-quartz flagstones in dead-straight, whisper-silent perfection thanks to Master Doran's brass truing wrench. "Zero wobble, zero pull to the right, and nobody put us on hold for forty-seven minutes!"

At the western arch of the Waystation terrace, Archivist Thorne showed the three of us onto the Valley Descent Platform—a broad, twelve-by-twelve-foot stone-and-bronze water-balanced lift sheltered beneath a carved cedar roof that descended the inner side of the mountain into Vaeloria's heartland. Unlike Earth's cramped hospital elevators where two wheelchairs forced everybody to back out awkwardly over steel door tracks, all three of us fit side by side behind a waist-high brass rail overlooking a breathtaking panorama of terraced orchards, waterfalls, silver-bridged streams, and golden-roofed cottages nestled across a lush green valley bowl: Cloudbrook Hollow, first market town of the Slow-Footed Order.

When the platform settled flush to the millimeter against the valley promenade at the bottom, Gwen and I rolled straight forward without even lifting our front casters.

Every lane through Cloudbrook Hollow was paved in the same tightly fitted hexagonal rose-quartz slabs as the borderland causeway, graded gentler than one inch in twenty where stone bridges arched over clear trout brooks. Better still, as townsfolk in woven russet tunics, indigo aprons, and soft leather boots opened timber shutters along the morning market square, we noticed something that made Gwen stop her chair outright in delighted shock.

Every bakery window ledge, fruit-seller's table, brass-smith's counter, and public drinking-fountain basin along the square was built at thirty-two inches off the flagstones—with open knee-space recessed underneath and wide cedar benches set right alongside so seated riders, crutch-users, elders with walking staves, and standing shoppers all met eye-to-eye at one shared level!

"Nolan—Mal—look at the bakery counter!" Gwen whispered, her green-hazel eyes shining bright as a smiling baker in a flour-dusted apron slid a warm tray of cinnamon-glazed seed rolls onto the low oak ledge directly at our seated chest height without either of us craning our necks upward like little kids at a bank teller window.

"Universal design baked straight into seventh-century masonry," Mallory said with quiet, glowing satisfaction behind her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses, pulling her blue ballpoint pen from behind her right ear to sketch the recessed counter geometry into her spiral pad. "When the founders of a civilization walk with staves and carts themselves, accessibility isn't an afterthought bolted onto the back alley—it's the front door."

## 11:05 AM — A Pale-Blue Cloak in the Archway and Grounding in Sunlight

Halfway down Weaver's Lane—where bolts of dyed cloud-wool fluttered from timber eaves above a bustling archway—a brisk young courier hurried out of a side court wearing an unbuttoned pale baby-blue wool cloak, letting out a sharp, high laugh over her shoulder that bounced off the stone vaulting behind my chair.

My logical brain knew immediately where I was. My body didn't ask my logical brain first.

Both of my palms slammed my wheelchair's push-rims to a dead stop before the echo finished bouncing off the stone. Every muscle across my shoulders locked rigid as iron, my heart hammering against my ribs as that split-second flash of baby-blue wool and sharp laughter dragged my nervous system straight back to Monticello Avenue and the locked corridor at Pasco Intermodal Terminal—my eyes darting wildly to make sure my wheelchair wasn't boxed against a wall.

Before the stranger had even walked ten paces away down the lane—completely oblivious, carrying nothing more dangerous than a basket of dyed yarn—two pairs of hands anchored my world from both sides at once.

On my right, Gwen angled her wheelchair smoothly inward until our rear tires touched, wrapping her warm left arm securely across my shoulders in a steady, unhurried safe-person squeeze right there in the sunlit lane. On my left, Mallory stepped calmly half a pace into my clear forward sightline—blocking the crowd bustle without crowding my knees—and rested her warm fingers lightly on the back of my hand atop my left push-rim.

"Yarn courier in wool, heading east toward the dye-vats, already forty feet past us, Nolan," Mallory reported quietly and literally, her calm blue-gray eyes steady behind her silver lenses. "Open twelve-foot lane behind you, Gwen on your right wheel, me on your left, and both of your brakes unlocked whenever you're ready to roll."

"Take five breaths, shortstop—nobody's in a rush in this whole valley," Gwen murmured warmly against my shoulder.

Nobody in Cloudbrook Hollow honked, sighed impatiently, or stared while we sat paused for thirty quiet seconds in the middle of the lane; two passing potters simply curved smoothly around our wide flagstone margin with a friendly nod of their caps. By the fourth breath, the cold adrenaline spike drained clean out of my wrists, and I was able to lift my left hand off the push-rim to squeeze Mallory's fingers and lean briefly against Gwen's shoulder.

"Thanks, you two," I breathed quietly, feeling proud rather than ashamed that three breaths had brought me all the way back into the sunlight instead of losing a whole afternoon to panic. "Baby-blue ambush averted."

"Anytime, Keeper," Mallory smiled softly, kissing my knuckles once before falling right back into step beside my left wheel.

## 11:05 AM — Warm Seed Rolls and Zero Stares

Rolling three abreast down Weaver's Lane with warm cinnamon seed rolls wrapped in linen napkins on our laps, I kept waiting out of twenty-four years of Earth conditioning for the catch—for a delivery cart to block the ramp, a shopkeeper to talk over my head to Mallory because she was standing, or a cobblestone rut to rattle my footrests loose.

It never happened once.

When we paused beside a glassblower's open workshop hung with sapphire and rose-gold marsh-lanterns, the master artisan addressed me directly at eye level to ask how the upper Astrolabe rings felt under our hands, thanking all three of us earnestly for clearing the silver Drift-haze off the valley stars the night before. Every curb sloped like silk; every doorway opened four feet wide without a brass threshold lip; and whenever Mallory spotted an intriguing runic sundial or water-clock mechanism along a guildhall wall, Gwen and I parked effortlessly in dappled orchard shade right beside her while she sketched every gear ratio in blue ballpoint ink.

Looking across at Gwen spinning happily to inspect a rack of burgundy woven shawls while Mallory held half her cinnamon roll out for me to take a bite, my chest felt lighter than mountain air.

"Look down by the waterwheel, you two," Gwen called eagerly, pointing past the pear blossoms where Master Doran the ironsmith was waving a friendly hand beside a kettle of spiced orchard tea. "Lunch with zero stairs—race you down the ramp at two miles an hour!"
