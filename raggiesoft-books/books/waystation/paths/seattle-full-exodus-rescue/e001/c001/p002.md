## 7:45 AM — Fog Behind a Locked Restroom Door in Pasco

By quarter to eight Saturday morning at the Pasco Intermodal Terminal in eastern Washington—after grinding across the Idaho line through Boise, Nampa, and the bleak predawn high desert with nobody from Virginia standing on the platform to stop us—ninety hours without real sleep and the deep, throbbing burn under my broken left sitting-bone skin had turned the bright tiled concourse into swimming, cotton-stuffed tunnel vision where I could barely string four words together.

Walking right beside my dragging silver forearm crutches toward the quiet rear restroom corridor while the other passengers queued for coffee up front, Tammy didn't yell or shove me at all. Out of nowhere after four days of snapping and cold shoulders, she slipped her arm softly through mine and dropped straight back into that warm, sugary Week-One voice that my exhausted, literal brain had been conditioned for two years to obey whenever my defenses were gone.

"Come inside the single family restroom with me for just a few minutes, baby," Tammy whispered softly against my ear, guiding my trembling elbow through the doorway while my dizzy head struggled even to stay upright over my forearm cuffs. "We haven't gotten to be together once all week, Nolan, and you know how much I've missed having sex with you... just come inside and let me be close to you like home."

Clicking the heavy steel privacy deadbolt shut behind us, Tammy eased my two silver forearm crutches out of my shaking hands to prop them against the tile wall, braced my shoulders back against the corner grab bar, and reached smoothly down to unbuckle my belt and unbutton my jeans.

"Tammy, please, my head's spinning, my hip hurts too bad—I don't want to right now," I mumbled weakly, too exhausted and disoriented after four sleepless nights even to lift my arms or push her hands away as she whispered *shh, stop overthinking, baby, just let me take care of you* against my neck—until my dazed mind froze completely under the buzzing fluorescent tube, staring up into gray static and wishing only to disappear.

## 8:02 AM — Quiet Crying Out of Pasco, Ellensburg, and Snoqualmie Pass

Fifteen minutes later, the harsh crackle of the terminal loudspeaker calling final boarding for Yakima, Ellensburg, and Seattle echoed through the vent above the sink, where Tammy was already zipping and buttoning her flared jeans back up in the mirror. Smoothing her pastel-blue cardigan into place, she stepped briskly back over to where I sat slumped and hollow on the closed porcelain lid, hooked my belt buckle shut for me when my numb fingers didn't move, pressed my silver forearm crutches back onto my wrists, and kissed my cold cheek with a bright, satisfied little smile.

"See, baby? Told you we just needed a few minutes alone together," Tammy chirped softly, unlocking the heavy steel door and hurrying me back up the steep coach steps two minutes before the bay doors hissed shut.

From the minute our westbound coach pulled away from the Columbia River out of Pasco, I couldn't make my eyes stop leaking.

Wedged upright on the aisle cushion beside Tammy as dry sagebrush hills rolled past toward Yakima, I kept my jaw clenched shut and pressed the cuff of my olive canvas jacket hard across my mouth so the passengers across the aisle wouldn't hear a sound—yet quiet, hot tears kept slipping steadily down my cheeks onto my heather-navy collar mile after mile. My stomach felt hollow and sick, deep heat throbbed relentlessly from my raw left hip all the way down my thigh, and violent shivers kept running across my shoulders inside my jacket even though the back of my neck felt sticky and damp against the vinyl headrest.

Beside the window, Tammy's sweet restroom voice vanished before we even cleared the outskirts of Pasco.

At first she ignored me and flipped through a crumpled glossy magazine; by the time our driver made a brief ten-minute highway stop in windswept Ellensburg at the foot of the Cascades, watching me sit motionless with wet eyelashes staring down at my knuckles made her dark eyebrows pinch sharp with disgust.

"Would you knock it off already, Nolan?" Tammy hissed under her breath as the doors closed in Ellensburg and the diesel engine lugged uphill toward the dark, rain-swept evergreens of Snoqualmie Pass. "People walking back to the bathroom are looking right at us. Nothing bad happened back there—you're my boyfriend, for crying out loud—so quit sniffling like a five-year-old!"

I didn't answer her. Turning my face slightly toward the center aisle while Tammy shoved her foam cassette headphones angrily back over her ears, I stared down at the scuffed rubber tips of my silver forearm crutches wedged beside my jeans as gray mountain rain began drumming hard against the coach roof over the pass.

As the dense cotton fog from four sleepless nights parted inch by agonizing inch inside my head during the long descent down the western slope toward Puget Sound, my literal brain replayed what had happened behind that locked steel door in Pasco step by step—stripping away every sugary word Tammy had whispered.

Back in that corridor, I hadn't wanted to go inside that bathroom. I had told her out loud that my head was spinning, my hip hurt too badly, and I didn't want to. I had barely been able to stand upright on my crutches or keep the room from tilting sideways, let alone agree to sex inside a locked bus-station bathroom—and when I told her *I don't want to right now*, she had thrown the deadbolt and kept going anyway until she was finished and buttoning her jeans back up in the mirror.

Nobody I knew had ever talked about a girlfriend doing something like that to a grown man. Yet as our coach crossed Mercer Island on Interstate 90, plunged through the tiled lid tunnel, and burst straight out onto the low concrete deck of the Lake Washington floating bridge—where the wet gray towers of the Seattle skyline suddenly rose all at once across the rain-chopped water ahead of the windshield—what had happened in Pasco finally dawned on me in plain, sickening clarity.

Sitting frozen in Row Seven with fresh tears spilling down my chin, one terrifying question echoed silently inside my head over the hum of the tires:

*Was I raped?*

***

## 1:15 PM — Screaming Goodbye in the Downtown Seattle Pathfinder Terminal

Steady early-afternoon rain was streaking down out of fifty-degree gray clouds when our coach finally swung off the wet street, rumbled beneath a low concrete overhang, and hissed to a final stop inside the cavernous, exhaust-choked arrival bays of the Downtown Seattle Pathfinder Terminal.

End of the line—ninety-six hours and three thousand miles away from Grandpa Walter's porch and Gwen.

Getting down those four steep aluminum steps onto the slick concrete apron took almost everything left in my arms. My teeth chattered so hard I bit the inside of my lip, thinking the damp Pacific Northwest chill was cutting straight through my olive jacket, and my legs trembled violently inside my jeans while the baggage handler slid my folded black manual wheelchair, two loose chromed-steel footrests, and battered olive duffel out of the underbelly compartment onto the floor.

Hauled inside through the automatic glass concourse doors out of the diesel draft, I leaned heavily on both silver forearm crutches beside a thick poured-concrete pillar hung with chrome wall payphones, setting my folded wheelchair, detached footrests, and duffel down beside my sneakers while quiet, helpless tears kept sliding down my cheeks.

Tammy didn't lift a finger to unfold my chair. Counting coins out of her purse, she marched twenty feet down the pillar row to an open wall payphone, dropped quarters into the slot, and dialed her cousin.

From twenty feet away across the echoing terrazzo, I couldn't hear a single word her cousin said on the other end of the line—only Tammy's voice melting instantly into a warm, breathless purr into the black handset, warmer than she had spoken to me once in months.

"Hey, Travis—it's me, I finally made it into downtown!" Tammy said brightly, curling one finger around the armored steel cord with an eager smile. "God, I missed you so much too—you have no idea how much I love you and how badly I've missed hearing your voice. Yeah, I'm back out here for good... I'm home for good now. I've been counting down the miles to being with you, and don't worry—I promise I'm going to uphold my end of the deal. Just tell me which bus I catch from downtown to get straight down to your place."

Leaning pale and shivering on both silver crutches against the cold concrete column, my literal brain caught what didn't fit before she even reached for her pen.

*I* finally made it. *I'm* home for good. Which bus *I* catch.

In every single sentence coming out of Tammy's mouth, she kept saying *I* and *me*—never once *we*, never once *us*, never once mentioning to her cousin Travis that I was standing twenty feet behind her at all.

Jotting two quick words onto the corner of her ticket envelope, Tammy blew a soft kiss into the mouthpiece, hung the heavy black receiver gently back onto its chrome cradle—her warm smile vanishing off her face the split second the plastic clicked—and turned back around toward me.

Seeing me staring back at her beside that pillar with wet tracks shining down my cheeks—still quietly crying five straight hours after leaving Pasco—blew the very last fuse in Tammy's head.

"Oh, for God's sake, are you STILL crying?!" Tammy screamed at the top of her lungs, stomping straight up in front of my crutches while travelers across the concourse turned to stare. "Five straight hours of sniffling and staring at the floor like a miserable little martyr ever since Pasco! I am sick to death of your nonstop crying, sick of hauling those crutches around, and I am NOT spending one more minute stuck with dead weight! Figure out your own ride—we're through!"

Before I could even draw breath through my chattering teeth to answer, Tammy yanked up the telescoping metal handle of her rolling suitcase—leaving my folded manual wheelchair, two loose steel footrests, and olive duffel lying abandoned on the terrazzo beside my shoes—and stormed straight out the rain-streaked glass street doors of the Seattle terminal to head wherever she was going, vanishing into the gray downtown rain without looking back once.

***

## 1:26 PM — Wheeling to the Ticket Counter and Calling Nine-One-One

For a long, hollow minute after the heavy glass exit doors swung shut behind Tammy, I just stood braced against that cold concrete pillar listening to strangers' footsteps echo across a city where nobody on Earth knew my name.

Then my left knee buckled half an inch under my shaking weight, warning me plainly that if I didn't sit down right away, I was going to hit the terrazzo floor.

Bracing my body against the concrete column with one forearm cuff, I pushed downward with all my remaining strength on the black vinyl seat rails of my manual wheelchair until the cross-brace clicked flat. Kneeling painfully for ten seconds on the cold floor, I hooked both chromed-steel footrest assemblies back onto their front hanger pins, eased myself onto the bare, unpadded seat canvas—biting back a sharp gasp as blinding heat flared straight up from my raw left sitting bone—strapped both of my matte-silver single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches upright into the vertical tubes behind my backrest, and pulled my battered olive duffel across my lap.

Gripping both steel push-rims with trembling hands, I wheeled myself slowly across the wide concourse toward the lighted Pathfinder ticket counter.

By the time my front casters rolled up to the lowered accessible counter window at the end of the glass partition, every dam left inside my chest had given way. Holding onto my duffel strap with both hands, I sat there crying openly in front of the window, hot tears dripping off my chin onto the olive canvas.

Behind the scratched safety glass beside a humming beige computer monitor, a forty-something male Pathfinder ticket agent in a crisp light-blue collared shirt, dark tie, and uniform vest looked up from his manifest printer—and stopped dead in his tracks. Setting his pen down immediately, he stepped right over to the lowered accessible window and leaned down toward the circular stainless-steel speaking grille, his eyes filling with instant concern.

"Hey there, son—take a slow breath for me," the agent said gently, zero impatience in his voice. "Are you traveling by yourself? Tell me what's wrong."

Wiping clumsily at my wet eyes with the back of my shaking wrist, choking around ragged sobs that split my voice in two, I told a stranger the plain truth out loud for the very first time.

"My—my girlfriend brought me here all the way from Virginia and she just screamed that we're through and walked out," I sobbed through the metal grille, my teeth still chattering hard between words. "And back at the Pasco bus station this morning, my head was spinning so bad I couldn't think straight and I told her I hurt too much, and she locked us inside the bathroom anyway... sir, I think I was raped. I don't have any money left, and I just want to go to the hospital and speak to the police."

Behind the glass, the ticket agent didn't question me, belittle me, or ask a single skeptical word. Instead, as he looked closely through the clear partition at my face under the bright overhead counter lights, his expression shifted in half a heartbeat from gentle sympathy to urgent alarm—seeing something written plain across my flushed, sweat-sheened skin and glassy eyes that I had zero idea my own body was presenting.

"Son, look at your face—you're burning up," he said quietly but fast, reaching straight across his counter blotter to snatch up the corded black desk telephone handset with one hand while pressing the keypad with the other. "I believe every word you just told me, and you did the exact right thing rolling straight up to my window. Lock your wheelchair brakes right here where it's dry and safe—don't move an inch. I'm calling nine-one-one right now to get Seattle paramedics and a police officer inside here to take care of you."
