## 1:15 PM — Ten Brutal Days Without Our Greeting Hug, Ray's Voice, and One Massively Earned Embrace

Four days before Chesapeake public schools opened for junior year after Labor Day—with seventeen-year-old Gwen carrying Grandpa Walter's notarized guardian travel letter and high-school ID card, Ray Halstead covering Mallory Walsh's Friday shift without a second's hesitation, and two Gateway Airlines tickets through Chicago Midway backed by Grandpa's Tidewater Maritime Credit Union card—the double doors of Puget Sound General's sixth-floor discharge lounge swung open.

Sitting dressed in clean jeans and my heather-navy t-shirt atop a brand-new four-inch medical gel wheelchair cushion inside my rescued manual wheelchair, a ten-day bottle of oral antibiotic capsules tucked safely in my olive jacket pocket, my breath stopped dead in my throat.

Clicking swiftly across the polished hospital linoleum on her tall matte-charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches—six-foot-one, eyes shining bright with fierce sisterly joy—came seventeen-year-old Gwen, with five-foot-five Mallory Walsh walking right beside her in a cozy forest-green zip cardigan and thin silver wire-rimmed glasses, carrying a folded fleece travel blanket over one arm.

Neither Gwen nor I said a word before our arms met.

Propping both charcoal crutches cleanly against the discharge counter, Gwen bent straight down over my wheelchair armrests and opened her arms wide, and I locked both of mine around my little sister's waist and shoulders in the deepest, longest, most aching greeting hug of our entire lives. Ever since we were children, hugging Gwen tightly every single time we greeted each other or said goodbye had been woven straight into my autism like breathing. I didn't have any clinical phrase or fancy vocabulary word for why I needed her arms like that—I only knew in my bones that ten brutal days without pressing my forehead beneath Gwen's collarbone—across nine states of highway diesel, Pasco's locked door, screaming rain, and hospital IV poles—had left every circuit in my chest starving for the one steady anchor on Earth that quieted the noise inside my head.

"Gotcha, shortstop," Gwen whispered thickly into my hair, holding me tight against her burgundy sweater for a full, unhurried minute without letting go until my whole trembling ribcage finally settled into hers. "Never letting you out of reach again."

All week while the IV drip brought my fever down, Gwen and Grandpa Walter had carefully avoided talking about Store Four-Twenty-One over the hospital phone—telling me only to focus one hundred percent on breaking my infection so the doctor would clear me before Labor Day. The instant Gwen eased half a step back beside my right wheel with one hand still resting warm on my shoulder and I looked straight up into Mallory Walsh's unmistakable blue-gray eyes three thousand miles from Emerald Creek Parkway, that single unanswered question burst straight out of my chest.

"Mallory—you came all the way out here!" My voice cracked wide open with sudden, desperate hope as both of my hands caught hers. "Did Mr. Halstead put me on medical leave once you called Grandpa's house last Friday?! Please tell me—is there *any* way I can get my job at Blue Tech back?!"

Mallory didn't stand above my wheelchair or feed me a gentle lie to save for Virginia. Dropping cleanly onto both knees right on the hospital linoleum beside my front footrests so her eyes met mine dead on the level behind her wire-rimmed glasses, she held both of my hands steady and warm in hers.

"Nolan, I promised myself I would always tell you the exact truth," Mallory said softly, her own voice thick with emotion. "Because I waited until last Friday evening's third missed shift on August twenty-fourth to call your grandfather's house, corporate's automated payroll computer up at headquarters had already processed an automatic job-abandonment termination with a corporate rehire freeze before Monday morning. Ray Halstead appealed straight to District HR with your hospital admission fax—and corporate still refused to unlock your employee number. I am so sorry I waited until Friday, Nolan."

Even after ten days of highway misery, Pasco, and hospital needles, hearing out loud that I had permanently lost my Blue Tech job stung so brutally hard that all the breath went clean out of my lungs.

I had loved that royal-blue polo. For almost two years I had never missed one single punch on the clock, never misplaced one coaxial adapter, never given anyone a reason to doubt me—counting on every biweekly Friday paycheck to prove an autistic guy on forearm crutches could take care of his little sister when their grandfather died. Losing even that made ten days of swallowed terror, homesickness, and shock break wide open at once.

My chin crumpled, a raw, ragged sob tore straight out of my throat, and right there in the middle of the sixth-floor discharge lounge, I buried my face forward and cried my heart out.

Neither Gwen nor Mallory let me cry alone for half a heartbeat.

Right on my right side, Gwen dropped onto the cushioned visitor chair flush against my wheel and pulled me hard against her shoulder; right on my left, Mallory rose off her knees onto the edge of the bench, wrapped both arms tightly around my neck and shoulders, and held my shaking head close against her green cardigan collar while her own tears dampened my hair. Caught safe inside four fierce, unhurried arms—zero yelling, zero shame, zero workplace wall left anywhere between Mallory and me—I sobbed in sheer, chest-crushing terror as Pasco, six days of hospital IVs, and losing the one job I knew how to do crashed down on me at once.

"Let the brave mask go, Nolan—just lean on the two of us," Gwen whispered fiercely against my temple, rocking my shoulders gently while cool Puget Sound rain drummed against the sixth-floor hospital glass three thousand miles from Virginia. "What Tammy did to you in Pasco wasn't your fault, and losing that blue shirt wasn't your fault either. Mallory and I are holding the line right beside you from here on out."

"I got fired, Gwenny—nobody's ever going to hire me with a termination on my record!" I gasped out in raw terror, my hands shaking uncontrollably against my jeans knees. "Grandpa's sick and I don't even have a job to keep a roof over us!"

![Inside the sixth-floor Discharge Lounge of Puget Sound General Hospital in Seattle on a rainy Friday afternoon at the start of Labor Day weekend in 2001, fully dressed twenty-three-year-old Nolan Lennox sits in his manual wheelchair crying openly inside the warm embrace of seventeen-year-old sister Gwen Lennox and Mallory Walsh as Mallory places Ray Halstead's folded reference letter and personal phone card into Nolan's hand]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/paths/seattle_p003_puget_sound_discharge_reunion.jpg#fullwidth)

"Hold right there, Nolan—somebody back home in Chesapeake made me promise to call him the minute I stood beside your wheelchair," Mallory said gently. Pressing a folded reference letter on crisp Blue Tech letterhead and a white business card into my palm, she reached into her cardigan pocket, flipped open her small silver cell phone, and dialed Virginia where it was just past four on Friday afternoon.

Two rings later, Mallory spoke quietly into the handset—"Mr. Halstead, I'm right here with Nolan in Seattle"—and held the warm phone gently against my ear.

"Nolan? You hear me alright out there, son?" fifty-two-year-old Ray Halstead's calm, unmistakable retired-Navy baritone came steady and warm down the line, carrying so much genuine kindness that my ragged breathing hitched in surprise.

"Mr. Halstead?" I choked out, grabbing hold of the phone with trembling fingers. "Sir, I didn't mean to miss work—Tammy wouldn't let me near a phone and then I ended up in the hospital—"

"Easy now, son—breathe slow for me, nobody in my building is mad at you," Ray said gently, his comforting voice cutting straight through a week of hospital fear. Because Ray had a five-year-old autistic granddaughter, he understood right away how Tammy had trapped my literal brain without me ever connecting boarding that bus to losing Store Four-Twenty-One. "First off, thank God that ticket agent got you to a doctor in time and your sister and Mallory have got you. Second, stop panicking right now about finding work when you get home. Corporate's automated computer up at headquarters locked me out after your third missed shift last Friday, and because it processed as job abandonment, corporate's system marked your file ineligible for rehire."

My heart lurched hard against my ribs—until Ray's steady voice laid down the exact plan to protect me.

"Look at the back of that card Mallory just put in your hand, Nolan," Ray told me clearly. "That's my direct home telephone number. Whenever you fill out a job application back here in Tidewater, do NOT give any employer Blue Tech's corporate eight-hundred employee-verification number. If an employer calls that corporate verification line, all they're going to get is a machine telling them you're ineligible for rehire—and they'll disqualify you before anybody hears the truth. Give every employer my direct home phone number instead. The minute anyone calls my house, I'm picking up off the clock myself, explaining that my best Quantum OS technician got stranded out of state in a medical emergency after twenty-two spotless months, and giving you the glowing professional review you earned. You focus on healing up and hugging your grandpa, son—I've got your references covered."

Tears of overwhelming relief spilled hot down my cheeks as the terror crushing my throat finally loosened.

"Thank you, Mr. Halstead," I whispered thickly into the mouthpiece. "I won't forget—I'll only put down your home number."

"Get home safe before Labor Day, sailor," Ray said warmly before Mallory thanked him, clicked her flip phone shut, and rested her warm hand back over mine.

"Every single mile of the way," Mallory murmured softly, meeting my eyes behind her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses. "And losing Blue Tech last Friday also means there isn't a Shift Supervisor pin standing between you and me anymore."

Wiping my wet cheeks on my wrist, I looked straight across two inches of air into Mallory's honest, freckled face—letting the whole picture sink into my literal brain at once.

The very minute Puget Sound General cleared me to travel, Mallory hadn't hesitated over burning her Labor Day weekend or crossing three time zones; she had driven Gwen to the credit union for Grandpa's notarized letter, rented a car at Sea-Tac, and flown clear across America just to bring me home before school even started.

"Mallory," I whispered thickly from my wheelchair, reaching both hands slightly toward her green cardigan lapels, "can I hug you right now—just for you?"

Tears shone bright behind Mallory's silver wire-rimmed glasses as the warmest, most relieved smile broke across her face.

"Come here, Nolan," Mallory whispered, leaning right down over my left armrest to wrap both arms all the way around my shoulders while I locked both of mine tightly around her waist and back—burying my cheek against the soft wool of her collar in a long, quiet, massively earned hug that neither of us rushed by a single second. Right beside my right wheel, seventeen-year-old Gwen leaned back in the visitor chair watching the two of us with shining green-hazel eyes and the happiest, most wholeheartedly approving grin on Earth.

![Inside the rainy sixth-floor Discharge Lounge of Puget Sound General Hospital in Seattle, fully dressed twenty-three-year-old Nolan Lennox sits in his manual wheelchair with his two matte-silver single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches stowed upright behind his backrest, locked in a deeply emotional, massively earned two-armed hug with Mallory Walsh kneeling warmly beside him while seventeen-year-old sister Gwen Lennox sits right beside Nolan's wheel with her matte-charcoal single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches leaning beside her chair, beaming with proud, wholehearted sisterly approval]({{CDN}}/raggiesoft-books/images/scenes/waystation/paths/seattle_p004_mallory_earned_hug_gwen_approving.jpg#fullwidth)

"Okay, you two," Gwen smiled gently when Mallory finally eased back just enough to lace her fingers warmly over my hand on my wheelchair armrest. "Your doctor cleared you to fly tomorrow morning as long as we rest your hip flat at the airport hotel tonight first—so let's sign Nolan's discharge papers, load Mallory's rental car downstairs, and get my brother out of this hospital."

## 9:40 PM — Four Crutches by the Nightstand and Wide Awake Inside Four Safe Arms

Rolling out through Puget Sound General's automatic glass doors into crisp, rain-washed late-August Seattle air for the first time since Saturday afternoon felt almost unreal—watching streetlights blur past the passenger window of Mallory's rented four-door Vanguard Fleet Services sedan for twenty quiet minutes down Interstate 5 until she pulled beneath the portico of a low-rise brick airport hotel just outside Seattle-Tacoma International Airport.

Inside our ground-floor accessible room, everything should have felt a hundred percent safe. My rescued manual wheelchair and brand-new four-inch medical gel seat cushion sat parked right inside the entry alcove, all four of our single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches stood lined up neatly by height against the nightstand—my shorter matte-silver pair beside Gwen's taller matte-charcoal pair—and after a warm shower, my evening antibiotic capsule, and hot chicken noodle soup, I lay stretched out in a clean cotton t-shirt and soft drawstring lounge pants right in the middle of the wide king-sized bed on my good right side.

Neither Gwen nor Mallory dreamed of leaving me on that mattress by myself.

Dressed in modest cotton sleep tees and soft lounge pants, seventeen-year-old Gwen climbed onto the right side of the king bed behind my shoulders, while Mallory double-checked the steel privacy deadbolt and security flip-latch on the hallway door, set her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses beside the lamp, switched off the light, and slipped under the warm hotel quilt along my left side.

Right away, both women cuddled tightly against me from either side—Gwen wrapping her long, familiar arm firmly across my waist and pressing her forehead warm against the back of my shoulder, while Mallory nestled close along my left side, draping her arm securely across my chest and lacing her warm fingers tightly through mine.

For six straight nights up on Puget Sound General's sixth floor, bright nurse-station lights, electronic badge doors, and twenty-four-hour hospital staff had stood ten feet outside my room.

The split second that hotel lamp clicked dark inside a quiet ground-floor room in Washington State—the exact state where Tammy had screamed in my face and vanished into the rain just six days ago after locking that Pasco bathroom door—pure, suffocating terror slammed into my chest like ice water.

My eyes snapped wide open in the dark, my pulse hammering so hard against my ribs I was sure both Gwen and Mallory could feel it through my shirt. Every metallic tick of the wall heater, every muffled holiday traveler's footstep walking past our corridor door, every distant rumble of a late-night jet climbing out of Sea-Tac sent cold panic shooting down my spine—half my brain terrified Tammy was still somewhere down Interstate 5 and about to rattle that doorknob, the other half terrified that if I let my eyelids close for ten seconds, I would wake up trapped behind white restroom tile with my crutches out of reach and gray static swallowing the ceiling.

Feeling my whole frame stiffen rigid between them, neither Gwen nor Mallory let go by half an inch.

"Hey—it's just us, Nolan," Gwen whispered softly against the back of my neck, tightening her warm arm around my ribs in that steady deep-pressure squeeze she had used since we were kids. "Deadbolt's locked, safety latch is flipped, and Tammy has zero idea where we are. Nobody is getting through that door."

"Right here on your left, Nolan," Mallory murmured gently in the dark, pulling my trembling knuckles closer against her collarbone and rubbing her thumb in slow, predictable four-second circles across the back of my hand. "You don't have to force yourself to sleep if your head won't let you. Gwen and I have both sides covered until morning."

All night long, neither woman loosened her hold around me once—even after midnight, when Gwen's breathing slowed into deep sleep against my right shoulder blade and Mallory drifted in and out beside my left arm, instinctively tightening her fingers around mine every time I flinched at a hallway ice machine or passing headlights outside the blackout curtains. Held tight and warm inside the safest embrace I had ever known, yet far too scared out of my mind to close my eyes in the dark, I lay wide awake staring at the thin silver seam of parking-lot light along the carpet without getting a single minute of sleep until pale gray Saturday dawn finally washed across the ceiling.
