## 1:15 PM — Sixty-Six Days Without Our Greeting Hug, My Lost Shirt, and One Massively Earned Embrace

Seven days after Gwen turned eighteen—carrying her brand-new adult Virginia identification card straight through post-September-eleventh airport security checkpoints beside Mallory Walsh on two Meridian Airlines tickets backed by Grandpa Walter's Tidewater Maritime Credit Union card—the double doors of Puget Sound General's sixth-floor discharge lounge swung open.

Sitting dressed in clean jeans and my heather-navy t-shirt atop a brand-new four-inch medical gel wheelchair cushion inside my rescued manual wheelchair, my breath stopped dead in my throat.

Clicking swiftly across the polished hospital linoleum on her tall matte-charcoal Lofstrand forearm crutches—six-foot-one, eyes shining bright with fierce sisterly joy—came eighteen-year-old Gwen, with five-foot-five Mallory Walsh walking right beside her in a cozy forest-green zip cardigan and thin silver wire-rimmed glasses, carrying a folded fleece travel blanket over one arm.

Neither Gwen nor I said a word before our arms met.

Propping both charcoal crutches cleanly against the discharge counter, Gwen bent straight down over my wheelchair armrests and opened her arms wide, and I locked both of mine around my little sister's waist and shoulders in the deepest, longest, most aching greeting hug of our entire lives. Ever since we were children, hugging Gwen tightly every single time we greeted each other or said goodbye had been woven straight into my autism like breathing. In two thousand and one I didn't have the vocabulary yet to call someone my safe person—I only knew in my bones that sixty-six brutal days without pressing my forehead beneath Gwen's collarbone—across Pasco, surgical needles, watching the country fracture on a muted hospital wall screen, and singing *Happy Birthday* down a three-thousand-mile copper wire—had left every circuit in my chest starving for the one steady anchor on Earth that quieted the noise inside my head.

"Gotcha, shortstop," Gwen whispered thickly into my hair, holding me tight against her burgundy sweater for a full, unhurried minute without letting go until my whole trembling ribcage finally settled into hers. "Never letting you out of reach again."

All two months I had lain on that sixth-floor ward, Gwen and Grandpa Walter had carefully avoided talking about Store Four-Twenty-One over the hospital phone—telling me only to focus one hundred percent on healing my skin so my heart monitor wouldn't spike. The instant Gwen eased half a step back beside my right wheel with one hand still resting warm on my shoulder and I looked straight up into Mallory Walsh's unmistakable blue-gray eyes three thousand miles from Emerald Creek Parkway, that single unanswered question burst straight out of my chest.

"Mallory—you came all the way out here!" My voice cracked wide open with sudden, desperate hope as both of my hands caught hers. "Did Mr. Halstead put me on medical leave once you called Grandpa's house on Friday?! Please tell me—is there *any* way I can get my job at Blue Tech back?!"

Mallory didn't stand above my wheelchair or feed me a gentle lie to save for Virginia. Dropping cleanly onto both knees right on the hospital linoleum beside my front footrests so her eyes met mine dead on the level behind her wire-rimmed glasses, she held both of my hands steady and warm in hers.

"Nolan, I promised myself I would always tell you the exact truth," Mallory said softly, her own voice thick with emotion. "Because I waited until Friday evening's third missed shift on August twenty-fourth to call your grandfather's house, corporate's automated payroll computer up at headquarters had already processed an automatic job-abandonment termination with a corporate rehire freeze before Monday morning. Ray Halstead appealed straight to District HR with your hospital admission fax—and corporate still refused to unlock your employee number. I am so sorry I waited until Friday, Nolan."

Even after sixty-two days of hospital needles, even after Pasco and watching the world fracture on a wall screen and missing Gwen's eighteenth birthday, hearing out loud that I had permanently lost my Blue Tech job stung so brutally hard that all the breath went clean out of my lungs.

I had loved that royal-blue polo. For almost two years I had never missed one single punch on the clock, never misplaced one coaxial adapter, never given anyone a reason to doubt me—counting on every biweekly Friday paycheck to prove an autistic guy on forearm crutches could take care of his little sister when their grandfather died. Losing even that made two months of swallowed terror, homesickness, and grief break wide open at once.

My chin crumpled, a raw, ragged sob tore straight out of my throat, and right there in the middle of the sixth-floor discharge lounge, I buried my face forward and cried my heart out.

Neither Gwen nor Mallory let me cry alone for half a heartbeat.

Right on my right side, Gwen dropped onto the cushioned visitor chair flush against my wheel and pulled me hard against her shoulder; right on my left, Mallory rose off her knees onto the edge of the bench, wrapped both arms tightly around my neck and shoulders, and held my shaking head close against her green cardigan collar while her own tears dampened my hair. Caught safe inside four fierce, unhurried arms—zero yelling, zero shame, zero workplace wall left anywhere between Mallory and me—I cried into both of their arms until two months of swallowed terror finally broke open.

"Let the brave mask go, Nolan—just lean on the two of us," Gwen whispered fiercely against my temple, rocking my shoulders gently while grey Seattle rain drummed against the sixth-floor hospital glass three thousand miles from Virginia. "What Tammy did to you in Pasco wasn't your fault, and losing that blue shirt wasn't your fault either. Mallory and I are holding the line right beside you from here on out. Your broken heart will mend with time—I promise you it will—and we're getting you out of this hospital and all the way home."

"Every single mile of the way," Mallory whispered steadily against my other ear once my ragged breathing slowed, pressing both a crisp letter on Blue Tech letterhead and a white business card into my palm. "Ray Halstead has a five-year-old autistic granddaughter, Nolan—the minute he heard Tammy dragged you to Norfolk, he understood your literal brain didn't connect boarding that bus with getting fired here because you assumed she'd let you call us from a rest stop. Ray couldn't overrule Corporate's computer, so he wrote his own personal phone number on the back of this card and promised that any employer you apply to can call him directly off the clock for a glowing professional reference. And losing Blue Tech also means as of August twenty-fourth, I stopped being your Shift Supervisor."

Wiping my wet cheeks on my wrist, I looked straight across two inches of air into Mallory's honest, freckled face—letting the whole picture sink into my literal brain at once.

For two straight months while skies reopened and my wound closed, Mallory hadn't moved on or forgotten the quiet guy from Computers; she had checked on Grandpa Walter and Gwen off Eden Way North week after week, booked the very first flight Gwen turned eighteen to board, and crossed an entire continent just to bring me back.

"Mallory," I whispered thickly from my wheelchair, reaching both hands slightly toward her green cardigan lapels, "can I hug you right now—just for you?"

Tears shone bright behind Mallory's silver wire-rimmed glasses as the warmest, most relieved smile broke across her face.

"Come here, Nolan," Mallory whispered, leaning right down over my left armrest to wrap both arms all the way around my shoulders while I locked both of mine tightly around her waist and back—burying my cheek against the soft wool of her collar in a long, quiet, massively earned hug that neither of us rushed by a single second. Right beside my right wheel, newly eighteen-year-old Gwen leaned back in the visitor chair watching the two of us with shining green-hazel eyes and the happiest, most wholeheartedly approving grin on Earth.

"Okay, you two," Gwen smiled gently when Mallory finally eased back just enough to lace her fingers warmly over my hand on my wheelchair armrest. "We aren't home in Tidewater yet—we still have Nolan's discharge signatures, post-September-eleventh airport checkpoints, and two flights ahead of us—so let's get my brother out of Seattle while Grandpa Walter's holding watch for his face."

## 8:10 PM, Saturday, October 27 — Keeping Watch Until Morning, Ray's Reference, and Studio 205

True to every word of his sailor's vow, seventy-nine-year-old Grandpa Walter was still awake inside his downstairs bedroom off Eden Way North when Mallory's silver compact rolled into our Chesapeake driveway Saturday night from Norfolk International Airport.

Propped against two pillows beneath his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket with nasal oxygen tubing tucked behind his ears, Grandpa Walter opened his tired gray eyes the instant I rolled my manual wheelchair right up beside his mattress—with newly eighteen-year-old Gwen standing tall on her charcoal crutches behind my right shoulder and Mallory standing quietly in the doorway. Reaching out one thin, paper-light machinist hand to cover mine and Gwen's together, Grandpa looked straight at my healed, rested face and smiled the most peaceful smile I had ever seen.

"Made it home, boy," Grandpa whispered softly. "Both of you... look after each other now."

Sometime before six o'clock the next morning—with both of his grandchildren finally safe under one roof and Gwen legally eighteen beyond any social-services claim—Walter Lennox kept watch one last quiet night and slipped away peacefully in his sleep.

Everything Grandpa, Gwen, Ray Halstead, and Mallory had protected carried us straight forward through November without me ever needing a retail register badge again. Back at the end of August—the Monday after Mallory first called our house—Danny from Blue Tech's warehouse and a Chesapeake sheriff's deputy had already cleared my full-sized bed, oak dresser, nineteen-inch television, silver VCR and DVD combo, and laptop out of Tammy's Pinecrest Court trailer into Grandpa's garage, while Grandpa mailed my certified ninety-day lot non-renewal notice so my name dropped off Tammy's lease automatically at midnight on November thirtieth despite Tammy's stubborn refusal to sign an early release.

Better yet, without retail shifts pulling me away from Grandpa's house while Gwen and I navigated Chesapeake probate and put our two-story childhood house up for sale to pay off Grandpa's winter medical home-equity loan, Gwen spotted a November staff opening downtown at the Community College of Hampton Roads Norfolk Campus inside the Martin Building for a twenty-eight-hour-a-week seated Part-Time Campus Computer Lab and Helpdesk Technician. When CCHR's campus IT director called the personal home number Ray Halstead had written on his card after my practical interview, fifty-two-year-old Ray gave me a glowing fifteen-minute reference—explaining plainly that I had been tricked onto an out-of-state bus without phone access after twenty-two spotless months and calling me the sharpest Quantum OS 2000 technician he had ever supervised—landing me the seated campus workbench job two weeks before moving day at two dollars more an hour than retail ever paid.

On December first, Gwen and I unlocked Studio 205 on the second floor of Harborview Towers—a twenty-story nineteen-sixty-five high-rise overlooking The Hague inlet off West Brambleton Avenue in Norfolk, six blocks from Ghent High School where Gwen transferred to finish eleventh grade, and one six-minute ride down CVTA Route 2 or Route 11 from my CCHR lab bench. When Mallory carried our microwave cart inside, took one look at our single corner full-sized bed draped in Grandpa Walter's plaid blanket, and surprised both Gwen and me by saying plainly that sharing the one bed that fit so Gwen wasn't out on the street made complete engineering sense—before confessing out on the stairwell landing that she had been in love with me since Store Four-Twenty-One and becoming my girlfriend—every broken piece of two thousand and one finally clicked whole.

Ten quiet, happy months later—with me twenty-four, Gwen newly nineteen in her senior year at Ghent High, Saturday morning open-lab shifts at CCHR followed by Ghent lunches with Mallory, and both of us curled up barefoot on our shared corner bed on a rainy October Friday night—Regional Mobility Supply's hold music warbled past forty-seven minutes as the air beside our dresser shimmered warm amber.
