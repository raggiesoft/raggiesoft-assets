## 7:20 AM — Returning the Rental Car at Sea-Tac and Why Mallory Booked Midway Over O'Hare

Pale, rain-washed Saturday morning light filtered across the concrete columns of the rental-car return garage at Seattle-Tacoma International Airport when Mallory backed our rented Vanguard Fleet Services sedan into its numbered stall, lifted my manual wheelchair and four-inch medical gel seat cushion out of the trunk, and helped me settle aboard with zero rush despite the dark, hollow exhaustion rings beneath my sleepless eyes.

Even over busy Labor Day weekend travel crowds inside my very first airport terminal, Mallory kept everything calm, literal, and one step at a time: seventeen-year-old Gwen handed the ticket agent her Emerald Creek High student ID alongside Grandpa Walter's notarized guardian travel letter while Mallory collected bright gate-check tags so ground crews would bring my own manual wheelchair right up to the jetbridge door at Chicago Midway and again in Norfolk, and a polite checkpoint screener helped us walk our four single-tube Lofstrand forearm crutches smoothly past the metal detector without a single delay.

Ten minutes later, rolling down the concourse to our Gateway Airlines departure gate, my tired eyes scanned the overhead flight monitors—and spotted our two hops home:

Seattle-Tacoma to Chicago Midway, connecting straight on to Norfolk International Airport.

Two gates down the glass corridor, a departure board for Meridian Airlines listed an earlier eastbound flight heading to Norfolk by way of Chicago O'Hare.

"Mallory," I rasped softly over my cup of terminal water as she and Gwen sat down on either side of my wheelchair beside the bulkhead pre-board sign, "how come we're flying Gateway into Chicago Midway instead of that earlier Meridian flight through O'Hare?"

Adjusting her thin silver wire-rimmed glasses, Mallory gave my hand a warm, steady squeeze.

"Because I checked both Chicago airports on Wednesday the minute your doctor mentioned Friday discharge, and I refused to touch O'Hare," Mallory answered plainly. "If you fly Meridian through Chicago O'Hare, changing between Concourse B and Concourse C forces every wheelchair and crutch passenger underground through what they call The Prismatic Corridor—eight hundred feet of kinetic flashing rainbow neon tubes overhead, mirrored walls on both sides, and loud electronic chimes echoing off the glass. I went through that tunnel once with my parents, Nolan, and because of my autism it tripped my sensory wiring so hard my chest locked up for half an hour. Coming straight out of a hospital ward on zero sleep last night, there was no way on Earth I was taking you anywhere near The Prismatic Corridor. Chicago Midway is one flat, ordinary concourse with normal ceiling lights."

Even through a sleepless fog, hearing that made tears prick hot behind my eyelids—not scared tears this time, just quiet awe that a woman existed on this planet whose brain worked like mine and who guarded my blind spots before I even knew they were there.

Every mile across the sky proved Mallory right. Seated three-across in Row One bulkhead seats on both Gateway Airlines jets—me resting on my four-inch medical gel cushion right in the middle shifting weight off my bandaged left hip every half hour, Gwen keeping one warm arm tucked through mine on my right, and Mallory holding my left hand steady during takeoff and landing—Chicago Midway turned out to be exactly as calm and straightforward as she had promised: ground crew met us right at the open aircraft door with my manual wheelchair, and our connecting Norfolk gate sat four flat gates down the exact same carpeted pier without a single flashing neon tube in sight.

## 8:10 PM EDT — Grandpa Walter's Porch Light and Home Before the First School Bell

Touching down just after dark at Norfolk International Airport—where humid Tidewater pine air greeted us outside baggage claim and Mallory loaded my wheelchair and all four crutches into the trunk of her own familiar silver compact waiting in long-term parking—we rolled down Interstate 64 into Chesapeake and turned up our maple-lined street off Eden Way North at ten minutes past eight on Saturday night.

Before Mallory even pulled the handbrake, the yellow porch lamp clicked bright and seventy-nine-year-old Grandpa Walter stepped steadily out onto the top landing in his pressed flannel shirt—no supplemental oxygen tubing yet, just thin, weathered Navy shoulders squaring upright with overwhelming relief the instant Mallory unfolded my manual wheelchair at the bottom of the walk and helped me up the three wooden steps on my silver crutches.

Wrapping both veined machinist arms tightly around my shoulders right inside the foyer while Gwen hugged us both from the side, Grandpa held on hard for ten quiet seconds.

"Home before Labor Day, boy," Grandpa Walter whispered thickly against my temple, patting the back of my neck. "Got my whole crew back under one roof."

That Labor Day Monday—after Danny from Blue Tech's warehouse and a Chesapeake sheriff's deputy had already cleared my full-sized bed, oak dresser, nineteen-inch television, silver VCR and DVD combo, and laptop out of Tammy's Pinecrest Court trailer into Grandpa's garage the previous week, and Grandpa had mailed my certified ninety-day lot non-renewal notice so my name would drop off Mr. Pruitt's lease automatically at midnight on November thirtieth—Mallory reported cleanly back to Store Four-Twenty-One on schedule, and bright and early Tuesday morning, September fourth, seventeen-year-old Gwen clicked out our front door on her charcoal crutches without missing a single minute of her first day of junior year at Emerald Creek High School.

Only nights on Grandpa's unfolded downstairs family-room sofa bed—where Puget Sound General's physician had ordered me to keep resting flat off my healing left sitting bone while finishing my oral antibiotics—betrayed what Pasco had left behind inside my nervous system: even under my own childhood ceiling with Gwen checking on me before bed, every creak of the floorboards or passing car headlight outside the curtains snapped my eyes wide open in the dark, sleeping with one ear open as if Tammy might somehow step out of the shadows again.
