## 6:30 PM — Eighteen Candles in Person and Grandpa Walter's Peaceful Morning

Sitting right across Grandpa Walter's warm kitchen table off Eden Way North on Friday evening, October nineteenth—watching yellow wax drip down eighteen crooked birthday candles on a homemade chocolate sheet cake instead of staring at a hospital wall three thousand miles away—made every ounce of fighting my way back from Washington worth it.

Even after Grandpa's tired heart had quietly slipped onto home supplemental oxygen at the end of September, seventy-nine-year-old Walter Lennox sat beaming at the head of the table beneath his dark-green and navy-blue plaid wool blanket as I sang *Happy Birthday* across the laminate tabletop and newly eighteen-year-old Gwen blew all eighteen flames out in one gust. And when I rubbed my damp palms on my jeans and asked nervously whether my six-foot-one sister would really want to move with me into a tiny eleven-by-eighteen-foot Norfolk studio with one corner full-sized bed once my written notice dropped my name off Tammy's Pinecrest Court lease on November thirtieth—since Tammy had stayed out west without ever signing an early roommate release—Gwen rolled two feet around the table corner and threw both arms around my neck shouting *A million times yes!*

Reaching both thin machinist hands across the tablecloth to cover Gwen's hand and mine together, Grandpa Walter smiled the most peaceful smile I had ever seen.

"Two grown Lennoxes under one roof come December," Grandpa whispered softly. "Look after each other now."

Sometime before six o'clock the next morning—with Gwen legally eighteen beyond any social-services claim and both of us safe beside him for the past seven weeks—Grandpa Walter slipped away peacefully in his sleep.

Without retail shifts pulling me away through late October, Gwen and I navigated Chesapeake Circuit Court probate, ten raised-seal death certificates, and listing our childhood two-story house to pay off Grandpa's winter medical home-equity loan side by side—and in early November, Gwen spotted a staff opening downtown at the Community College of Hampton Roads Norfolk Campus inside the Martin Building for a twenty-eight-hour-a-week seated Part-Time Campus Computer Lab and Helpdesk Technician.

Following Ray Halstead's exact warning over Mallory's cell phone back in Seattle, I left Blue Tech's corporate eight-hundred employee-verification number completely off my CCHR application so corporate's automated ineligible-for-rehire flag couldn't disqualify me, and wrote Ray's direct home phone number right into the supervisor box instead. When CCHR's campus IT director dialed Ray's house after my four-minute TCP/IP practical interview, fifty-two-year-old Ray picked up on the second ring and gave me a glowing fifteen-minute professional review—explaining plainly that I had been stranded out of state in a medical emergency after twenty-two spotless months and calling me the sharpest Quantum OS 2000 technician he had ever supervised—landing me the seated campus workbench job two weeks before moving day at two dollars more an hour than retail ever paid.

On Saturday afternoon, December first, Gwen and I unlocked Studio 205 on the second floor of Harborview Towers—a twenty-story nineteen-sixty-five brick-and-concrete high-rise overlooking The Hague inlet off West Brambleton Avenue in Norfolk, six blocks from Ghent High School where Gwen transferred Monday morning to finish eleventh grade, and one six-minute ride down CVTA Route 2 or Route 11 from my CCHR lab bench. When Mallory Walsh carried our rolling microwave-cart pantry inside, took one look at our single corner full-sized bed draped in Grandpa Walter's plaid blanket, and surprised both Gwen and me by saying plainly that sharing the one bed that fit so Gwen wasn't out on the street made complete engineering sense—before walking out onto the second-floor stairwell landing to confess she had been in love with me since Store Four-Twenty-One and becoming my girlfriend—every outward piece of our life clicked whole.

Only deep in the middle of the night inside Studio 205—curled close beside Gwen under Grandpa's plaid blanket whenever a radiator pipe clanked or footsteps echoed past Door 205—did my chest still jolt awake in cold terror remembering Pasco's deadbolt, carrying one quiet scar across ten otherwise happy months until a rainy October Friday night at nine-fifteen when Regional Mobility Supply's hold music warbled past forty-seven minutes and the air beside our dresser shimmered warm amber.
